Local decisions and overall performance#

Mihajlo Mesarović, D. Macko and Yasuhiko Takahara's Theory of Hierarchical, Multilevel, Systems concerns descriptions of complex systems, local decision problems and overall performance. Its publisher preview sets out a programme for arranging local decisions and higher-level intervention to meet a larger requirement without making the higher level solve the entire problem again. [W096, publisher preview; W097, pp. 23–26; SB-W008, pp. 3–5]

The 1969 coordination paper develops the formal mechanism. Richardson’s 1975 conference paper, Findeisen and colleagues’ 1980 book, and Abraham, Tribolet and Winter’s 2013 article examine practical control, modeling and organizational difficulties. [W097; SB-W006; SB-W007; SB-W008]

Strata, layers and echelons ask different questions#

Richardson describes three distinct hierarchy concepts in the 1970 programme. Strata concern levels of description or abstraction. Layers concern decision complexity. Echelons concern priority of action and organizational coordination. A coarser representation, a more complex decision and a superior organizational office are consequently different things. They may be related in an application without becoming interchangeable meanings of higher level. [W097, p. 24]

The distinction helps recover what has been delegated. A detailed model and an aggregate model can describe the same system differently. A local regulator can act within a setting selected by another decision process. An organization can assign intervention rights among offices. Calling all three hierarchical would conceal whether the relevant economy concerns representation, the division of computation or the allocation of authority. The sources deliberately provide several descriptions for these different relationships. [W097, pp. 24–26; SB-W007, hierarchy framework]

Richardson also describes the programme as combining heuristics for decomposing and representing large problems, mathematical theory, and methods for improving behavior. Satisfactory performance, rather than exact optimization, is included in his reconstruction. [W097, pp. 24–25]

The 1969 paper: local success has to map into overall success#

In Two Coordination Principles and Their Application in Large Scale Systems Control, the same three authors distinguish an overall control problem, local control problems and a coordination problem. Local units directly influence the controlled process. The higher or supremal unit acts indirectly by influencing those units. Its task is not defined simply as being above them: it has to make their decisions compatible with the overall requirement. [SB-W008, pp. 3–5]

The formal setup considers families of local problems indexed by intervention parameters. A transformation maps a joint solution of the local problems into a candidate solution of the overall problem. The crucial condition is that every relevant joint solution under the chosen intervention maps into an overall solution. Without such a condition, separately satisfactory local decisions need not constitute a satisfactory whole. A decomposition supplies multiple problems; it does not automatically supply their compatibility. [SB-W008, pp. 4–5]

The law of consistency constrains the coordinator's own problem. Solving that problem should be sufficient for the joint local results to meet the overall requirement. One trivial way to ensure this is to

make the coordinator solve the complete original problem. The authors explicitly recognize why that misses the intended advantage: the art is to find a simpler coordination problem while retaining consistency. [SB-W008, p. 5]

Coordinability adds an existence question. It concerns whether some intervention in the specified family can produce the required relation between local solutions and the overall solution. That differs from saying what follows if a suitable intervention is found. The paper itself distinguishes a principle's applicability from the existence of a parameter that meets its matching condition. [SB-W008, pp. 5, 8]

Prediction and balance retain different work#

Interaction prediction begins by specifying anticipated interface inputs for the local units. Each local controller optimizes with those predictions fixed. When their controls are applied to the connected process, actual interface inputs result. The coordinator compares actual inputs with predictions and changes the predictions to reduce the discrepancy. The architecture has separated local decisions by supplying information about their interfaces; it has retained a problem of making that information consistent with actual joint operation. [SB-W008, pp. 6–8]

Interaction balance instead modifies local performance functions. The local problems then optimize over their controls and desired interface inputs. Actual operation supplies interfaces that may differ from those desired. The higher level changes its intervention parameters to bring the desired and actual values into agreement. Local autonomy in this construction is conditional on the objective modifications and on the interfaces supplied by the connected system. [SB-W008, pp. 7–8]

The authors allow a mismatch measure to be minimized when exact zero error is not attainable. Such a formulation is a further problem definition. [SB-W008, p. 8]

What the conditional optimality argument actually uses#

Theorem I in the 1969 paper supplies sufficient conditions for applying interaction balance. For every relevant intervention and feasible overall control, global cost must be expressible through an order-preserving aggregation of the modified local costs evaluated at actual interfaces. Local units must attain minima over the stated local controls and freely chosen interface inputs. If their chosen interfaces then match those arising from the joint controls, the resulting overall control minimizes global cost. [SB-W008, pp. 9–10; appendix A]

The proof explains why all these premises matter. Each local minimizing pair is no worse in its local cost than the corresponding pair associated with an arbitrary feasible overall alternative. Order preservation carries these componentwise comparisons into a comparison of global cost. Matching allows the locally selected pairs to be interpreted as the actual interconnected result. The conclusion concerns that declared objective and feasible set, not every property a real system might be expected to have. [SB-W008, appendix A, proof of theorem I]

Theorem II provides an additive special case. Modifications can be introduced into local objectives while summing to zero at feasible interconnections. The overall cost is then preserved by those modifications, allowing the first theorem's reasoning to apply. The important feature is the verified relation among objectives, rather than an assumption that any collection of individually optimized goals will combine well. [SB-W008, p. 10; appendix A]

Existence results add further hypotheses about attained and unique local minima, convexity, lower semicontinuity, compactness and the relevant dual relation. These hypotheses cannot be suppressed when moving from the conditional matching argument to a claim that matching can be achieved. The

paper does not provide a general convergence algorithm, a response-time guarantee or a measured reduction in total computation. [SB-W008, theorems III–V and appendices]

In Example III of the 1969 paper, the printed parameter makes one local quadratic unbounded below despite the claimed minimum. Theorem V's compactness reasoning also requires appropriate space and attainment assumptions beyond an unrestricted normed-space reading. [SB-W008, example III, proof of theorem V and appendix B]

Four questions consequently remain separate in this account: whether matched local solutions satisfy the global criterion; whether a matching intervention exists; whether an actual procedure reaches it; and whether the procedure does so reliably and soon enough for the real application. An answer to an earlier question does not answer the later ones. Correct specification of the real objective and constraints is another obligation throughout. [SB-W008; SB-W006, ch. 1]

Findeisen and colleagues: control must work on an operating system#

The introductory chapter of Control and Coordination in Hierarchical Systems, by W. Findeisen, F. N. Bailey, M. Brdys, K. Malinowski, P. Tatjewski and A. Wozniak, develops the practical coordination problem. [SB-W006, introduction and ch. 1, pp. 4–74]

Its introduction identifies reasons to divide decision responsibility: limited individual capability, spatial separation, communication cost and delay, distorted information, and private local information. For control, it also insists that the system is operating under disturbances and that current information can be used to improve decisions. Models, real constraints, measurement and response horizons are therefore part of the subject from the beginning. [SB-W006, pp. 1–4]

The chapter distinguishes multilayer organization of different control tasks from multilevel decomposition and coordination of related local problems. Different horizons and kinds of information can justify different decision processes. Treating all units as copies of one static optimizer would hide what a faster regulator, a slower planning process or a model-updating function actually does. The architecture has to respect the process and the time available for action. [SB-W006, §§1.2–1.4]

The formulation of global performance has a consequential assumption: it is an order-preserving function of local performances. The authors say that this excludes gamelike situations among decision makers from their discussion. Their coordination results therefore cannot be carried directly into an organization with unresolved conflicting aims simply because its chart contains local units and a center. The required objective relationship has to be supplied, not inferred from the word hierarchy. [SB-W006, p. 36]

Direct coordination illustrates the feasibility problem. The coordinator prescribes outputs and allocates resources; local units then try to achieve those demands. Some demands may be impossible under local equations or constraints, and disturbance or model uncertainty can change the feasible region. Staying inside a known safe region can preserve feasibility but may require conservative decisions and substantial knowledge. Issuing a target is not proof that a local unit can produce it. [SB-W006, pp. 36–38]

The chapter also distinguishes solving local models offline from coordinating people or controllers acting on a real process. An optimization result computed from models becomes open-loop control if applied without correcting it from actual system behavior. In real interaction, separate decision makers can cause instability while each is attempting to do well locally. Even a mathematically convergent coordination scheme needs an appropriate relation to the process operating between its updates. [SB-W006, pp. 40– 41]

An adverse case: decomposition can enlarge the central problem#

The ammonia-plant example directly challenges an automatic saving from decomposition. The plant contains feed-forward and recycle relationships, including heat flows that contribute to its operation. Cutting it into subprocesses while representing all relevant cross-boundary quantities would require roughly forty coordination variables. Yet the original overall optimization concerned only twenty-two adjustable control variables. The result would be a larger coordination problem plus the added local problems. The authors call that version more complex and expensive than direct treatment. [SB-W006, pp. 42–45]

Their reported response uses engineering understanding and operating experience. Only five quantities were retained as essential coordination variables for an approximate solution; others were related to them, treated as nearly constant or judged less relevant to the objective. The reduction is not supplied by the drawing of the boundary. It depends on knowing which couplings can be compressed or neglected for the application. [SB-W006, pp. 44–45]

This motivates subcoordination: using fewer variables than a strict interface description would require. Its economy is bought through an approximation whose adequacy must be examined. Knowing a model exactly enough to encode a perfect low-dimensional prescription would be a different situation from controlling a process with model–reality differences. The source itself cautions against making that exact-knowledge assumption. [SB-W006, pp. 45–47]

The authors’ plant example provides significant adverse evidence within their account: hierarchical decomposition can increase work, and successful simplification can require more retained system knowledge than the abstract partition suggests. [SB-W006, pp. 43–47]

Dynamic control, information and model revision#

The introductory chapter extends coordination to dynamic problems with states, time functions and finite horizons. Solving a steady-state problem repeatedly is not automatically an adequate dynamic controller. Actions influence later conditions, and the time spent measuring, communicating and computing can matter to the result. Decomposing a horizon or using a shorter horizon changes the problem unless its consequences are controlled. [SB-W006, ch. 1, dynamic control and information-structure discussions, pp. 49–63]

The information structure specifies who knows what and when. Local decision makers may have measurements unavailable to the coordinator, while the center may possess information about interactions unavailable locally. Feedback can help with model error, but measurements, transmission and interpretation are retained activities. A formally convenient hierarchy is therefore only part of the arrangement needed for actual control. [SB-W006, §1.4]

Richardson: building and using integrated models#

Richardson's 1975 paper reports on the Multilevel Regionalized World Modeling Project and related work on Lake Erie pollution. Richardson is an insider describing a continuing research programme. His paper connects the earlier framework to later modelling practice and explicitly says that technical and organizational skills developed together. [W097, pp. 23–25]

His Lake Erie discussion uses strata to organize submodels associated with different disciplines. Partial decomposability permits some development and validation before final integration. The qualification is important: in crisis situations, couplings can become stronger. A decomposition useful under ordinary conditions may not preserve the same independence when the situation changes. Integration remains a task even when subgroups can make progress separately. [W097, pp. 25–27]

The regionalized world model differentiates regions by relevant economic and political conditions rather than treating the globe as one uniform aggregate. Trade, migration and other flows connect the regions. Within regions, causal, organizational and normative descriptions address different aspects of behavior. Values and beliefs affect the selection among satisfactory policies; allowing users to supply norms interactively is one response to the difficulty of simulating them completely. [W097, pp. 28–30]

This makes retained judgment concrete. Model builders must decide which regional differences matter, how decisions are represented and what belongs in the scenarios. The paper's food-policy example joins separately developed submodels whose detailed documentation is much larger than the conference exposition. Their existence and integration are reported facts about the project. The scenario curves remain historical model outputs under their stated policy assumptions. [W097, pp. 30–31 and figs. 10–12]

Richardson's agenda is unusually explicit about limits. He argues that difficulty quantifying social and value questions does not justify ignoring them. He emphasizes judgment and design in training, cooperation across disciplines and an institutional environment that supports integrated work. A framework for modelling cannot itself create the professional relationships and durable organizations required to use it. [W097, pp. 32–33]

He also questions whether fitting historical data is sufficient validation for long-term models when parameters and structures are changed to explore alternatives. Claims to decision makers should remain conservative relative to actual utility. Institutional pressures can favor short horizons even where a model asks long-term questions. [W097, pp. 33–35]

Abraham, Tribolet and Winter: transformation as well as operation#

Abraham, Tribolet and Winter's 2013 article applies distinctions among strata, layers and echelons to enterprise architecture management. It positions architecture management across these dimensions and proposes interlinked feedback loops for transformation. [SB-W007, hierarchy framework and reference 6]

The authors distinguish a functional understanding of an enterprise from an understanding of its construction. Principles can restrict design freedom and help coordinate planned change, but the relevant perspective is broader than a single place in an organization chart. An architectural model can describe different strata while transformation decisions and intervention responsibilities occupy other dimensions. [SB-W007, introduction and hierarchy discussion]

Their feedback model distinguishes routine optimization within an existing understanding, planned transformation that changes models and goals, and improvisation under severe unpredictable disturbance. The latter can require making sense of a new situation before returning to planned projects and routine operation. The categories are part of the authors' conceptual proposal. [SB-W007, feedback-loop model and fig. 4]

The model also makes continued activity visible. An enterprise must keep operating while it undertakes a transformation, and useful experience must enter its representations if it is to handle a formerly unfamiliar exception routinely later. Models, goals and practices do not update simply because a new diagram is available. The Portuguese Air Force case serves as an illustrative application in the article. [SB-W007, feedback and application sections]

The article’s assertion that observability is universally necessary for controllability is not a general control-theoretic result. Its enterprise-transformation proposal joins observation, modeling and intervention, whereas Mesarović and colleagues’ 1970 book also addresses dynamic control. [SB-W007; W096, contents; W097, p. 24]

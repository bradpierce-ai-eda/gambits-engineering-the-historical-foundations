An inventive task can begin with a difficulty in an existing design, an unexplained result or a representation that makes some possibilities hard to see. The accounts in this section follow transformations of a technical problem, the recognition of a mathematical relationship, the use of analogy and the construction of new terms in a computational search. They make the work between encountering a problem and obtaining a candidate solution available for close examination.

Bush and transformations of the technical problem#

G. Ya. Bush's Methods of Technical Creativity, published in 1972, classifies inventive methods along several dimensions. A method can be general or specialized, simple or complex, and directed toward a particular stage of creative work. His stages include preparation, forming a conception, searching for a solution and realizing it. The classification gives problem formulation and implementation a place alongside generating ideas. Bush describes heuristic methods as recommendations or incomplete procedures whose use requires judgment. [CRE-W007, §3.1]

Inversion is one of his developed families. It includes changing an object's orientation, reversing an action, substituting a physical state, changing the course of a process in time, or turning an unwanted influence toward a useful purpose. The operation begins by identifying something in the situation that has been taken for granted. A specific reversal can then be considered. The question is not simply whether an object can be turned upside down, but which relation, property or sequence might usefully be changed. [CRE-W007, §3.3]

Bush's turbine-shaft example gives this change of direction a concrete form. He recounts the difficulty of obtaining stable operation at high rotational speed by shortening, thickening or strengthening a shaft. In his account of de Laval's experiment, a deliberately flexible shaft supplies an alternative to pursuing greater rigidity. The example belongs to Bush's explanation of antithesis: examine a response opposed to the one that has organized the unsuccessful attempts. The technical function provides the reason to reconsider the conventional remedy. [CRE-W007, §3.3]

His complex methods distinguish several kinds of combination. Established elements can be combined with new ones, new elements with one another, or established elements in a new relationship. Integration can bring functions together within an object. Agglutination attaches something to a principal object, potentially without redesigning the connected parts and potentially only for a time. Bush's computing example joins peripheral devices to a principal machine as their functions are required. The combination changes what the larger arrangement can do. [CRE-W007, §3.4]

An intermediate element can also make a combination possible and then be removed. Bush describes an assembly method in which a film helps position components for a printed-circuit board. After the connections have been made, the intermediate film is removed. The temporary support participates in making the final object without remaining one of its permanent parts. This example explains why an inventive method may concern the sequence of construction as much as the finished geometry. [CRE-W007, §3.4]

Bush's account supplies several different questions to put to a technical difficulty. What has been held fixed? Which property could be reversed? What functions could be joined? Could an intermediate object or operation make a desired connection possible? His examples give those questions specific operations and technical settings. S44 develops the earlier account of inventive creativity by Altshuller and Shapiro; Bush's selected method families provide a further substantial treatment of technical invention.

Poincaré on recognizing a fruitful combination#

Henri Poincaré's 1908 chapter on mathematical invention begins with a problem of selection. New combinations of familiar mathematical objects are easy to describe in the abstract; most would have little interest. Invention, in his account, includes discerning the rare connections that reveal an unsuspected relationship between facts previously treated as separate. A connection across distant areas can be fruitful, but mere distance does not make it so. [CRE-W008, Livre I, §III]

He illustrates the work through the history of his first memoir on Fuchsian functions. For about a fortnight he made sustained attempts without obtaining the result he sought. One night, after taking black coffee contrary to his habit, he experienced a crowd of ideas while unable to sleep. He describes two ideas forming a stable combination. The next morning brought work that could be written out. The episode appears within a longer sequence of deliberate attempts and subsequent mathematical development. [CRE-W008, Livre I, §III]

A later episode occurs during a geological excursion. Boarding an omnibus, Poincaré suddenly recognizes a relationship between transformations used in defining Fuchsian functions and those of non-Euclidean geometry. He does not verify it on the spot. He continues the conversation and later checks the result at leisure. Other episodes include unsuccessful work, a stay by the sea and an interruption for military service. The chapter gives mathematical invention a temporal texture: directed effort, interruption, recognition and renewed deliberate work. [CRE-W008, Livre I, §III]

Selection raises a further question for him. The inventor cannot inspect every possible combination one after another. Poincaré proposes that mathematical sensibility admits some combinations into attention because of their promise of order, elegance or useful relation. His aesthetic explanation concerns the recognition of fruitful structure. It does not equate mathematical invention with generating arbitrary novelties and later checking all of them. [CRE-W008, Livre I, §III]

He also distinguishes the felt certainty of an arrival from a completed demonstration. Sudden ideas can be mistaken. Conscious work is required to formulate and verify them, as well as to prepare the problem that precedes them. The chapter joins a first-person history to a proposed explanation of why some combinations attract attention. Its description of interrupted work gives a historical setting for the later experimental accounts of incubation in S67. [CRE-W008, Livre I, §III]

Gick and Holyoak and the analogy that must be noticed#

Mary Gick and Keith Holyoak's 1980 experiments use two stories with different objects but a corresponding problem structure. A general wants to capture a fortress. Roads leading toward it are mined: small groups can pass safely, while a large force would trigger destruction. The army is divided, approaches along different roads and converges at the fortress. The separate passages preserve the surroundings while concentrating the force where it is needed. [CRE-W009, pp. 308–312]

The medical problem concerns a tumour that cannot safely be treated by sending a destructive intensity of radiation through healthy tissue along a single path. Several low-intensity rays can approach from different directions and combine at the target. Mapping the general's solution requires relating troops to rays, separate roads to different paths and convergent attack to combined treatment. The analogy concerns organized relations among the elements. [CRE-W009, pp. 308–312]

The fourth experiment separates knowing the source story from using it. Twenty-seven participants learn the critical story among other stories. Twelve receive a hint that a previous story may help solve the radiation problem. Eleven of those twelve produce the complete dispersion solution. Among the fifteen without the hint, three produce complete or partial dispersion solutions, and the authors examine whether those participants actually noticed the analogy. [CRE-W009, pp. 340–343]

The source can therefore be available in memory while its relevance goes unrecognized. Gick and Holyoak's procedure brings retrieval and mapping into the problem-solving account. A participant must find a useful earlier situation, identify the relations that matter and use them to construct a response in the target setting. Learning an instructive example and recognizing when to apply it are distinguishable accomplishments. [CRE-W009, pp. 340–343]

Dunbar and Blanchette and analogies used at work#

Kevin Dunbar and Isabelle Blanchette study analogy during scientists' laboratory meetings. Their initial analysis covers sixteen meetings in four laboratories and finds more than ninety-nine analogies. Most comparisons stay within biology or immunology. A useful analogy need not cross into a remote intellectual area: a familiar experiment can supply the comparison needed to understand why another experiment behaved unexpectedly. [CRE-W010, discussion of analogy in laboratory meetings]

A near comparison can isolate a consequential difference. Two experiments may share a gene, proteins and temperature while differing in incubation time. Drawing attention to that difference can help scientists repair an experimental problem. The authors therefore give superficial similarity a useful role when the immediate goal is troubleshooting. The relation between the comparison and the goal matters more than a general preference for distant analogies. [CRE-W010]

When the goal changes to formulating a hypothesis, the comparisons also change. Scientists draw on underlying structural relationships, including similarities between genetic sequences, to suggest a possible common function. The paper reports that structural analogies were a minority of the analogies observed, but more than eighty percent of those structural analogies were used to formulate hypotheses. What scientists compare is responsive to what they are trying to accomplish. [CRE-W010]

The authors connect these observations to laboratory tasks in which participants generate analogies rather than merely remember or choose a supplied one. Constructing a comparison, recognizing a reminder and using a comparison in a discussion are different activities. Their combined observational and experimental approach makes that distinction visible. It gives analogy a place within continuing research, where an anomaly, a colleague's suggestion and the design of another experiment can redirect the conversation. [CRE-W010]

Klahr and Simon and connected spaces of discovery#

David Klahr and Herbert Simon's 2001 review examines historical studies, laboratory tasks, naturalistic observations and computational models of discovery. Their account describes a researcher searching among hypotheses, experiments, representations and instruments. Work in one space changes what is worth trying in another. The initial problem is not always a fully specified question waiting for a single correct answer. [CRE-W011, pp. 75–78]

In the device-learning studies they discuss, participants investigate an unfamiliar control on a simulated rocket ship. They must propose an account of what the control does and choose manipulations that reveal its function. Some give greater emphasis to developing explanations; others concentrate on experiments. The task makes the coordination of the two searches observable. An intervention is informative in relation to a question, and its outcome can create a new question. [CRE-W011, discussion of dual search]

The review also gives surprise an active place. A result that contradicts expectations can become the beginning of an inquiry into a phenomenon. The Curies' encounter with unexpectedly strong radioactivity in pitchblende is one of its historical examples. Their attempt to obtain a substance helps reveal a problem about what else is present. The review follows this reversal between hypotheses motivating experiments and phenomena motivating hypotheses. [CRE-W011, discussion of surprise]

Representations and instruments can join the search as well. A different way to describe a problem can make a new analogy or experiment possible. The review's contribution is an account of discovery in which generating, testing and reformulating are connected activities. It complements the treatment of experiments and dual control in S33 by explaining how the possible objects of investigation themselves change. [CRE-W011, discussion of multiple search spaces]

BACON.5 and a discovered relation used as new data#

Pat Langley, Gary Bradshaw and Herbert Simon's 1981 BACON.5 paper describes a program that seeks regularities in numerical and nominal data. Its user specifies independent terms, values to consider and dependent variables. The program applies heuristics for constancy, trends and combinations of terms. Its ideal-gas example shows how these operations build on one another. [CRE-W012, pp. 121–122]

The program first holds temperature and amount of gas fixed while pressure varies. Volume decreases as pressure increases, suggesting that the product of pressure and volume should be examined. That product is found to be constant for the setting. Changing temperature produces different constant products. Relating those products to temperature suggests a ratio. Changing the amount of gas leads to a further relation, ultimately expressing the ideal-gas law through a constant value of pV/(nT). [CRE-W012, p. 121]

Each step supplies a term that can be used at the next level. The product pV is a hypothesis about the lower-level observations and becomes data for investigating its relationship to temperature. The program reapplies similar heuristics to the resulting descriptions. A useful representation is produced during the search instead of being supplied only as the final name for what was found. [CRE-W012, pp. 121–122]

The discovered pattern also changes data collection. Once the program expects a lower-level relation, later settings need not repeat the entire earlier variation. A selected observation can be used to estimate the relevant constant. The paper contrasts this procedure with a complete factorial search. Representation and control of investigation work together: finding a relation can reduce the observations required to investigate a higher-level relation. [CRE-W012, pp. 121–122]

The authors present BACON.5 as a computational way to perform part of discovery, including demonstrations on known physical laws. Their aim is to exhibit productive heuristics, not to reconstruct every action taken by the historical discoverers. The worked example makes the program's inputs, transformations and use of intermediate results explicit. [CRE-W012, Introduction]

Bonnardel and Lubart and constraints that make an analogy useful#

Nathalie Bonnardel and Todd Lubart describe creative design through an interaction between analogy and constraint management. Analogy can expand the field of ideas. Constraints help direct attention, evaluate a candidate and progressively determine what a satisfactory solution requires. Some constraints are prescribed; others are inferred from the consequences of those already accepted. A requirement for a lightweight but resistant aerospace component raises further questions about material and manufacture. [CRE-W013, §2.4]

Their field observations give this account a physical example. Designers need a reflector larger than those they normally make, but it must fit beneath a rocket fairing during transport. An umbrella suggests a structure that can be compact for launch and deployed later. Another designer draws on the movement of curtains in the office to propose a different solution. Ordinary objects become sources of invention when a designer recognizes a relation to a specific difficulty. [CRE-W013, §3.1]

The transport constraint makes deployability relevant. The analogy then supplies something concrete to examine and develop. In the authors' model, analogy, association and management of constraints continue to interact as representations and evaluations change. Their account gives a developed

alternative to treating creative work as an unrestricted generation phase followed by an entirely separate judgment phase. [CRE-W013, §2.4]

Across these histories, the work can concern what is varied, what is compared, what becomes a new term and what counts as a promising relation. The technical transformations, mathematical memoir, experiments and observational cases give those questions different forms. Their detailed operations can be studied alongside the accounts of form in S08 and problem structuring in S44.

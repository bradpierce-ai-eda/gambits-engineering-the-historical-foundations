S66 Generating performance in music theater and choreography #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

A performer may need a way to begin, a partner to respond to, a rule that redirects attention, or material that can be varied in rehearsal. The practices described here organize those conditions in different ways. Theater games make a shared problem available for action and observation. Choreographic work develops movement through images, imitation, discussion and recording. Chance composition specifies operations through which material becomes a score. Each account explains a practice in which generating something and learning how to work with it are closely connected.

Spolin and the shared acting problem#

Viola Spolin's Improvisation for the Theater, first published in 1963, organizes learning around games that give actors a concrete problem. Its Point of Concentration is the focus that keeps participants working together. At an early stage it might concern a cup, a rope or a door. Later problems can involve more complex events. Spolin compares the focus to a ball in a game: participants attend to something they can act upon together, rather than continually assess themselves as performers. [CRE-W020, pp. 21–25]

The simplicity of the immediate task is deliberate. Acting involves many simultaneous relationships between body, voice, setting, partners and audience. Spolin divides this complexity into problems that can be experienced. A player can concentrate on the object or action while developing other capacities through participation. The teacher understands how the exercise fits within the larger work, but need not give the player a lecture on every capacity it is intended to develop. The focus supplies enough direction to act and enough openness to respond to what happens. [CRE-W020, pp. 21–25]

Mirror Exercise #1 shows how that arrangement works. Two players face each other. One initiates an ordinary activity such as washing or dressing; the other reflects the movements and expressions as a mirror. They later exchange roles. The task requires attention from head to foot. Spolin asks the observer to notice whether the reflecting player stays with the partner or anticipates a familiar sequence. Knowing what usually happens when someone applies makeup can become an obstacle if it replaces observation of what this person is doing now. [CRE-W020, pp. 60–61]

The initiating player has a different opportunity. Actions can vary in rhythm and inventiveness and can challenge the partner's attention. Spolin also suggests performing the exercise without telling the audience who initiates. Concealing that distinction intensifies the pair's concentration on one another. A small change to the conditions of presentation therefore alters how the exercise is played. Its parts include an activity, differentiated roles, a shared focus and an audience with something specific to observe. [CRE-W020, pp. 60–61]

Side coaching helps sustain the task while the action continues. Spolin recommends short, direct interventions that return attention to the problem: share the stage picture, make contact with an object, continue working. The teacher is responding to what is happening, rather than stopping the experience to deliver an explanation after every difficulty. The intervention must also preserve the nature of the game. A stream of commands directed toward the teacher's approval would replace the shared problem with a different one: satisfying the teacher. [CRE-W020, pp. 28–29]

Evaluation completes the arrangement. Players and observers ask what was actually communicated and whether the concentration was sustained. Spolin recommends questions about the acting problem rather than personal verdicts about a good or bad actor. The teacher joins this inquiry instead of supplying the entire judgment. The audience consequently has an active responsibility: watch closely enough to help the players understand what occurred. Acting, observing, coaching and evaluation become related ways of working on the same problem. [CRE-W020, pp. 25–29]

The discipline of the exercise is compatible with spontaneity in Spolin's account. A player does not have to invent every condition of an encounter before entering it. The task supplies a point of engagement, and its demands change as the other player acts. Her handbook develops this relationship through instructions, coaching suggestions and observations attached to particular exercises. The practical knowledge includes how to keep an exercise alive, not merely its opening rule. [CRE-W020, pp. 21–29, 60–61]

Itani on similar exercises and different explanations#

Nobuhiko Itani's historical comparison of Spolin and Konstantin Stanislavski examines what can differ when an exercise looks similar. Both ask actors to work with objects that are not physically present. A performer must give an action enough detail for an absent object to become part of theatrical experience. Itani relates Spolin's space-object exercises to Stanislavski's work on action without objects, then examines the purposes attached to them. [CRE-W023, §§2.4–2.5]

In Itani's interpretation, Spolin emphasizes contact and present physical activity, while Stanislavski gives belief in the truth of the action a different explanatory role. The similarity of an instruction therefore does not settle its place within a teaching practice. Itani also discusses Spolin's resistance to deliberately recalling past emotions. Her body memory describes experience becoming available through present action, rather than a requirement to retrieve and display an earlier feeling. [CRE-W023, §§2.4–2.5]

This comparison gives a history to the relationship among exercise, attention and explanation. The physical action of handling an absent object can be taught within different accounts of what the actor is learning. Itani's analysis preserves both the overlap and the disagreement, including the distinction between avoiding forced emotional recall and excluding past experience altogether. [CRE-W023, §§2.4– 2.5]

Waterhouse on making and learning Duo#

Elizabeth Waterhouse's Processing Choreography follows William Forsythe's Duo through archival rehearsal videos, interviews and her experience as a dancer. Her account moves between the creation of the piece and the practices through which dancers continued to perform and transmit it. The chronology of the 1996 creation begins with Forsythe developing movement while Jill Johnson and Regina van Berkel observe, imitate and repeat it. Recording is already part of the encounter: he checks that the camera is running as the material takes shape. [CRE-W021, chapter 10, pp. 221–224]

Imagery helps make a movement available to the dancers. A gesture can be described through a steering wheel, a suitcase or another familiar action. The image does not simply name an appearance to reproduce. It gives a direction for making and attending to movement. Waterhouse describes a moment in which Forsythe's steering-wheel image becomes a view over trees for van Berkel, while Johnson imagines something else. Their exchange can proceed with several images in play. What matters in the rehearsal is how those images help the dancers produce and recognize the movement. [CRE-W021, pp. 231–234]

The movement called showerhead receives particularly close attention. Dancers imagine the fingers moving around a circular shower dial. The action begins with an image that seems local to the hand, but its development involves shoulders, torso and both sides of the body. Waterhouse explains the twists and counter-twists through épaulement, the coordinated orientation of the upper body in ballet. The body is not held rigid while a hand draws a circle. The image invites movement that passes through a trained, responsive body. [CRE-W021, pp. 138–146]

Her discussion of Improvisation Technologies gives other examples of this relationship between an image and an operation. A dancer can imagine a line between the fingertips, extend or match lines with the arms, or write in space with different body parts. In the instructional videos, white lines make some of

that imagined geometry visible. The operations begin from knowledge familiar to ballet dancers, including points, lines, circles and planes, then use it to explore unfamiliar movement. Going backward or downward can enlarge a range that habitual movement would leave unused. [CRE-W021, pp. 143–146]

Waterhouse describes these procedures as ways to develop perception and movement rather than routines to reproduce verbatim. Her account of residual response gives the rest of the body an active place in what follows an initiated action. A movement can be deliberately begun and then developed through its physical consequences. Learning involves becoming sensitive to those consequences and coordinating them, as well as acquiring a recognizable shape. [CRE-W021, pp. 143–146]

The office rehearsals show another part of the creative work. Johnson and van Berkel study the recorded material together without Forsythe present. They alternate between watching the small viewing device and getting up to move. They ask where an action begins, which parts of the body participate and what paths they take. The video permits a developing movement to become something that can be inspected repeatedly. Their discussion, bodily attempts and occasional use of a mirror develop its details. [CRE-W021, pp. 234–235]

They also name phrases and make decisions within material that contains variants. Waterhouse recounts how seven phrases from the initial rehearsal became nine sequences as the dancers worked through the movement. Names help the dancers refer to parts and retain them. Forsythe's recorded comments sometimes indicate a preferred version, while other details become settled through the dancers' joint interpretation. Making a piece includes this passage from an improvised event to material that others can remember and perform. [CRE-W021, pp. 233–235]

The wider creation practice permits changes of position, reversals, restrictions and exceptions. Waterhouse's dancers describe an exploratory situation in which neither the final piece nor the precise path toward it is fully known in advance. Their existing skills and shared practices make it possible to continue under those conditions. The detailed Duo history gives this openness a concrete setting: people work with particular movements, images, recordings and one another over time. [CRE-W021, §4.5, pp. 115–117]

Cage and the composition of chance#

In Silence, John Cage describes the procedures used to compose Music of Changes and Imaginary Landscape No. 4. The procedure begins with prepared possibilities. Charts provide elements for sounds, durations, dynamics, tempi and the number of events occurring together. Coin operations derived from the I Ching select among them. Three coins tossed six times yield a hexagram that can be related to one of sixty-four positions. The selection is put to work through the contents and organization of the charts. [CRE-W022, pp. 57–59]

The charts have different behaviors. Some are fixed; others change after an element has been used, allowing a new element to take its place. In the sound charts, silence is represented alongside sounds. A decision about how many events can occur together interacts with the durations and sounds selected for those events. The musical result arises through these connected selections. Chance enters a prepared compositional system whose different components have specific jobs. [CRE-W022, pp. 57–59]

The radio piece changes the relationship between selection and sounding material. Numbers on a tuning dial take the place of specified sounds. A selected position may yield a broadcast, static or silence. Cage accepts what occurs there. The chart can determine a setting while the broadcasting environment supplies what that setting produces. His account identifies the operation clearly enough to show how the composition engages a situation beyond the score. [CRE-W022, pp. 58–59]

Music for Piano 21–52 begins with imperfections in paper. Cage prepares a master page containing musical systems, then uses chance operations to determine how many sounds a page will contain. On a transparent sheet, he marks the corresponding number of imperfections. Registering that sheet against the master allows the points to be converted into musical notation. Further operations determine clefs and characteristics of the tones. A material feature that would ordinarily be ignored becomes an input to composition. [CRE-W022, pp. 60–61]

The procedure combines incidental material with deliberate conventions. The paper supplies points, but the master page supplies their musical interpretation. Decisions about notation and playing methods turn those points into something a pianist can use. Chance operations also work within ranges related to performance difficulty. The process is therefore describable as a sequence: prepare the field, select and mark points, map them onto notation, and determine further features through specified operations. [CRE-W022, pp. 60–61]

Cage also states what remains open for performers. The pieces may be played alone or together, with or without other pieces, and with possible overlap. Durations and dynamics remain free within the conditions described. A programmed concert length can still be maintained through advance calculation and a stopwatch. Determination during composition and freedom during performance occupy different places in this account. Cage's stated aim of freeing continuity from personal taste is expressed through these particular arrangements of choice, material and performance. [CRE-W022, pp. 60–61]

Different purposes for teaching improvisation#

Eeva Siljamäki and Panagiotis Kanellopoulos review music-education research to ask what improvisation is understood to be for. Their analysis of seventy-seven selected articles identifies five visions and eleven approaches. They examine how those views inform an educator's immediate response to learners. A teaching decision about an improvised sound can depend on whether the aim is expression, musical understanding, stylistic accomplishment or the generation of material. [CRE-W024, Method; Results, research question 2]

One vision encourages an open relationship to sound and questions inherited criteria about what counts as music. Another treats improvisation as a way to cultivate expression and social relationships. A third uses it to learn musical structures and skills. In that setting, manipulating tonal and rhythmic material can help learners understand conventions through use, rather than encounter them only as an explanation. The same broad word, improvisation, can therefore identify activities with different educational directions. [CRE-W024, Results, visions I–III]

A fourth vision emphasizes expertise within a musical tradition. Students learn building blocks and stylistic conventions and acquire the capacity to vary them fluently. A fifth emphasizes invention, giving improvisation a place in discovering material for composition. Faithfulness to a tradition and discovery of unfamiliar material can each organize an exercise, but they do not ask the teacher or learner to attend to precisely the same thing. The review makes those purposes available for examination through its map of the literature. [CRE-W024, Results, visions IV–V]

The performing practices in these accounts include setting a problem, sustaining attention, generating movement, preparing a compositional field and choosing how a learner will participate. S41 develops other aspects of shared skilled practice and ensemble coordination. Here the games, rehearsals and scores show how particular working conditions become part of making the performance itself.

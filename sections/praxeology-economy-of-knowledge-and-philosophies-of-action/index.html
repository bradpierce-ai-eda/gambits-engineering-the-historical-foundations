Several different accounts of prepared action#

The sources here examine effective action, economical representation, division of intellectual work, habit and institutions. Some describe how a favorable situation or prepared arrangement supports an action; others ask how people interpret conduct and its purposes.

Kotarbiński: a favorable position relative to a requirement#

Sprawność i błąd includes a chapter on praxeology, the study of effective action. The chapter examines minimizing intervention by finding or creating a favorable situation. A plant requiring moisture is in a positive position with respect to that requirement when its site already supplies the moisture. In a dry site, the gardener must act to provide it. The favorable relation is to a specified property at a later time, not an assertion that the plant is generally independent of conditions. [W019, pp. 100–101]

The example is informative because it identifies the work supplied by the situation. The gardener's reduced action depends on a continuing environmental provision. A condition relevant to one demand can be favorable while other demands remain.

The selected chapter also distinguishes intervention from surveillance or monitoring, alongside potentialization, mechanization and organization as ways of improving action. Reducing the frequency of direct action is different from abandoning readiness to notice and respond. The proposed categories organize possible improvements. [W019, selected chapter, pp. 97–111]

Kotarbiński developed praxeology in Traktat o dobrej robocie (1955) and Sprawność i błąd (first published in 1956 and subsequently expanded). [W018; W019]

Babbage: distribute the intellectual work of calculation#

Babbage's selected chapter on mental labor reports the organization of de Prony's calculation of mathematical tables. It distinguishes people who determine analytical methods, those who translate them into executable arrangements and those carrying out large quantities of elementary arithmetic. The report's interest is not simply that a calculation becomes shorter for everyone. The required knowledge is distributed across roles, with substantial repetitive labor assigned to people who need not know the complete analysis. [W086, chapter XIX, §§183 and 187–193]

The manufacturing analogy retains the intermediate role. An inventor or designer prepares the machinery; operative engineers construct it and understand the relevant processes; other workers use it. Babbage explicitly says that the work and performance of the machines still require supervision by the second group. Less knowledge demanded of an individual operator is not disappearance of the knowledge needed by the undertaking. [W086, §188, first-edition printed p. 158]

The difference-table explanation gives an actual computational mechanism. Successive squares have first differences increasing by a constant second difference. Once appropriate starting values and differences are known, repeated additions can extend the table. The prepared mathematical arrangement changes the later operations required; it does not make their correctness independent of the initial setup and execution. [W086, §§189–193]

Babbage connects the scheme to the possibility of a calculating engine replacing routine arithmetic. In the text that is a proposed development. His account of the French tables is historical author testimony. [W086]

Mach: economical thought represents more than it repeats#

In the opening of his 1882 lecture, Mach starts from thought's limited resources in relation to the richness of experience. Scientific concepts and organized representations can let thought handle relationships without repeating every encounter. The relevant economy concerns representing experience and using thought to anticipate it, rather than possessing an exhaustive duplicate of the world. [W021, chapter XIII, selected opening]

The account treats scientific formulae and conceptual organization as intellectual instruments. Its pertinent claim is about what a representation can spare a thinker from separately retaining or rehearsing. The economy of a description and the operation described remain distinct.

Dewey: habit incorporates an environment#

The discussion of habit in Human Nature and Conduct argues against locating habit wholly inside an isolated person. Walking implicates ground as well as legs, and speech requires air and other people as well as vocal organs. Dewey describes habits as organized skills involving materials, tools and surroundings. An acquired disposition operates in relation to a world that supports and resists it. [GL-W093, selected printed pp. 14–17 and 25–26]

This is more specific than saying that a repeated action becomes automatic. A craft can depend on external implements and arrangements, and moral or social conduct can likewise depend on institutions and other people. The selected account treats habit as a way of using and incorporating an environment. It does not make the environment an inert background or the person irrelevant to action.

The discussion of deliberation examines how habits and impulses enter inquiry when action is unsettled. Considering consequences and possible courses can revise conduct, rather than merely applying a ready-made end to neutral means. Established habits make action possible but can also require change. [GL-W093, selected printed pp. 172–184 and revised-habits passage]

Anderson's selected reconstruction of Dewey's moral philosophy adds the relation between means, ends and social ethics. Ends are examined through the activity and consequences of pursuing them, rather than assumed permanently fixed outside inquiry. The reader can therefore study a philosophy of organized conduct without turning every stable habit into a justified institution. [GL-W007]

Institutional and technical relief#

Turner reads Gehlen's Der Mensch as an account of human beings whose conduct is only partly directed by instinct. Institutions and habits provide a relatively stable cultural setting, reducing repeated demands for motivation and control. Turner presents Entlastung as relief supplied through a taken-for-granted background that permits more focused foreground activity. [UX-W032, introduction, printed pp. 9–11] [UX-W033, through Turner]

The same reconstruction connects Gehlen's account to a conservative criticism of modernity: persistent reflection can destabilize the institutional background on which action relies. This is a controversial philosophical position as presented by Turner. Stability of a background does not settle who benefits from it or bears its costs.

In Die Seele im technischen Zeitalter (1957), Gehlen distinguishes tools that substitute for a bodily capacity, amplify it or relieve people of exerting it. A microscope amplifies sight. A wheeled wagon relieves people of dragging the load themselves. He relates these functions to the ways humans change their surroundings to make action possible. [UX-W039, ch. I, §1]

Spinner gives relief a different setting in his account of scientific institutions. He argues that the separation of inquiry from practice gives researchers room to pursue questions without an immediate obligation to act on the answers or assume responsibility for their use. In his account this separation becomes harder to sustain as research moves into technologies that have to be built, financed and operated. [UX-W034, §§III, V]

Historical time: a persistent structure is not necessarily beneficial#

The selected passages of Braudel's La longue durée direct attention to structures and temporal scales that differ from a short event sequence. A slowly changing arrangement can condition what is possible over many events, without being immediately visible in an event-centered narrative. The relevant contribution is a perspective on duration and constraint. [GL-W051, printed p. 726 and discussion of structure]

Daniel Little's Philosophy of History entry, first published in 2007 and revised in 2025, examines causal mechanisms, contingency and the selectivity of historical explanation as methodological questions. A broad pattern needs evidence about particular processes and contexts. [GL-W050]

Daoism through Hansen: guidance and normative autonomy#

Hansen's selected interpretive account distinguishes strands associated with Laozi and Zhuangzi and examines natural possibilities, guidance and normative autonomy. Its relevance is a philosophical question about how action relates to an order or path without every step being imposed through an explicit command. [GL-W098]

Hansen’s discussion of wuwei concerns guidance and normative autonomy. Mechanical passivity and the absence of effort describe different questions from those philosophical distinctions.

S67 Incubation attention and bodily activity #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

Setting a problem aside can be part of working on it. The interval may contain rest, an undemanding activity or another demanding task, and the return can concern the same problem or a new one. Walking changes another condition of thinking: what the body is doing while a person generates a response. The studies in this section make these arrangements explicit and examine what changes in the resulting work. Poincaré's account in S64 supplies an earlier history of preparation, interruption and renewed mathematical effort.

Sio and Ormerod and the structure of an incubation interval#

Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod's 2009 meta-analysis examines experiments in which people return to a problem after an interval. Their selection contains twenty-nine publications, many reporting several experiments or conditions. The analysis constructs 117 comparisons between an incubation condition and a control condition, adjusting the allocation of shared controls. [CRE-W026, Method]

The review finds a positive average effect of incubation and asks how the arrangement of work relates to it. Preparation matters because the person has already encountered a particular problem before leaving it. The intervening task then occupies the interval in a particular way. A return to that problem is the occasion on which an incubation effect can be observed. [CRE-W026, Results]

Problem type, preparation and the demands of the intervening task differentiate the results. Highly demanding intervening activity tends to be associated with smaller benefits. The relation between low-demand activity and rest varies with the tasks being examined, and preparation time is related to outcomes differently across problem types. The review gives the interval contents and the earlier work a substantive place in the explanation. [CRE-W026, moderator analyses]

Incubation is consequently studied as a sequence, not simply as the passage of time. A task is attempted, an interval changes the person's activity, and performance is examined on return. This structure allows different explanations to be compared and makes it possible to ask which part of the arrangement contributes to an improvement. [CRE-W026, Results and Discussion]

Baird and colleagues and returning to the same material#

Benjamin Baird and colleagues investigate the contents of the interval through an unusual-uses task. Participants first generate uses for objects. The intervening conditions include a demanding task, an undemanding task and rest, alongside a no-break comparison. The break lasts twelve minutes in the interval conditions. Participants subsequently work on previously encountered objects and on new ones. [CRE-W025, Method]

The undemanding condition uses a simple response task, while the demanding condition requires maintaining and updating information. Both occupy participants with something other than the creative problem. They nevertheless make different demands on attention. Including new objects in the later task also helps separate an effect on material already encountered from a general improvement in generating responses to anything. [CRE-W025, Method]

The undemanding task leads to greater improvement in uniqueness on the previously encountered problems than the other conditions. The same advantage does not appear for new problems, and the finding is distinct from simply producing more responses. What changes is connected to returning to material on which work has already begun. [CRE-W025, Results]

Participants in the undemanding condition also report more mind wandering, without reporting more explicitly directed thought about the unusual-uses problems. The authors connect that pattern with their incubation result. The study gives a concrete example of an interval that helps creative work while attention is engaged in a modest external task. It distinguishes the condition that was assigned from the mental activity participants later describe. [CRE-W025, Results and Discussion]

Ritter and Dijksterhuis and explanations of what happens during a break#

Simone Ritter and Ap Dijksterhuis review several explanations of incubation. A break can allow recovery from fatigue, weaken misleading associations, change a mental set or create an opportunity to encounter a useful environmental cue. Their own argument also gives active unconscious processing a role. These accounts describe different changes that could occur while direct attention is elsewhere. [CRE-W027, “Mechanisms Underlying Incubation Effects”]

The review examines studies that vary whether people expect to return to a problem. In one arrangement, participants receive a task and then perform another activity before making a later response. In a comparison condition, participants believe the original task has ended. Both groups can be distracted, but only one retains an active problem-solving goal. The contrast asks whether having a goal during the interval contributes something beyond distraction itself. [CRE-W027, mechanisms section]

The authors also distinguish generating ideas from selecting among ideas. A period without direct deliberation could affect one of those activities differently from the other. Their discussion of studies with and without misleading cues likewise bears on an explanation through forgetting: an improvement where no misleading cue was supplied requires further explanation. These are ways to give competing accounts different empirical implications. [CRE-W027, mechanisms section]

Ritter and Dijksterhuis favour an active contribution from unconscious processes. Their review makes that position part of a developed argument about goals, association, selection and the contents of the interval. The earlier finding that a break helped is a starting point for those questions. The proposed mechanism is presented through the comparisons on which the authors rely. [CRE-W027]

Oppezzo and Schwartz and thinking while walking#

Marily Oppezzo and Daniel Schwartz's fourth experiment separates walking from moving through an outdoor setting. Forty adults are assigned to sitting indoors, walking on a treadmill indoors, being pushed outdoors in a wheelchair or walking outdoors. The moving-wheelchair condition supplies changing outdoor stimulation without requiring the participant to walk. [CRE-W028, Experiment 4]

The task asks participants to generate symbolic analogies for three prompts within five minutes. An appropriate response must express an analogy rather than merely restate the prompt or identify a surface feature. The scoring then distinguishes novelty from quality. High-quality analogies are specific, reach a relatively distant domain and preserve the relevant relational structure. [CRE-W028, Experiment 4, Method]

Walking produces more novel high-quality analogies whether it occurs indoors or outdoors. Ninety-five percent of those who walk produce at least one such analogy, compared with fifty percent of those who sit in the conditions combined. When lower-quality novel analogies are included, outdoor movement also contributes to novelty. The different measures reveal different relationships with bodily activity and surroundings. [CRE-W028, Experiment 4, Results]

The paper's earlier experiments distinguish divergent production from a convergent association task. Its developed account therefore concerns particular kinds of creative response, rather than an undifferentiated improvement in thought. The analogy experiment gives a particularly clear case: a

person receives the same kind of prompt while the body and environment are arranged differently, and the resulting relations among ideas are assessed. [CRE-W028, Experiments 1–4]

These accounts develop several ways of examining activity around a problem. Preparation and return organize work over time; the intervening task changes attention; bodily movement changes a condition under which responses are generated. Their detailed comparisons give substance to the intervals and activities that a brief description of inspiration can leave unexplained.

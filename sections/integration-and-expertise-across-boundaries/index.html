A supplied component does not specify the whole obligation#

The sources address boundaries between firms and suppliers, applications and communication layers, flight crews and automation, and internet design under changing requirements.

Brusoni, Prencipe and Pavitt: knowing can extend beyond making#

Stefano Brusoni, Andrea Prencipe and Keith Pavitt distinguish a firm's production boundaries from its knowledge boundaries. In the selected discussion of complex products such as aircraft engines, the systems integrator can outsource detailed design and manufacture while retaining concept design and integration capabilities. Those capabilities coordinate suppliers' research, design and manufacturing and handle interdependencies at the product level. Buying the component consequently does not settle whether the buyer can also dispense with technological knowledge about it. [W068, abstract and discussion, pp. 617–618]

Their account gives two reasons overlapping knowledge can matter. A rapid advance in one technology can create an imbalance among component performances; an innovative opportunity at a specialized supplier can also make a different system architecture worth exploring. In either case, the assembler needs capabilities to recognize and work with the opportunity, including temporarily tighter links with outside knowledge. The authors call for cognitive overlap between assembler and supplier, rather than treating perfect separability as the definition of every useful supply relationship. [W068, pp. 617–618]

They describe this as a loosely coupled network organization: differentiated production and knowledge activities remain connected in ways that can change as technologies and products change. The selected conclusion also identifies limits. The empirical framework should be extended across sectors, and variables such as appropriability, past research investments and internal coalitions can affect make-or-buy decisions without being captured by their technological categories. [W068, pp. 617–618]

The source's substantive contribution is the explicit distinction between what is performed elsewhere and what must still be understood to integrate and change a complex product.

Saltzer, Reed and Clark: the endpoint must check the actual application result#

Jerome Saltzer, David Reed and David Clark's End-to-End Arguments in System Design asks where a function belongs among distributed-system modules. Its careful-file-transfer example identifies several places a transfer can go wrong: storage, programs, memory and transmission all participate. Making a communication subsystem highly reliable addresses only some of those opportunities. It cannot by itself check the complete application result, including the copy actually written at the destination. [GL-W030, author reprint pp. 1–3]

The alternative procedure retains an end-to-end checksum, comparison and retry/commit plan. After transfer, the destination reads its stored copy back, computes a checksum, and returns it for comparison with the original. Agreement permits the application to commit; disagreement can prompt another attempt. The proposed effectiveness depends on sufficiently rare faults, an appropriate checksum and a correct endpoint procedure. Repeated failures also indicate a need to repair the system, rather than retrying indefinitely while declaring reliability achieved. The detailed sequence shows what knowledge and action remain at the endpoint. [GL-W030, careful-file-transfer example]

The reported gateway incident makes the boundary problem concrete. Per-hop checksums protected transmissions but not data during internal copying in a gateway. A transient copying fault could therefore

alter data between those protected portions. Application programmers' inference that the network already guaranteed the whole required transfer was the mistake the account exposes. [GL-W030, pp. 3–4, A too-real example]

The argument does not make all lower-level reliability useless. Lower-level checks can substantially improve performance by reducing endpoint retries. The choice depends on error rates, relative costs and the application's obligation; an extremely unreliable lower level can make a theoretically correct end-to-end arrangement impractical. The selected source distinguishes completeness of the correctness function from helpful partial support. [GL-W030, pp. 3–4 and performance discussion]

Flight-deck automation: responsibility includes monitoring and procedure revision#

The recommendations of the PARC/CAST Flight Deck Automation Working Group frame the operational goal as flight-path management, rather than merely correct use of automated equipment. Their selected policy recommendation retains pilots' responsibility for that goal. The procedure-design recommendation emphasizes monitoring, cross-verification and allocating tasks between the pilot flying and pilot monitoring. These are identified parts of the supported operational arrangement, not residual activities that become unnecessary when automation is introduced. [GL-W083, recommendations 8–9]

The same passage calls for procedures to be updated using operational experience and research, and for investigation of when and why procedures are not followed. Retained expertise therefore includes learning from the interaction of people, procedures and equipment over time.

Clark and Blumenthal: stakeholders can change the architecture's problem#

David Clark and Marjory Blumenthal's September 2000 working paper revisits end-to-end arguments under a wider range of internet purposes. The institutional abstract identifies new stakeholders, including service providers and government interests, changed user motivations, and a tension between trustworthy overall operation and limited trust in individual users. An architecture chosen for one set of assumptions can therefore face a different task as its users and institutions change. This is the authors' proposed problem. [GL-W095, working-paper abstract]

Their stated concern is that accommodating emerging requirements could compromise support for new and unanticipated applications, one of the original architecture's valued features. That places benefits to one purpose beside possible restrictions on another.

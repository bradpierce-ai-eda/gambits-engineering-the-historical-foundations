What a speaker can furnish#

In the Greek text of the Rhetoric, Aristotle distinguishes evidence already available from means of persuasion a speaker can construct. He differentiates the character the speaker conveys, the hearer’s disposition and demonstration or apparent demonstration in the speech. This last distinction makes room for an address to affect judgment without settling whether its case is true. The University of Geneva’s historical account places instruction in these arts in settings such as advocacy, where the speaker has to address a particular audience about a disputed matter. [T16-W001] [T16-W004]

Preparing speech for an occasion#

Quintilian’s eleventh book treats suitability as a property of speech in its circumstances. Elaborate language can be unsuitable to a small case, just as a light manner can be wrong for a grave one. The account of delivery treats voice and gesture as parts of the speaker’s practice. A written draft can establish the words available to the speaker. How a voice is used or a gesture timed belongs to the performance for which the author offers instruction. [T16-W002]

The received Chinese text of the Guiguzi describes opening and closing in persuasive exchange. Its opening chapter considers when to encourage another person to disclose something and when to close or withhold. The interlocutor’s words give the speaker material from which to consider intent. The chapter develops its own questions about exchange and disclosure. Its traditional attribution and the dating of the received text require separate historical judgment. [T16-W003]

An address in manuscript and circulation#

Helen Dalrymple distinguishes an early draft of the Gettysburg Address from the manuscript prepared for publication in Autograph Leaves in 1864. The Library of Congress describes the occasion as the dedication of a military cemetery on 19 November 1863. Edward Everett had been invited to deliver the main oration. Lincoln was asked to follow him with brief remarks formally setting apart the grounds. [T16-W005] [T16-W006]

In the Hay manuscript, the passage that begins by referring to the dedication of part of the battlefield turns to the soldiers who fought there. Lincoln says their actions have already consecrated the ground beyond the speakers’ power to add to it. He ends by asking the living to dedicate themselves to the unfinished work. Within the text, the object of dedication shifts from the burial ground to the work left to the living. [T16-W006]

The next day Everett wrote to Lincoln that the short remarks had come closer to the central idea of the occasion than he felt his own long oration had. He singled out their simplicity and fitness to the occasion. His letter records the judgment of the speaker who preceded Lincoln. [T16-W007]

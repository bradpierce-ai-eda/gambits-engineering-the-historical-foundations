When delay becomes procrastination#

Piers Steel’s 2007 review defines procrastination through the relationship between an intention, a delay and its expected consequences. A person voluntarily puts off an intended action while expecting the postponement to leave them worse off. The definition makes the prior intention consequential. Evidence that an action started late leaves open whether the person changed their mind, lost an opportunity or chose to wait because the task had become more favorable later. Steel discusses task aversiveness and the delay before a reward as different influences on the appeal of acting now. The histories of intentional waiting in S61 and S62 concern decisions in which postponement can serve a purpose. [T01-W001]

Recognizing delay in ordinary behavior#

Svartdal and colleagues asked how behavioral delay might be examined alongside a questionnaire. Among the routines they considered was bringing lunch from home versus buying it. Participants who generally brought lunch and those who generally bought it had mean Irrational Procrastination Scale scores of 2.71 and 2.99 respectively. The contrast associates a reported routine with a score. Classifying a particular postponement would require further evidence about what the person intended to prepare and what happened to that intention. [T01-W002]

Changing willingness to start#

童廷婷, 白幼玲 and 冯廷勇 investigated positive reappraisal among participants selected for high procrastination. Their report describes two groups of 34 and a week of experience sampling. They assessed willingness to execute the task, how aversive it seemed and the usefulness of the result. At post-test, the positive-reappraisal group reported greater willingness to begin the task and less aversion to it than the comparison group. In the authors’ mediation analysis, reduced aversion accounted for part of the relationship they studied, while increased perceived usefulness did not mediate it. [T01-W003]

Rozental and colleagues’ review pooled 21 comparisons from 12 studies, involving 718 participants. Against inactive controls, the pooled post-treatment difference on procrastination measures was 0.34 standard deviations in favor of treatment, with a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.11 to 0.56. Effects varied considerably among comparisons. Most studies assessed the outcome at the end of treatment. The cue-linked plans discussed in S98 specify when to initiate an action, while the reappraisal study above asks how the task feels when a person considers doing it. [T01-W004]

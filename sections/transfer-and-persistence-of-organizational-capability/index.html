S58 Transfer and persistence of organizational capability #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

A practice working in one place may be difficult to establish elsewhere. These accounts examine the transfer process, the work of reproducing an operating organization and the dependence of an individual’s performance on the setting. They ask what the receiving unit can do and what support makes that possible. S53 develops investment in receiving capacity; the material here follows particular attempts to transfer or reproduce capability.

Szulanski: transferring a practice through actual use#

Gabriel Szulanski’s 1996 study treated the transfer of a best practice as the recreation of coordinating relationships among resources. A practice could already work in one unit while remaining difficult to establish in another unit of the same company. He divided this process into initiation, implementation, ramp-up, and integration. The decision to transfer was followed by exchanges of resources and preparation, then by actual use and attempts to reach satisfactory performance, and finally by efforts to sustain routine use. A signed decision or a completed installation therefore did not exhaust the transfer process. [CG-W017, pp. 28–30]

Szulanski distinguished a transfer’s cost from the difficulty that people experienced while carrying it out. Costs could be hard to interpret without knowing what the same transfer would have cost in the absence of problems. Moreover, two equally expensive problems might place very different demands on an organization. One could be handled through established procedures; another could require senior managers and improvised coordination. His alternative was to examine the transfer’s “eventfulness”: the nonroutine problems that called for special attention. A company with established ways of handling an otherwise demanding transfer might experience it as relatively uneventful. The measure consequently concerned difficulty in relation to the organization undertaking the transfer. [CG-W017, pp. 29–30]

The four stages made that difficulty more specific. Before the decision to transfer, participants had to identify a need, recognize a promising practice, and investigate its suitability. During implementation they had to establish communication, prepare resources, and make adaptations. During ramp-up, actual use exposed problems that preparations had missed. The questionnaire asked, for example, whether trained people left, replacements were poorly qualified, changes introduced further problems, or support teams disbanded because other work intervened. Integration concerned maintaining the practice after satisfactory performance had first been achieved. Temporary workarounds could become habitual, unresolved problems could be displaced by daily pressures, or the recipient could reconsider adoption. These questionnaire items made visible several ways in which a transfer could remain incomplete after an apparently successful start. [CG-W017, pp. 28–30, 41–42]

The survey collected 271 questionnaires concerning 122 transfers of 38 practices in eight companies. Responses came from source units, recipient units, and third parties. The practices required coordinated work by multiple people; they included software-development procedures, drawing standards, appraisal, and activity-based costing. Szulanski measured difficulty partly through the problems participants encountered at each stage. His measures of the recipient’s ability to absorb knowledge included existing technical and managerial competence, clear responsibilities, and knowing whom to approach for help. The relationship between units was measured through the difficulty of communication and each side’s readiness to collaborate. These were organizational conditions of learning, rather than a count of documents delivered. [CG-W017, pp. 32–34, 41–43]

The strongest barriers in the survey analysis were limited recipient absorptive capacity, uncertainty about why the practice worked, and a laborious relationship between source and recipient. Motivational barriers were less prominent in this sample. The first analysis used 87 complete observations; excluding

an outcome measure increased the usable set to 142 and preserved the ordering of the three leading barriers. These were associations among survey measures, with transfers reported four to eight months after first use. Aborted transfers were difficult to include, and the design could not establish that changing a particular barrier would cause a corresponding improvement. Szulanski accordingly presented investment in learning capacity, relationships, and understanding practices as an alternative to relying only on incentives. [CG-W017, pp. 35–38]

The study’s unexpected result for retentive capacity also concerned timing. Its coefficient had the opposite sign from the one anticipated. Szulanski suggested that, only four to eight months after first use, some established routines might still preserve the old practice rather than the new one. Replacing them could itself be difficult. He offered this as a possible interpretation of a survey result, rather than evidence that retaining knowledge generally harms transfer. In interpreting the leading barriers, he instead stressed the recipient’s prior ability to understand and use the practice, uncertainty about its workings, and access to help from the source. [CG-W017, pp. 36–38]

Winter and Szulanski: reproduce the capability to operate#

Winter and Szulanski called a transfer broad in scope when it created or substantially changed the recipient organization’s context. Recreating an outlet capable of producing a service locally required more than relabeling goods received from a central supplier. It involved a collection of routines, people, equipment, supplies, and procedures. The central organization therefore needed knowledge of the features customers valued, the processes producing those features, the resources needed by those processes, and the environments where the combination could work. Their account distinguished copying the visible result from developing the capability to establish and maintain its production elsewhere. [CG-W018, pp. 732–733]

In Replication as Strategy (2001), Sidney Winter and Gabriel Szulanski distinguished a working example, or template, from the features that were both worth reproducing and reproducible elsewhere. Their term for knowledge of those features, how to create them, and suitable environments was the “Arrow core.” A successful outlet might also contain irrelevant details, a manager’s unrepeatable talents, or advantages peculiar to its location. Its success alone did not identify which elements a new outlet needed. The authors described learning through successive attempts, followed by a decision to stabilize a business model for wider replication. [CG-W018, pp. 731–736]

The template supplied something that a description alone could not: a functioning example to consult when a difficulty arose. Yet it could embody many things that a replicator should not reproduce. Winter and Szulanski offered a thought experiment about a successful local business whose profits might depend on an unusually capable manager or on local access to inexpensive skilled workers. They also used the example of a diner whose apple pie might travel well while its proprietor’s decorating tastes would not. Their question was which parts of a successful combination could be reproduced, at what cost, and in which other environments. This was the purpose of distinguishing the Arrow core from the actual outlet that served as a template. [CG-W018, pp. 733–735]

Their Banc One example concerned acquired banks being converted to common computer systems and operating procedures. A recently converted sister bank, selected for comparable systems and market conditions, supplied a working reference. Staff could visit it, receive training, and obtain temporary specialist assistance. A separate model bank provided a place to try new systems and procedures without disturbing an operating bank. The central organization’s job included selecting examples, training staff, and coordinating the conversion, rather than merely distributing software and instructions. [CG-W018, pp. 737–738]

Banc One Systems Corporation coordinated the conversion. In the authors’ reported history, departmental work initially proceeded with limited coordination and tacit procedures. The later creation of conversion management brought documentation, meetings across application groups and explicit attention to dependencies. Problems encountered during conversions became checkpoints and tasks. The organization doing the transfer developed alongside the transfers it carried out. [CG-W018, pp. 737–739]

The authors also examined combining selected features from different outlets. A proposed outlet might borrow one element from a St. Louis design and another from a San Francisco innovation. They argued that such a combination could introduce interactions absent from either original. The same employees might, for example, have to carry out both activities, creating conflicts over schedules and workloads. A mix of individually successful parts was consequently a different proposition from copying an operating whole. The authors allowed that new combinations could produce favorable interactions as well as problems. Their hypothesis favored guidance by a working template during exploitation; it did not establish that every combination or adaptation would fail. [CG-W018, pp. 735–736]

The authors proposed that using a working template, and initially copying it closely, could improve replication once early exploration had produced a satisfactory model. They also described the costs of this approach: copying irrelevant features, delaying improvement, and reproducing a design poorly suited to its new environment. Their precision-versus-adaptation argument was a set of hypotheses, illustrated by reported organizational experience. The Banc One account described 1970–1995 and did not establish a controlled comparison of replication methods. [CG-W018, pp. 735–739, endnotes 2, 7–8]

Huckman and Pisano: performance that depends on the setting#

Robert Huckman and Gary Pisano’s The Effect of Organizational Context on Individual Performance (2003) examined whether a skilled person’s performance could be carried unchanged between organizations. Their setting was coronary artery bypass graft surgery in Pennsylvania in 1994–1995. Many surgeons worked at more than one hospital during the same period, while nurses, technicians, anesthesiologists, and other resources were supplied by the hospitals. This allowed the researchers to compare a surgeon’s results across organizations without relying solely on a move from one employer to another many years apart. The underlying records covered more than 38,000 procedures by 203 surgeons at 43 hospitals. [CG-W019, pp. 1–10]

The principal regressions treated death in hospital following an individual procedure as the outcome. They included patient characteristics and clinical conditions, calendar-quarter effects, and measures of prior surgeon and hospital quality. The key comparison divided a surgeon’s case volume in the preceding quarter into procedures at the hospital where the current operation occurred and procedures at other hospitals. Using prior-quarter volume was intended to reduce reverse-causality concerns. A specification with hospital fixed effects checked whether the relationship survived a different way of accounting for stable hospital differences. The study did not randomly allocate surgeons or patients between hospitals. [CG-W019, pp. 11–18]

Higher volume at the current hospital was associated with a lower probability of death; volume at other hospitals had a much smaller, statistically insignificant association. In the first hospital-specific specification, a one-standard-deviation increase in local volume corresponded to a 0.34-percentage-point reduction relative to a predicted mortality probability of 1.8 percent. The corresponding estimate for volume elsewhere was 0.01 percentage points. The local and elsewhere coefficients differed at the 3 percent significance level in the first four specifications, including the hospital-fixed-effects comparison. These results concerned where a surgeon had recently performed procedures, rather than treating all of that surgeon’s experience as interchangeable. [CG-W019, pp. 17–18]

Huckman and Pisano considered two explanations for this hospital specificity. Familiarity with a hospital’s people and resources could improve coordination. Alternatively, a surgeon accounting for a large share of the hospital’s business might obtain preferential access to its resources. To investigate the latter explanation they added the surgeon’s share of the hospital’s procedure volume. Local volume retained its association with mortality, but the statistical distinction between local volume and volume elsewhere weakened to the 13 percent level. The share measure was only a proxy for influence and was correlated with local volume. The authors accordingly described the separation of familiarity and influence as suggestive. The data did not identify whether particular employees, team structures, or operating routines were responsible. [CG-W019, pp. 18–22]

Their staffing implications followed from this limited portability of measured performance. Hiring a highly skilled individual might not reproduce the results that person achieved with another organization’s complementary resources. The study also raised a question about dividing skilled workers among projects or units. Its evidence remained specific to cardiac surgery in this period. It could not distinguish learning to work together from a stable compatibility between a surgeon and a hospital. The authors’ estimates of the benefits from consolidating split practice were thought experiments under simplifying assumptions, rather than observed results from a consolidation program. [CG-W019, pp. 4–6, 19–22]

S07 and S12 develop neighboring accounts of organizational knowledge and retained expertise. The studies here concern the work of recreating a practice and the conditions under which demonstrated performance can be sustained in another setting.

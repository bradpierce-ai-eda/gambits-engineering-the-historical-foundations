Reaching a candidate pool#

The French study coordinated by Yannick Fondeur and Guillemette de Larquier distinguishes reaching candidates from assessing them after they come into view. Its Table 1 reproduces an analysis by de Larquier and Marchal of the 2005 Dares Offre d’emploi et recrutement survey. In 35 percent of the reported cases, the establishment examined one application per vacancy. In that group, personal and professional networks and contacts with former staff together accounted for 49.8 percent of the channels reported to have brought in the most candidates. Press or internet advertising accounted for 4.3 percent. In the group with more than ten applicants per vacancy, the corresponding shares were 7.2 percent for networks and 31.2 percent for advertising. Those percentages describe recruitment cases in each group, classified by the channel that brought in the most candidates. [T05-W001]

The authors distinguish a channel that brings a candidate already known or recommended to the employer from a channel that invites a wider field to apply. In their retail fieldwork, some employers placed a notice inside the shop or advertised only in the local press to limit the response or reach people living nearby. Even within advertising, the place where an employer announces a vacancy can serve as an initial filter. S13 discusses other ways access to occupational opportunities is organized. [T05-W001]

Assessment goes in both directions#

Wilhelmy and Kleinmann illustrate reciprocal selection through a publishing house. A potential candidate sees the vacancy, checks the publisher’s site and reads an employer portal. Inside the publishing house, staff identify job requirements and choose ways of assessing them. The candidate’s own inquiry also continues during the contact. The authors discuss the tension between a standardized interview, which gives applicants a more comparable assessment, and room for the two parties to learn about each other. [T05-W002]

What a validity estimate describes#

In their 1998 review, Schmidt and Hunter distinguish the validity of a single predictor from that of a combination. In Table 1, their estimated correlation with job performance is 0.51 for the structured interview alone and 0.63 for a combination of the interview and a general mental ability test. The second value estimates the performance of the pair. [T05-W003]

Sackett and colleagues revisited the adjustments behind estimates of predictive validity. When people are hired partly on the basis of a test, their scores can have less spread than the scores of the full applicant pool. A study of hired workers can then understate the relationship between test scores and later performance. A correction needs a defensible estimate of how much variation was lost. The authors question applying a correction from studies with evidence of restriction to other studies where the selection process could have been different. [T05-W004]

Their reanalysis of job-specific knowledge tests gives a concrete example. In the 59 studies of such tests assembled by Dye and colleagues, the observed mean correlation with job performance was 0.31. Correcting for unreliability in the performance measure raised it to 0.40. Dye and colleagues also assumed that selection directly on the test retained half the applicants, yielding an estimate of 0.62 after the additional adjustment. Sackett and colleagues retained 0.40 as a conservative estimate from that evidence. The selection ratio had been assumed, and the original analysis did not identify which studies followed applicants into the job and which tested workers already employed. The difference between 0.62 and 0.40 is therefore a difference in the treatment of the same body of studies. [T05-W004]

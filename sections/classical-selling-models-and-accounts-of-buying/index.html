Attention, interest, desire, action and satisfaction#

AIDAS describes five successive objects of attention in a selling encounter: attention, interest, desire, action and satisfaction. In Pramod Kumar Nayak’s teaching account, the seller first establishes rapport and a receptive audience, then develops the relevance of the offering. A presentation can attract attention without producing interest, and interest can remain weaker than a desire to obtain the benefit. Objections and the buyer’s concerns enter the work of developing that desire. The seller must then recognize an appropriate point to seek a purchase, rather than assuming that wanting something automatically produces action. [SL-W003]

The Sales and Distribution Management course illustrates the sequence through insurance and family security. The concern for a family’s future gives a proposed policy its relevance; the presentation connects the policy to that concern, addresses questions and seeks the purchase. Satisfaction extends the account beyond closing. Delivery, reassurance and subsequent service bear on whether the experience fulfils what the buyer expected. AIDAS consequently gives follow-through a place within the selling sequence rather than treating the order as its sole endpoint. [SL-W004]

What the early formulations meant by a completed sale#

Akinori Iwamoto’s historical investigation questions the familiar attribution of AIDA to E. St. Elmo Lewis in 1898. He compares dated formulations and their later retelling, including Strong’s accounts, rather than treating repetition of an attribution as evidence for it. His reconstruction distinguishes Dukesmith’s connected selling argument in 1903–1904, Sheldon’s discussion of conviction, resolve and action, and Brown’s 1904 ordering of the familiar four terms. Differences among these formulations matter because they concern what completes a sale. [SL-W005] Iwamoto argues that Sheldon can be regarded as the originator and that Sheldon and Dukesmith contributed decisively to formulation; this is the historical conclusion advanced by his reconstruction. [SL-W005]

Conviction that an offering is suitable, a resolve to buy and an actual purchase are different accomplishments. An account that ends with conviction may leave the transition to action unexplained; adding another mental state does not by itself identify the practical step that completes the transaction. Iwamoto uses the early texts to examine this distinction as part of the history of the formula. His argument contests an inherited origin narrative while preserving the differences among the formulations that narrative had grouped together. [SL-W005]

One of the Sheldon discussions reproduced by Iwamoto concerns the retail customer who arrives knowing what is wanted. Attention, interest, desire and resolve may already have developed before the encounter. The seller can then assist completion instead of recreating each state through a fresh presentation. This example qualifies a mechanical use of the familiar sequence: where the encounter begins depends on what the customer has already done. [SL-W005]

The right set of circumstances#

The Right Set of Circumstances account directs attention to a seller’s use of the situation. Suitable circumstances, stimuli and appeals are expected to secure attention and interest and produce the desired response. Nayak presents it as a seller-oriented situation–response formulation. The practical object is the selling setting and the action taken within it, rather than only the sequence of mental states named by AIDAS. [SL-W003]

The university-course exposition similarly makes the seller’s creation or use of circumstances central. Choosing an appeal, arranging the occasion and presenting it to a particular prospect belong to the proposed influence process. The formulation is a teaching account of what the seller should attend to; it does not provide a calibrated prediction of how a given buyer will respond. [SL-W004]

Need, product, source and satisfaction in the Buying-Formula#

The Buying-Formula treats a purchase as a set of connections on the buyer’s side. A need or problem is connected to a product or service, then to a particular trade name or source, purchase and satisfaction. Recognizing a kind of solution differs from accepting a particular supplier. A buyer may want the product category while remaining unconvinced that this source will supply it satisfactorily. Nayak’s teaching diagram makes both connections explicit. [SL-W003]

At the product/service connection and again at the trade-name connection, the account places adequacy alongside a pleasant anticipation of satisfaction. The proposed purchase must answer the problem, and thinking about it must carry an agreeable expectation. The seller’s problem-solving work therefore concerns more than making the brand familiar: it may concern whether the product will do the job, whether the supplier can be relied upon, or what experience the buyer anticipates. Satisfaction after purchase feeds the continuing relationship and later buying. The corresponding university-course exposition develops these links as a guide to understanding buying. [SL-W003; SL-W004]

Drive, cues and reinforcement in the Behavioral Equation#

Nayak’s Behavioral Equation connects a buying response with learning. Its printed expression is B = P × D × K × V: B is the buyer’s response, P predisposition or habit, D drive or motivation, K incentive potential, and V the intensity of cues. Incentive potential concerns the product’s value or potential satisfaction. A familiar response can thus be supported by habit while still depending on motivation and the attractiveness of the anticipated result. [SL-W003]

The account distinguishes cues that trigger a decision from cues that influence a choice without initiating one. Product colour, weight and price can provide product cues; advertising and conversation can provide informational cues. The categories concern what a cue does in the decision: a price can also trigger action. A rewarding result reinforces the response and can strengthen the predisposition to respond when a cue appears again. [SL-W003]

Multiplication makes a strong claim inside the formulation: if drive is zero, the proposed response is zero even when habit is strong. The slides use that implication to explain why predisposition alone is insufficient. They supply meanings and a learning account rather than fitted coefficients or a probability scale for estimating purchases. [SL-W003]

Confidence, evidence and participation in the customer’s work#

The chapter of 销售管理实务, edited by 安贺新, compares several ways of organizing a selling argument. GME concerns the seller’s own confidence in the goods, the enterprise and personal capability. FABE separates features, advantages, benefits and evidence, making support for a benefit claim a distinct part of the presentation. The six steps printed under DIPDA move through definition, identification, proof, acceptance, desire and action. The related discussion connects discovering a need, showing the offering’s relevance, demonstrating it and obtaining acceptance. [SL-W006]

The chapter also distinguishes attending to customer needs in order to sell a fixed product from participating more broadly in the customer’s work. Its consultative comparison gives the seller roles as team member, adviser and partner. Two-way information, knowledge of the customer’s business,

participation in decisions and subsequent service become part of the relationship. These distinctions appear in the third edition of a work first published in 2009. [SL-W006]

The Changan illustration brings a service adviser and technician into continued contact with the customer. They visit, inspect the vehicle and answer questions; the chapter reports renewed intentions following that contact. The pairing joins technical examination to the service relationship, extending the encounter beyond an initial product argument. [SL-W006]

S88 Organizing product development and evolution #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

Product management continues through development, introduction, use, revision and withdrawal. Its work includes deciding what information is needed before a commitment, which aspects of a design can remain changeable, and how an existing offering fits a changing portfolio. The following accounts examine processes and continuing responsibilities across that span.

Stages of work and gates of commitment#

Smolnik and Bergmann's 2020 review traces the evolution of the Stage-Gate process. Stages gather and analyze information; gates decide whether further resources should be committed. Discovery and screening lead through scoping and a business case toward development, validation and launch, with post-launch review. The distinction between work and authorization explains how a process can organize inquiry while reserving a decision about proceeding. [MG-W009]

The review also describes adaptations involving iteration and agile work. Iterating within a stage and deciding whether to continue the larger undertaking need not be the same event. That distinction allows repeated development cycles to coexist with broader investment decisions. The authors discuss the evidence available for hybrids, including its limitations for some physical-product applications. Their historical review presents a changing family of processes rather than a single unaltered sequence. [MG-W009]

Making changes less expensive#

Thomke and Reinertsen define development flexibility through the economic cost of modifying a product under development. Its value depends on what may change and when. Improving a forecast can reduce surprises; changing a process or technology can make a later revision cheaper. Their account considers both routes and explains why a design strategy should reflect the cost of changing its underlying technology. [MG-W010]

Progressive freezing makes commitments piece by piece. A requirement or design feature can be approved when sufficient information is available, while other features remain open. Qualifying a backup material early similarly prepares an alternative before it becomes urgently needed. These arrangements require identifying which changes have consequential downstream effects and which permissions can be granted independently. [MG-W010]

The Boeing 777 illustration makes authorization granular. A part drawing could have six or more release levels, permitting such different downstream activities as purchasing material, designing tools or machining them. Releasing a drawing was therefore a statement about what someone could safely begin, rather than a declaration that every detail was final. The authors contrast this with one release that freezes the entire part at its procurement lead time. [MG-W010]

Their tooling-shop discussion also changes the criterion used to manage a supporting activity. Minimizing the unit cost of a tool can delay an iteration whose timing matters to the larger program. Delivery timing brings that relationship into the measure. Their survey of 391 integrated-circuit projects compares development using technologies with different change costs; respondents were not randomly assigned to those technologies. The comparison supports their investigation of flexibility and development strategy within that observed setting. [MG-W010]

Actual processes and long-lived platforms#

Jetter, Albar and Sperry compare prescribed processes with development practice through literature and high-technology cases. Their measurement-tool company maintains a platform over years while adapting interchangeable heads or modules. Several projects can therefore contribute to the evolution of an offering whose life exceeds that of any one project. Platform continuity and project closure are different time horizons. [MG-W011]

The case research describes departures from standard processes, including agile software development and decisions concentrated in the owner of a small firm. Its second phase includes twelve project cases and interviews concerning project management offices. These qualitative accounts show why a standard process may be modified and how monitoring is carried out. They also distinguish disciplined management of projects from choosing a successful product mix: following a process supplies information about execution without by itself determining whether the right offerings were selected. [MG-W011]

Strategy throughout the product's life#

Bauch's 2020 process navigator distinguishes product, product-portfolio and enterprise strategy. A product moves through definition, development, launch, growth and improvement toward withdrawal, while its business plan and strategy continue alongside that work. The roadmap communicates a time dimension across functions. It connects intended changes with the continuing offering rather than serving only as a schedule for an initial release. [MG-W012]

The portfolio perspective compares the current and intended offering mix. Introducing one product and phasing out another must be coordinated with product-level plans and enterprise direction. Bauch also identifies supporting work, such as troubleshooting and assisting sales, that competes for attention with strategic work. His practitioner framework makes these continuing processes visible as product-management responsibilities. [MG-W012]

Springer and Miler investigate software product-management challenges through a literature review, ten interviews and an 89-person survey. Reported difficulties include establishing customer value, changing priorities, technical debt, organizational silos and balancing reactive with proactive work. Their categories distinguish core product decisions from participation in related work and orchestration across functions. The product manager's responsibility can consequently extend across activities over which that person has no direct command. [MG-W013]

The study reports perceived frequency and severity. These reports describe difficulties experienced in practice, including continuing maintenance and withdrawal as well as development. Together with the lifecycle frameworks, they give substance to product management after delivery: the offering, its dependencies and the organization supporting it continue to change. S62 develops learning before commitment, and S80 examines the connection between sales work and customer value. [MG-W013]

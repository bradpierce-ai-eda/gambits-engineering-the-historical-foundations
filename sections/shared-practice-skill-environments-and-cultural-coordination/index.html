Organized action has purposes as well as demands#

These sources examine how people act with environments, learned skills, conventions, other people and material things. Their purposes include artistic performance, education, cultural participation, religious practice and therapy. The value of these activities is not exhausted by minimizing effort.

Kirsh and Maglio: an action can change what is easy to know#

Kirsh and Maglio distinguish pragmatic actions, which advance a physical task, from epistemic actions, whose principal function is to improve cognition. Their Tetris account considers translations and rotations that can look unnecessary when judged only as moves toward a final board position. An action can instead expose information that is difficult to compute mentally, reduce a memory demand or make an error less likely. The extra movement can be part of a better problem-solving process. [W041, selected manuscript pp. 2–5 and 40–43]

The definition separates the apparent physical distance to a goal from the informational work needed to act. An external intermediate state can make a distinction visible. A reminder or partially organized work area can similarly change the memory or search task. Its informative contribution is that an account of action can miss its purpose if it considers only direct physical progress.

Maglio and Kirsh’s 1996 Epistemic Action Increases With Skill reports a relation between skill and actions such as backtracking, qualifying the supposition that expertise always eliminates such movements. [GL-W027, abstract and introductory and result passages]

Todd and Gigerenzer describe a shortcut for predicting the winner of a Wimbledon match. If you recognize only one of the two players, predict that player will win. They explain why recognition can be informative even for tennis novices. In a setting where successful players are more often discussed, knowing a name provides a clue about performance. [W040, pp. 167–168]

The other authors discussed here draw on Gibson's The Theory of Affordances. [W042, through the accounts below]

Ecological interfaces: display functional relations rather than isolated readings#

Vicente and Rasmussen tested two displays for a simulation of a system of tanks, pipes and heaters. Both showed physical settings and target values. One also showed higher-level information about how the system was functioning. After viewing each sequence for 25 to 30 seconds, participants recalled values and diagnosed the system state. Diagnosis was more accurate with the display that included functional information, chiefly among participants with theoretical expertise. The authors report better recall of functional variables with the richer display on trials showing normal operation or a fault. More accurate recall of those variables was associated with more accurate diagnosis. [TA-W026, §VII]

Shi and Zhang's 2004 review explains an abstraction hierarchy and the distinction among skill-, rule- and knowledge-based behavior. A work domain must be represented in its complexity, while its information must also be communicated to an operator. Means–ends links can show how a lower-level variable relates to a higher-level purpose, rather than merely arranging readings by part–whole location. [TA-W027, printed pp. 65–67]

Shi and Zhang also review studies of fault detection and navigation. They report advantages for displays that include functional information in demanding tasks, with normal-operation completion times sometimes similar across displays. [TA-W027]

The review also addresses training, self-explanation and individual differences. The review explicitly limits generalization from DURESS studies. Its assertions about fixed cognitive styles are not adopted as settled current psychology. A useful display still depends on the information pathway and the operator's acquired understanding; fewer visible numbers or a cleaner appearance alone do not establish the effect. [TA-W027]

Sweller: solving a problem and acquiring a schema can compete#

The analysis in Sweller's 1988 article distinguishes successful immediate problem solving from learning a reusable schema. His explanation concerns means–ends search: repeatedly comparing a current state with a goal and seeking moves that reduce the difference can consume processing capacity without directing attention to the relations needed for later generalization. Effort spent reaching an answer is not automatically effort spent learning how to solve a class of problems. [TA-W035, printed pp. 257–259, 273 –274 and 284]

The paper discusses domain-specific knowledge rather than a general superiority in memory or search. Reported expert–novice contrasts concern meaningful configurations, not simply random items. Its computational model and educational examples support a proposal for changing instructional tasks, including worked examples or less specific goals under appropriate conditions.

Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller's review of the expertise-reversal effect adds a material qualification. Guidance that helps a novice can become redundant or interfere once a learner has acquired the relevant schemas. Instructional support has to be assessed in relation to existing knowledge, rather than assigned one permanent benefit. [TA-W036, printed pp. 23 and 29]

Wood, Bruner and Ross: a tutor carries part of the task#

The selected discussion of tutoring specifies six functions: recruitment, reduction of degrees of freedom, direction maintenance, marking critical features, frustration control and demonstration. The tutor can temporarily handle parts of a task that the learner cannot yet manage, leaving a component within reach. That arrangement explains the scaffold metaphor more precisely than simply saying assistance makes learning easier. [GL-W016, printed pp. 97–99]

Direction maintenance is not the same as repeated instruction. A learner can remain attached to a simpler success and need support to attempt the next step. Marking a critical feature also requires interpreting the discrepancy between the learner's production and what would satisfy the task. Demonstration is more than doing the task alongside the learner; it supplies a modeled solution relevant to the learner's attempt.

The source is a small tutoring study with proposed instructional functions. The tutor's knowledge, attention and judgment remain active. A reduced task for the learner is a distribution of work in this account, not evidence that the rest of the task has ceased to exist. [GL-W016]

Billett and Orth: participation is not guaranteed by an available setting#

Billett's abstract frames workplace learning through both workplace affordances and individual engagement. Opportunities offered by a workplace do not compel a person to learn, and participation can depend on how access and activities are distributed. [GL-W052]

The abstract and implications discussion in Orth, van der Kamp and Button's article situate adaptive learning across a coach–athlete system. In their constraints-led interpretation, coaching helps shape conditions under which an athlete explores and adapts. The coach's design, observation and adjustment remain part of the arrangement. [GL-W071]

Ferrer i Cancho and Solé: a model with competing effort proxies#

The language article models a binary association matrix linking signals and referents. It assigns a speaker-side effort proxy using signal entropy and a hearer-side proxy using uncertainty about the referent conditional on a signal. Its stated objective combines the two with a weight. A single signal can be economical under one proxy while leaving the hearer unable to distinguish referents; a large differentiating vocabulary places a different demand on the speaker. [WME-W010, equation 9 and model definition]

The authors optimize the associations and report a transition between regimes, with a Zipf-like frequency pattern near a transition in their model. The proposition is not merely that language is short. It concerns a balance between the modeled demands of producing signals and distinguishing what they refer to, with assumptions including uniformly treated referents and the permitted association structure.

The effort variables are proxies, not direct measurements of all human effort or physical energy. The paper's governing objective in equation 9 specifies the tradeoff used in its model. [WME-W010]

Kirby and colleagues: learnability and expressivity are different pressures#

Kirby, Tamariz, Cornish and Smith examine cultural transmission and communication using models and artificial-language experiments. Their claim is that compositional structure can arise when a language must be both learnable by new users and sufficiently expressive for communication. Compressibility alone can favor degenerate systems that merge distinctions. Expressivity alone can preserve holistic forms that work for established users without being easy for new learners. [TA-W034, selected PDF pp. 2 –3 and 8–9]

The experiment contrasts chains introducing fresh pairs of learners with closed groups in which the same pair continues. Training, communication and transmission occur in both, but the introduction of naive learners changes the learning pressure. The design uses sixty students and twelve meanings. Its initial holistic languages and controlled tasks are part of the evidence, not incidental details to be discarded when generalizing.

The selected discussion explicitly treats the appearance of structure as contingent rather than inevitable. Their relevant contribution is a specified account of how communication and transmission can impose different demands on a shared system. Underlying natural-language examples reported in the discussion remain mediated. [TA-W034, selected PDF p. 13]

Nagy: formulas support reperformance without fixing an exact text#

Nagy's discussion of oral poetry examines composition in performance and the relation between text and reperformance. In the oral-poetic account, formulas and conventions can support a performance as it is made. It is misleading to assume that the performer's task is always verbatim preservation of one prior written text. The identity and success of a reperformance belong to the practice under discussion. [GL-W013, §§1.3–2]

The selected account is relevant because a shared repertoire can organize action without an explicit complete script for every event. It does not follow that the repertoire is merely compressed text or that its only value is reducing memory effort.

Goodman through Giovannelli: removing distinctions can change the work#

The selected scholarly reconstruction of Goodman's aesthetics examines work identity and notation. A score can specify properties a performance must possess to count as an instance of a work. Other arts give different significance to production history and authenticity. The relevant question is what distinctions are constitutive of the work, rather than how much descriptive detail can be removed without any stated criterion. [WME-W009, selected main-text paragraphs 14–15 and 46–48]

This is a useful limit on an economy interpretation. A convenient simplification may change what is being performed or preserved, instead of producing the same work with less effort.

Musical ensemble: common frames support skilled mutual adjustment#

Keller, Novembre and Loehr's chapter distinguishes musical dimensions and explains an underlying metric frame. Rhythmic patterns can be coordinated relative to temporal units organized into beats and bars, while pitch relations create another aspect of joint organization. A shared framework can help performers locate their own and others' parts without making those parts identical. [GL-W001, manuscript PDF pp. 2–3]

The discussion retains representation of self, others and joint outcomes. Skilled performance involves anticipation and adjustment as well as common structure. Expressive timing and the desired musical result matter; strict mechanical simultaneity is not a universal success criterion.

Vesper and colleagues' joint-action review similarly considers shared information and cultural coordination while calling for a more overarching framework. Conventions can help align expectations, but their presence does not eliminate contextual adaptation or prove that every joint action relies on one mechanism. This is a review-level account with its own open framework question. [GL-W002]

Bitner: the physical service setting is part of action#

Bitner's selected Servicescapes passages distinguish spatial layout and functionality from signs, symbols and artifacts. The arrangement of equipment, furniture and movement routes can affect whether people can carry out a service task. Signs can communicate where to go or what conduct is expected. Customers and employees may occupy the same production setting, so an environment can shape more than one participant's experience. [GL-W005, opening framework and printed pp. 65–66]

These are conceptual propositions supported by reported prior work. [GL-W005]

Lucas: a behavior setting includes a maintained pattern#

Lucas's field guide reconstructs behavior-settings theory and provides an operative method. A room or physical milieu alone is insufficient. A setting includes a standing pattern of behavior in relation to a bounded physical and temporal environment. The pattern can persist as different people enter and leave: a shop open for business is more than the walls of the shop. People create and maintain the norms and material conditions to which they subsequently conform. [SG-W026]

The method also examines interdependence within a proposed setting and between neighboring ones. Structural and dynamic tests help determine the boundary rather than accepting an architectural label. The gallery application and K21 method illustrate the observational work involved. Identifying a stable-looking setting requires attention to what people actually do, how parts depend on one another and where the pattern stops.

Lucas's method provides a way to describe and investigate recurring behavior settings, addressing their boundaries through observation and interpretation. [SG-W026]

Maller and Hyysalo: practices are performed, reproduced and contested#

Maller's selected practice-theory discussion relates materials, competences and meanings. A practice as an entity is distinguishable from its particular performances. The breakfast example places an apparently simple activity within interwoven practices and material arrangements. Knowing how to act is not represented solely as information stored in one person's head; it is connected to equipment, routines and understandings reproduced through performance. [GL-W034, practice and materiality discussion]

Reproduction is an accomplishment. Changing a material element can alter a practice, but competence, meaning and other practices can sustain or obstruct the change. Maller's account also notes differences among materialist and practice approaches over agency, which are not resolved by grouping them under one heading.

Hyysalo's two-page review of that book supplies a separate critical reconstruction. It questions the adequacy of illustrative evidence and the abstraction involved in accounts of how practice elements are transmitted and connected. This is a useful challenge to an attractive diagram: naming elements and links is not yet a complete empirical account of how a practice changes. [GL-W035, printed pp. 41–42]

Li, Holstein and Wedekind: material resistance participates in knowing#

The selected qualitative study of craft draws on observation, interviews with twenty studio potters and an eighteen-month participation context. It treats knowing as something enacted through relations among bodies, clay, tools and surroundings. Resistance, unexpected encounters and changing material behavior can alter how a practitioner senses and acts. A learned hand is not working on an inert object whose behavior is already completely known. [GL-W038, selected methods and interpretive findings]

The relevant contribution is a detailed account of situated adjustment and the material conditions of learning. The authors' language of material agency belongs to their interpretive framework. The study retains practice and encounter rather than assuming that knowledge can be fully transferred as a detachable verbal instruction.

Public cultural guidance: knowledge has custodians and conditions of use#

The Assembly of Alaska Native Educators' Guidelines for Respecting Cultural Knowledge addresses researchers, language specialists and community organizations in its own setting. The selected pages call for informed consent, accurate representation, protection of cultural integrity and attention to community expertise. They distinguish public knowledge from sensitive information whose distribution has not been authorized. [GL-W003, selected preface and printed pp. 15–20]

The recommendations also concern local review, accurate interpretation, language and community repositories. Their relevance is that circulation of knowledge involves relationships, rights and responsibilities, not only an efficient transmission channel. The document is not represented as the view of every Indigenous people.

Armstrong and colleagues’ ethnoecological discussion concerning Gitxsan and nłeʔkepmx territories makes a related but distinct argument. It locates stewardship in laws, institutions, beliefs and relational views of land. Its account explicitly resists treating reciprocity merely as a useful management tool: reciprocity can be a moral or legal obligation within the described relationships. Reducing the account to a technique for lowering management effort would change its stated purpose. The authors also criticize extractive use of knowledge. [GL-W004, selected discussion]

Xunzi through Wang: ritual and custom are not the same category#

Wang Weiwei's scholarly abstract discusses the roles of ritual propriety and custom in Xunzi's thought. The relevant contrast concerns different ways that human conduct is shaped through learned forms and habitual social practice. [GL-W008]

礼义 and 习俗 have distinct normative and cultural roles in the interpretation.

Ritual and ascetic practice can involve deliberately sustained effort#

The Stockholm University field orientation presents asceticism and ritual self-practices as subjects of religious-studies inquiry. The relevant account concerns practices through which people cultivate or transform themselves in relation to religious aims. Reducing effort need not be their purpose; disciplined effort can be constitutive of the activity. [GL-W037]

The abstract and ritual-components discussion in Boyer and Liénard's article explicitly resist treating ritual as one scientific mechanism. Their proposed components occur in different combinations. The coalitional discussion concerns signals of affiliation, commitment and cohesion; costly initiation or training can be meaningful as a signal because commitment is difficult to establish in advance. It is an account of a proposed mechanism within some practices. [GL-W042]

An action that appears repetitive or costly under an external task criterion can have a different meaning within the practice.

Assistance includes services and participation around an object#

The WHO framework describes assistive technology as more than a product. Its five-part approach brings people together with products, provision, personnel and policy. A device's usefulness depends on selection, fitting, learning, maintenance, access and the wider service environment. A product can enable participation while still requiring continuing support. [GL-W041, 2024 framework and GATE five-P account]

Its relevance lies in the explicit support work that an apparently simple aid can depend on.

Personal constructs: interpreting a situation is itself an organized activity#

Gaines's introduction presents Personal Construct Psychology through systems of constructs by which people anticipate and interpret events. It identifies research and elicitation resources, including repertory grids. [WME-W017]

The George Kelly Society FAQ supplies a field-native description, including the distinction from claims that a person simply creates reality. Its account of repertory-grid elicitation retains the respondent's distinctions and the interviewer's influence. A structured grid can make an interpretive system discussable, but eliciting it requires skill and can introduce bias. [WME-W018]

Repertory-grid elicitation makes assumptions explicit in a representation that can be discussed and examined.

Kriz: influencing conditions differs from prescribing a result#

The selected translated chapter by Kriz proposes a person-centered systems account in which patterns arise through interaction. The environment supplies conditions but does not uniquely prescribe the resulting pattern. His family example asks how patterns once appropriate to one stage can become rigid when circumstances change. The therapist's role, in the argument, is to influence conditions under which

change becomes possible rather than dictate a new interaction in every detail. [WME-W022, translated chapter, pattern formation and family interaction]

IFS: protective parts and internal relationships#

The IFS Institute outline describes parts, a leading Self, protective patterns and contextual constraints. It proposes that parts can take on different roles and that extreme beliefs or emotions can be described as burdens. The stated goal involves changing relations within the model rather than simply suppressing a troublesome part. These are the model's own terms and assumptions. Its therapeutic use of burden is not the monograph's accounting of material or informational demands. [WME-W023, selected public outline]

The outline also lists external unsupportive conditions and practitioner mistakes, including proceeding before a system is ready or mistaking a part's activity for the Self. Its account consequently retains interpretation, timing and the practitioner within the process. This is the institute's public model description.

Bowen: apparent stability can leave a problem unresolved#

The Bowen Center's public theory presentation describes a triangle as a three-person emotional relationship in which tension can shift among relationships. It explicitly says that distributing tension can stabilize a system without resolving the underlying issue. The outsider position and changes in alliance can matter to the people involved even if the overall pattern continues. [SG-W028, Introduction to the Eight Concepts; Triangles]

Its emotional-cutoff account similarly distinguishes apparent improvement through reduced contact from resolution. The public presentation proposes that unresolved patterns can recur in later relationships. These are Bowen-theory descriptions, not universal facts established by the institutional page.

The useful relevant distinction is persistence of a pattern versus benefit to each participant or resolution of its causes. [SG-W028]

Haken: order parameters summarize and influence collective patterns#

Haken's 2017 conceptual article presents synergetics through control parameters, instability, collective order parameters and circular causality. The parts exchange matter, energy or information. Under appropriate conditions, a small number of collective variables can describe a pattern and constrain how the parts move. [RGC-W038, article]

The examples include coordinated movements, common rhythms and pattern recognition. A low-dimensional description is not automatically a low-energy implementation. It is a claim about relevant collective variables within a model or observed organization. Applying it elsewhere requires identifying those variables, checking disturbance responses and the relation between slow collective change and faster component dynamics.

The pattern-completion example makes retained preparation particularly visible. Stored or learned prototypes and network dynamics allow an incomplete input to develop toward a complete pattern. Storage, learning, sensing, transmission and physical implementation remain. Haken's use of a landscape to discuss intention is an interpretation, not a direct physical energy measurement. [RGC-W038, PDF pp. 7 –10]

His discussion of embodied aesthetics goes on to proposed relations among perception, action, beauty and therapeutic conditions. Group consciousness and mind–body identifications are philosophical or speculative extensions, not experimentally proved consequences of the network. His conclusion explicitly

warns that the apparent generality of order parameters can be seductive and requires care. [RGC-W038, PDF pp. 9–12]

Environments, skills and shared practices#

The accounts offer concrete ways to examine action organized by surroundings, skills, representations, social relations and cultural forms.

Recognition and the work of a continuing staff#

In the posthumously published Wirtschaft und Gesellschaft, Max Weber examines what happens as a relationship sustained by recognition of an exceptional leader has to continue in ordinary conditions. The administrative staff acquires interests in recruitment, economic opportunities and the regular conduct of its work. People initially joined through devotion may later occupy positions with more stable claims to resources or authority. Weber describes ways in which those claims and procedures can change the relationship through which authority is recognized. [T17-W001]

François Chazel distinguishes two layers of Weber’s analysis. In his reading, the question of succession is also a question of the basis on which a successor can be recognized. An expectation that authority passes within a lineage differs from attaching it to an office with its own recognized conditions. Chazel connects these variants to different aspects of everyday continuation and objectivation in Weber’s writings. The distinctions are part of Chazel’s textual reconstruction. [T17-W002]

Continuation and disputed succession#

Claudia Jetter compares Joseph Smith Jr. and Ralph Waldo Emerson through what each made of continuing revelation. In her account, Smith gave church-wide revelation a place in a hierarchy. Emerson treated access to revelation as a capacity individuals could exercise and rejected an exclusive institution as its center. Jetter uses the contrast to explain why their accounts of revelation supported different kinds of religious organization. [T17-W003]

Joseph F. Smith Jr.’s later polemic locates succession in the authority of the Twelve Apostles and their recognition by the church. He cites votes in August and October 1844 at which, in his account, the assembled members sustained the Twelve as the presiding quorum. He treats those votes as confirmation of the quorum’s right to preside and disputes the authority of those who later ordained Joseph Smith III. [T17-W005]

Community of Christ’s institutional history recounts the recognition of a different successor. It describes Joseph Smith III, the founder’s son, as coming to believe he had a divine calling, accepting leadership and being ordained president at Amboy, Illinois, on 6 April 1860. It also describes the Herald, first published in 1860, as a forum where leaders and members exchanged accounts of their missions and discussed the church’s affairs. The ordination and the periodical are two concrete acts in that institution’s account of its reorganization. [T17-W006]

Behaviors that observers rate#

Antonakis, Fenley and Liechti examine whether speakers and managers can learn behaviors judged charismatic. One study randomly assigned 34 managers and reassessed them after three months. In a second, 41 MBA participants gave a speech and delivered it again six weeks after instruction, with ratings by independent assessors. The authors report increases in ratings of charisma and discuss how those ratings relate to judgments of who looks like a leader or emerges as one. The outcomes are behavior and leadership impressions under specified measures. Continuing authority, in Weber’s account, concerns the relationships and arrangements through which people keep recognizing a claim. [T17-W004]

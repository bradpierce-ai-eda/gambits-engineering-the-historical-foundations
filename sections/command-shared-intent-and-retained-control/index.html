Acting together under uncertainty#

The relevant military literature asks how people can act together when no commander can know or decide every detail. Shared intent, training, delegated decisions, reporting, lateral relationships and intervention are different parts of the answer. The sources also ask when the organization itself should change.

Clausewitz: apparent simplicity and the resistances of execution#

The selected chapter on friction in On War describes why an organization that looks simple on paper becomes difficult to operate. It consists of people rather than one perfectly integrated mechanical part. Small delays, fatigue, uncertainty, weather and mistakes accumulate. An instruction can be intelligible while its execution still encounters circumstances absent from the diagram. [MIL-W036, book I, ch. VII]

The journey illustration makes the distinction accessible: expected transport can be unavailable, roads can be worse than anticipated and darkness can compound the delay. The point is the interaction of ordinary obstacles, not an unexplained mystical force. Clausewitz emphasizes experienced judgment and warns against expecting unattainable precision. Forceful will can also damage the organization while overcoming obstacles. [MIL-W036, book I, ch. VII]

Sunzi: disposition and collective potential#

The selected Art of War passage, traditionally attributed to Sunzi, directs attention toward disposition and

collective situational potential, associated with 勢, shi, rather than demanding the whole result from isolated individuals. Giles's 1910 translation and commentary use the image of logs and stones: shape, position and slope affect whether they remain still or move. The arrangement changes what effort can accomplish. [MIL-W027, ch. V, §§21–23 and commentary]

The received text and commentaries contain variant readings and different explanations of the passage. Across these interpretations, disposition and collective potential connect the configuration of circumstances with the effort required. [MIL-W027]

Implicit communication is prepared, not empty#

The Command and Control, MCDP 6, passage says that shared experience and a common outlook can permit implicit communication. People can interpret a brief indication through an established background rather than requiring every detail to be stated. The economy concerns explicit message traffic; interactive feedback remains necessary. It is a doctrinal explanation. [MIL-W001, selected printed 2-32 passage]

MCDP 6 was published in 1996 and amended by Change 1 in 2018. [MIL-W001]

The selected 2019 Army ADP 6-0 passages likewise tie delegation to competence, resources, shared understanding and trust. A commander can make fewer detailed decisions when subordinates understand the purpose and can act appropriately within it. Responsibility is redistributed; it is not enough to remove an approval step while leaving people without the capability or means to carry out the task. [MIL-W002, selected paras.1-26–1-41, 4-48–4-49]

Boyd: orientation grows through interaction#

John Boyd's Organic Design for Command and Control emphasizes implicit orientation and the relationships that make initiative coherent with a larger purpose. In the selected slides, orientation is shaped by heritage, culture, previous experience and unfolding circumstances; it also changes through interaction. It is a process of cross-reference, comparison and rejection, not merely a fixed instruction stored once. [MIL-W008, slides 7–23]

The proposed preparation exposes people with different abilities to varied situations in which they orient to one another as well as to events. Repeated shared experience can build similar expectations and trust. Boyd then argues that lower-level initiative can follow higher-level intent with less explicit communication. The source thus places substantial developmental work before the apparent ease of coordination. [MIL-W008, especially slides 13–18]

The warning against inward-looking formal arrangements is part of this argument. A procedure can become an obstacle if adherence cuts people off from changes in their environment. Yet the briefing's appeals to Gödel, Heisenberg and thermodynamics do not establish formal transfers of those results into organizations. Its organizational proposals are interpreted as a conceptual briefing, not a theorem derived from physics or logic. [MIL-W008, slides 20–23]

Logistics can require more than common intent#

Christopher Paparone and George Topic's 2014 article uses James Thompson's dependency distinctions to challenge a simple application of unity of command to the logistics enterprise. Pooled contributions can be coordinated partly by keeping activities from interfering. Sequential dependencies add requirements for order and transfer. Reciprocal dependencies mean that the output of one activity becomes another's input and vice versa. [MIL-W004, main argument]

The authors argue that reciprocal logistics relationships require continuing coordination across organizational boundaries. A common operational picture, lateral communication and trusted relationships support mutual adjustment. A single commander's statement of intent cannot specify every relevant interaction in a network shaped by industries, appropriations, transport, raw materials and political arrangements. This is a specific conceptual diagnosis of retained coordination work. [MIL-W004]

Paparone and Topic reconstruct Thompson’s 1967 Organizations in Action to explain these dependencies and challenge enterprise-level central regulation. [MIL-W005, through MIL-W004]

Van Creveld through later accounts, and Smith's criticism#

An MCDP 6 epigraph quotes Martin van Creveld's Command in War: a commander can increase information-processing capacity or redesign tasks and organization to reduce the information required. [MIL-W006, through MIL-W001, ch. 2 epigraph]

Christopher Smith's Network Centric Warfare, Command, and the Nature of War supplies a separate selected reconstruction of van Creveld's qualifications about technology and command. Smith criticizes narrow definitions of command and assertions of network advantage detached from context. More information or connectivity does not, in his argument, automatically resolve the human and organizational character of command. [MIL-W007, pp. 12–15, 64–68]

Power to the Edge: self-synchronization has conditions#

David Alberts and Richard Hayes's Power to the Edge proposes conditions under which participants can synchronize their activity without detailed central direction. The selected passage lists consistent

understanding of intent, good information and shared awareness, competence and trust. It explicitly retains command through congruent intent, authoritative allocation of resources and rules that guide action without dictating every detail. [MIL-W009, pp. 27–33]

The text also says that self-synchronization is not assumed appropriate when its conditions are absent. Its organizational proposal therefore differs from the claim that installing a network creates collective control. Responsibilities for information, architecture, preparation and resources persist, including in the selected later discussion. [MIL-W009, selected pp. 186–187, 208–210]

A passage in Alberts and Hayes's Understanding Command and Control distinguishes understanding from prediction. The SAS-085 overview develops the command-approach framework discussed below. [MIL-W010; MIL-W011]

SAS-085: changing the command arrangement is itself work#

The SAS-085 executive overview distinguishes decision rights, interaction patterns and information distribution as dimensions of a command approach. They can vary separately. Giving more people access to information does not, by itself, specify who may decide or which relationships carry coordination. The approach has to be described across those dimensions. [MIL-W011, selected pp. 9–15]

Agility includes recognizing when an arrangement no longer fits and being able to change it. That requires diagnosis, an alternative and a feasible transition, not simply permanent maximum decentralization. The group reports retrospective and simulation support. [MIL-W011]

The 2016 report on the 2014 Västmanland forest fire is a civil-emergency application of this programme. Its institutional abstract describes an initial mismatch in command arrangements and a case-analysis template. It demonstrates the scope of an application claim beyond combat. [MIL-W033, abstract and metadata]

Bousquet: a history of scientific images is not completed transformation#

In the abstract of Chaoplexic warfare, Bousquet relates military organization to scientific accounts of mechanism, thermodynamics, cybernetics, chaos and complexity. These provide the historical categories for his interpretation of changing images of order. [MIL-W018, abstract]

In Josefin Svensson's interview about The Scientific Way of Warfare, Bousquet describes scientific images of order and adaptation alongside a continuing tension between decentralizing ambitions and the hierarchical organization of state military institutions. [MIL-W019, through MIL-W020]

Stahel: doctrine, culture and historical practice do not coincide#

David Stahel's 2025 article examines the origins and application of Auftragstaktik and the problems of importing it into another institutional culture. A compact definition can hide differences in command level, available communications, personalities and organizational expectations. Stahel rejects a uniformly understood and practiced historical ideal. [MIL-W021, introduction and historical discussion]

Through Stephan Leistenschneider, he describes contested origins and early debates. Through Marco Sigg, he reports uneven practice across levels and periods. Better communications could permit close direction at lower levels, while higher-level autonomy could extend beyond the spirit of orders through rivalry and ambition. Initiative was not uniformly beneficial, and a culture emphasizing boldness had costs as well as capacities. [MIL-W021, reconstruction of historical studies]

Stahel also distinguishes the social and ethical purposes of later professional institutions from a purely functional reading of earlier military effectiveness. A historical technique cannot simply be imported as though its surrounding contract, authority and values were unchanged. [MIL-W021, conclusion]

Stahel reconstructs the mission-command histories developed in Sigg's Der Unterführer als Feldherr im Taschenformat and Leistenschneider's Auftragstaktik im preußisch-deutschen Heer. [MIL-W022; MIL-W023, through MIL-W021]

The supported historical qualification is more informative than a binary choice between always delegating and always directing. Stahel's account leaves an arrangement responsive to the operational circumstances and to the institution that can sustain it. [MIL-W021]

French, German and allied statements retain boundaries#

The selected French FT-05 passages distinguish command by detailed orders from command by objectives as complementary tendencies. Information collection does not remove uncertainty, and waiting for exhaustive information can itself obstruct decisions. Delegation can place execution where appropriate information can be acquired and used, while acute circumstances can lead to more centralized decisions. [MIL-W024, pp. 27–28]

Its subsidiarity passage retains systematic reporting and the commander's responsibility while warning against unnecessary intrusion into subordinate conduct. Local freedom exists within a wider relation of intent, reports and accountability. These are the source's doctrinal prescriptions. [MIL-W024, p. 38]

The selected German Agile Fehlerkultur passages similarly describe bounded independence under Führen mit Auftrag, with resources, training, responsibility and an acceptance of some errors. Nor does a modern discussion of Innere Führung establish that its social and legal assumptions are identical to every historical use of Auftragstaktik. [MIL-W025]

The selected 2022 allied land-doctrine passage makes trust earned and delegation conditional on capability, common training and professional understanding. Multinational cooperation can make that common background difficult. Sharing a doctrinal label cannot be assumed to supply the relationships and interpretation needed among organizations. The text is doctrinal prescription. [GL-W036, p. 43]

Command by veto: a prepared default with continuing oversight#

Alastair Cooper and Felicity Petrie's Mission Command at Sea and Command by Veto describes delegation within defined limits, with the delegating commander present physically or on a communication circuit. A trained delegate can proceed on a stated intention unless the commander modifies or vetoes it. Routine approval exchanges can therefore be reduced while the commander remains informed and able to intervene. [MIL-W034, definition and comparative argument]

The mechanism retains shared interpretation, competent delegates, explicit limits and attention. Authority may be delegated while accountability remains with the commander. The ability to act before a veto is useful only within the defined scope and appropriate timing. Silence in an unrelated, unobserved or misunderstood situation is not the same arrangement as the authors' command-by-veto construct. [MIL-W034]

The article connects changing communication and information capacities with changing command practice. Better information can support either more local action or a more active higher headquarters. It explicitly treats naval development on its own terms rather than reducing it to an identical land-doctrine history. [MIL-W034, conclusion and notes]

Duus: the bill for discretion is paid in preparation#

Anthony Duus's Paying the Mission Command Bill argues that trust rests on education, training, shared experience and execution. A hands-off posture without initial guidance, resources and appropriate supervision can leave a subordinate to discover the commander's intention only after expensive divergence. The relevant preparation is institutional as well as personal. [MIL-W035, selected introduction and trust/education sections]

Duus treats detailed and guided command as available within a spectrum, with circumstances and the relationship between people affecting the appropriate choice. His criticism is that terminology can hide the work needed to make discretion useful. This is a professional argument with historical illustrations. [MIL-W035]

Automation can remove interactions that were doing useful work#

The selected Re-Envisioning Command and Control passage proposes that repetition, communication in several forms, group ideation and reformulation can build understanding and trust. Efforts to automate apparently repetitive activity may remove some of the interactions through which that shared understanding develops. An activity can transmit content and also contribute to the relationship needed for later coordination. [MIL-W029, arXiv v2, introduction and framing]

The paper's human–AI organization is a research vision. [MIL-W029]

Civil incident organization and public accountability#

FEMA's 2017 National Incident Management System supplies a selected civil example of modular organization. Functions can be activated according to incident size and complexity; management responsibility is delegated, and unused elements can be demobilized. The arrangement changes the organization with the task rather than requiring every position to operate at every scale. [GL-W046, pp. 30, 90 (printed p. 81)]

Manageable span and modular expansion remain prescriptive principles. They depend on communications, experience and the relationships through which functions cooperate. [GL-W046]

Kevin Krupski's Who's the Boss? introduces another limit to importing an organizational model. He questions the wholesale transfer of a private-market principal–agent picture into civil–military relations, where public values and multiple principals matter. The selected discussion presents cultivated political and professional relationships as relevant to how accountability operates. [GL-W053, p. 28]

Krupski reconstructs Feaver’s monitoring argument while asking which purposes and constituencies an accountable command arrangement must serve. [GL-W053]

Common understanding, local decisions and accountability#

The selected sources offer substantive accounts of preparing common understanding, distributing decisions, preserving lateral coordination, monitoring defaults and adapting command relationships. They also expose the work of education, trust, resources, communication and accountability that a short instruction or a sparse organization chart can conceal.

A short expression can depend on a history of joint work. The following accounts examine how people establish a reference together, learn what another person knows, reuse local descriptions and remember an exchange. Their experiments also expose occasions when agreement, familiar wording and successful action come apart.

S41 treats language, learnability and shared repertoires; S42 develops context, interpretation and records. The sources here follow the interaction through which particular partners make an expression usable, and the consequences when participation, membership or memory changes.

Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs: a reference is completed together#

Herbert Clark and Deanna Wilkes-Gibbs's 1986 account treats referring as an activity that the speaker and listener complete together. A speaker presents a description or invites the other person to supply one. The listener can accept it, reject it or hold judgment while seeking more information. A problem can lead either person to repair the wording, add information or offer a different way of seeing the object. The resulting description is considered again. Their model follows this cycle through to mutual acceptance, including cases where an apparent acceptance is followed by another check. [CG-W025, pp. 7–9, 16–25, table 3]

The distinction among ways of changing a description matters. Adding that a person is standing up can supplement an earlier description of someone holding a book. Replacing an awkward description of an upside-down hat with a person pointing left proposes another perspective. In the recorded exchange, the original speaker subsequently uses the listener's replacement. The listener's contribution changes what both can say next. An acknowledgment can also accept the description so far while postponing a final decision about which object it identifies. [CG-W025, pp. 21–25]

Their experiment used eight pairs of students, with a screen preventing each person from seeing the other's twelve unfamiliar tangram figures. One person's ordering supplied the target; the other rearranged the cards through conversation. The same figures appeared in different orders across six trials. The experimenter identified mismatches after each trial, and the overall error rate was two percent. Transcripts recorded hesitations, interruptions, acknowledgments and repairs alongside the descriptions themselves. These features made it possible to examine how an exchange reached completion. [CG-W025, pp. 10–11, fig. 1]

The director's average word count per figure fell from about forty-one on the first trial to eight on the sixth. Speaking turns fell from 3.7 to about one. Requests for expansion and confirmation became less frequent, as did the speaker's repairs and follow-up checks. The authors explain the change through previously accepted perspectives: an elaborate description of a person skating can later support a compact reference to the ice skater. Their proposed economy concerns the work of both participants through the whole exchange. A brief initial formulation that requires extensive repair can demand more joint work than a somewhat longer one that is readily understood. [CG-W025, pp. 12–17, 21–28, figs. 2–3]

In their discussion, acceptance is judged against the purpose of the conversation. Directions intended to help someone reach a house require a different level of understanding from mentioning an address at a party. They also describe pressures that can make acceptance misleading: a listener may avoid requesting clarification to spare embarrassment, avoid offending the speaker or keep the exchange moving. The authors distinguish the participants' acceptance of an utterance from perfect comprehension, and allow an earlier acceptance to be reopened when a problem becomes apparent. [CG-W025, pp. 26, 33–35]

Wegner: remembering an item and remembering where to find it#

Daniel Wegner's 1987 chapter develops a transactive memory system from individual memories and the communication connecting them. His distinction among an item, its label and its location explains how people can retrieve information they have not retained personally. Someone may remember the label of a needed document and who has it. Another person can therefore serve as a location for knowledge. The resulting system includes differences in what its members know, together with knowledge of those differences. [CG-W028, pp. 186–191]

Retrieval can require a transaction that changes the initial cue. Wegner describes a requester asking a colleague for information; the colleague cannot immediately supply it but remembers having given it to the requester on a previous occasion. That reminder lets the requester locate the document. The information is recovered through their combined exchange. At encoding, participants can likewise negotiate what an item should be called, who should retain it and where it will be available. These arrangements distribute responsibility as well as content. [CG-W028, pp. 190–192]

Wegner reports a study involving twenty dating couples who attempted to remember sixty-four items from different knowledge domains. Participants judged their own and their partners' relative expertise. One partner studied an item set for a minute before passing it to the other for thirty seconds, making the difference in exposure apparent to both. Recall varied with personal expertise and this circumstantial responsibility. When a person had the longer exposure, recall also depended on whether that person believed the partner possessed relevant expertise. Wegner interprets this as an individual's memory responding to what the other member of the pair is expected to remember. An inaccurate understanding of the partner's expertise can leave both relying on storage that has not occurred. [CG-W028, pp. 192– 194, table 9.1]

Labels can also change the information being retained. In Wegner's illustrative account, calling an ambiguous sighting a UFO supplies an organizing interpretation for later discussion. Recollection can come to reflect that discussion rather than the original observations. Retrieval can drift from its initial question, while bringing together separately held details can produce an interpretation that neither person initially possessed. These possibilities are part of his account of how the transactions transform information. [CG-W028, pp. 195–197]

The arrangement can be lost. When a group dissolves, a remembered location may point to a person who is no longer available. Private expressions can remain intelligible to former participants but convey little to someone outside the relationship. Wegner describes this as a loss of access and organization even when individuals retain portions of the information. [CG-W028, pp. 198–199]

Schober and Clark: hearing an agreement and participating in it#

Michael Schober and Herbert Clark's 1989 study examined the difference between participating in such an agreement and hearing it develop. Ten pairs arranged twelve of sixteen unfamiliar figures across six trials. Forty other students later attempted the same arrangement while listening to recordings. Some heard the conversation from its beginning; others entered at the third trial. Half of each group could pause the recording. Listeners present from the beginning placed 88 percent of figures correctly, compared with 68 percent for those entering later. The original conversational partners nevertheless achieved 99 percent accuracy. Hearing every word had not supplied the same opportunity to establish what an expression meant. Pausing produced no statistically significant accuracy advantage. [CG-W009, pp. 214–220, figs. 1–3]

A second experiment addressed the possibility that recordings themselves caused the disadvantage. An overhearer now listened in the room, separated by barriers and presented as a coder so that the speakers

would not address that person. The principal analysis used ten triples after four exclusions, including two groups whose original matchers continued making unusual numbers of errors. Matchers achieved 98 percent accuracy and overhearers 85 percent. Including those two error-prone groups reduced matcher accuracy to 95 percent but preserved the difference. [CG-W009, pp. 222–223, fig. 4]

The difference was not simply a slower average response by overhearers. Their placements were spread much more widely around the points when the partners agreed to proceed. When overhearers placed a figure after that point, errors were more frequent. The authors' explanation concerns participation in grounding: a listener can request another description or propose a different perspective until understanding is adequate for the task. An overhearer must work with the perspective and stopping point chosen by the other two. Their experiment distinguishes access to the conversation from influence over how its references become established. [CG-W009, pp. 224–230, figs. 5–7]

Brennan and Clark: provisional agreements with particular partners#

Susan Brennan and Herbert Clark's 1996 experiments examine what people establish when they agree on a way of referring to something. Their conceptual pact is a temporary agreement about how to conceptualize a referent. One person can propose a description, the other can question it or offer a substitute, and both can subsequently use the expression they settle on. In one recorded exchange, a listener did not recognize a proposed shoe name and initially wondered whether it named a dog. An explanation established an acceptable shoe description. In another, the listener supplied the fish identification that both subsequently used. [CG-W007, pp. 1484, 1487]

The first experiment separated this history from the information needed to distinguish an object at the present moment. Forty-eight students, working in pairs, arranged picture cards without seeing their partners' cards. An initial set contained only one shoe; another introduced competing shoes, requiring a more specific description. When the original set returned, the specific expression could remain useful as an established precedent even though a simpler term would now distinguish the target. Directors retained the same content words on that return 69 percent of the time after four trials with competitors, compared with 35 percent after one. The number of previous uses mattered, beyond simply repeating the most recent formulation. [CG-W007, pp. 1484–1487, table 2]

The pact could also change. In the second experiment, partners alternated the speaking and matching roles, and either could use an established description. Expressions became simpler, and markers of uncertainty became less frequent as an agreement developed. A third experiment introduced a different listener after four matching trials. Across fifty participants in ten continuing-partner groups and ten partner-switch groups, directors retained the previous description on the first subsequent trial 48 percent of the time with their existing partner, compared with 18 percent with a new partner. Some initially offered an elaborated version of the old description; over further trials they accommodated to the new listener. The authors describe agreements that participants can revise, rather than names fixed once and retained indefinitely. [CG-W007, pp. 1488–1492, table 5]

Their appendix preserves an informative exception. Two partners repeatedly used different terms for the same dog when their roles changed. The authors describe them as understanding each other's terms while maintaining different perspectives. Agreement sufficient to perform the task could therefore coexist with a failure to converge on one expression. [CG-W007, pp. 1492–1493, appendix]

Mitsuda and colleagues: compare the layouts people believe they share#

Koh Mitsuda, Ryuichiro Higashinaka, Yuhei Oga and Tetsuya Kinebuchi's 2021 study records a shared task as the participants negotiate and act. Each person sees a private screen containing the same collection of shapes, initially arranged differently. The pair chooses what to make through text chat and

moves shapes toward a common layout. The shapes can be moved but cannot be rotated, resized or deleted. The task therefore lets the researchers compare the conversation with the participants' changing arrangements, including occasions when verbal agreement and the resulting objects diverge. [CG-W026, pp. 1697–1698, fig. 1]

One pair discusses making a face, then agrees on Pinocchio. When a participant discovers that a triangle cannot be rotated, they decide to make the face tilted instead. They continue discussing the positions of its parts and eventually express satisfaction. Their final layouts nevertheless differ. A name for the intended whole and repeated assent have supported the interaction while leaving a consequential difference unresolved. [CG-W026, pp. 1698–1699, table 2, fig. 2]

The report describes a corpus of 984 dialogues involving 287 participants. To examine final outcomes, the authors developed seven layout categories and had a separate annotator classify two hundred sessions. Exact matches accounted for nineteen percent. Another six percent matched after allowing a translation of the whole layout, which the task instructions had not required participants to eliminate. Twenty-eight percent were wholly mismatched. Other outcomes included swapped shapes, a reflected arrangement and a difference in the overall scale. These categories give the researchers a way to describe what the apparent agreement had preserved and what it had left unsettled. [CG-W026, pp. 1698–1699, tables 1, 3]

They also introduce a distance based on the relationships between pairs of shapes in the two layouts. Following its changes over the conversation distinguishes arrangements that converge early, those that converge later and those that fail to converge. Inspection of unsuccessful exchanges reveals different interpretations of an oblique orientation and failures to check locations. In this preliminary analysis, common ground is made observable through the specified layout task and its operations. The researchers can compare what participants say with what they actually construct. [CG-W026, p. 1700, fig. 3]

Naming a whole and describing its parts#

The human-dialogue analysis reported by 齋藤結, 光田航, 東中竜一郎 and 南泰浩 in 2023 examines how names enter the construction of a shared layout. Their annotation distinguishes a name for one shape, a name for several shapes forming the intended goal, and names for other groups of shapes. A separate classification describes what kind of thing the name invokes, including ordinary names already associated with the displayed shapes or building icons. This separates naming the intended whole from identifying a component. [CG-W027, pp. 1985–1986, §§2–3]

Their example begins with a proposal to arrange two circles like the wheels of a car. The other participant recognizes a truck. The conversation can then describe a quadrilateral as its passenger compartment, another part as its front and a particular orientation for those parts. The shared proposed object supplies relationships that the participants can use in subsequent instructions. Naming here participates in choosing and describing what to build. [CG-W027, p. 1985, fig. 1]

In the comparison of recorded dialogues, the first name for the overall goal appeared at a mean of 5.7 turns in successful dialogues and 8.1 turns in unsuccessful ones. The authors associate this earlier agreement with subsequent discussion of constituent parts. Their closer classification also preserves different routes. Many dialogues succeeded without naming an overall goal, using familiar names for the shapes instead. Conversely, repeated proposals for a goal in the middle of a dialogue could accompany participants talking past one another or losing track of which idea was under discussion. A goal agreed near the end could leave insufficient time for the remaining construction. The recorded examples and timing comparisons make the progression of the dialogue consequential alongside the availability of names. [CG-W027, pp. 1986–1987, §§5.1–5.2, figs. 4–5]

Nault and colleagues: remembered words, remembered meaning and familiarity#

Daniel Nault, Rohit Voleti, Matthew Nicastro and Kevin Munhall's 2023 experiments extend the referential task to delayed memory for what was said. Participants discussed images while arranging them to match a partner's screen. In a later individual test, they saw the same images and were asked to recall the most efficient descriptions used in the conversation. The images served as cues, and participants had not been told during the original interaction that their memory would later be tested. The study compares retained wording with an estimate of retained meaning. [CG-W011, Experiment 1, Procedure and Dependent Measures]

The first experiment analyzed twenty-four participants in twelve acquainted pairs; twenty-three completed the later recall task. They matched photographs of ordinary objects across two rounds, with three trials in each round and a change of roles between rounds. Delayed recall occurred seven to fourteen days later. A second experiment analyzed forty-eight participants, half in friendship pairs and half paired with a stranger, using unfamiliar faces whose visual similarity could be more systematically controlled. Recall in that experiment was supervised individually over a video call. Both experiments also included immediate recognition of previously seen images, a separate task on which performance was near ceiling. [CG-W011, Experiments 1–2, Methods and Recognition Memory]

The local common-ground measure was the director's normalized reduction in words between the first and third descriptions of an image. For later wording, the researchers measured word overlap with the final description, allowing specified spelling and word-root variants. For meaning, they used sentence-model estimates of semantic similarity between that final description and the typed recollection. These measures let them ask whether the efficiency developed during the interaction predicted two different aspects of later recall. [CG-W011, Experiment 1, Dependent Measures]

In both experiments, greater local shortening predicted more overlap with the original words at follow-up. It did not significantly predict the estimated semantic similarity. The authors also found that shorter final descriptions were easier to recall. They explicitly identify the need to separate that explanation from a contribution of common-ground formation itself. Their account therefore preserves an association with remembered wording while leaving the proposed relationship with richer remembered meaning unresolved. [CG-W011, Experiments 1–2, Recall Memory; Experiment 1, n. 1]

An additional control addressed a basic difficulty with image-cued recall. A picture can elicit similar words from people who never discussed it together. Twelve additional participants in each experiment described the images without the preceding conversation. The recalled descriptions from conversational participants overlapped their own original interaction more strongly than these independently generated descriptions did. The authors used that comparison as evidence for conversation-specific recollection, while recognizing that some raw overlap came from the images themselves. [CG-W011, Experiments 1–2, Control Experiment]

Friendship supplied a different pattern from local shortening. Friends described the faces with fewer words overall, but their descriptions did not shorten at a significantly different rate from those of strangers. They also showed no significant recall advantage in the principal mixed-model analyses. Secondary analyses that considered average description lengths favored strangers. The authors distinguish efficiency associated with prior familiarity from the change achieved during the task. They also argue that constrained picture descriptions may give a poor view of the richer semantic memories formed in ordinary conversation. Their results leave room for that further investigation while retaining the observed difference between concise communication and later recall. [CG-W011, Experiment 2, Description Lengths and Recall Memory; General Discussion]

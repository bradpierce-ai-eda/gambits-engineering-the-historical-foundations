Connecting experiments with changes in practice#

An experiment can identify an unexpected dependency without changing it. An organization can observe repeated failures without learning how to prevent them. The material selected in this section concerns the practices that connect intervention, observation, interpretation, and subsequent work.

The practitioner principles: make a claim about observable behavior#

The Principles of Chaos Engineering formulation defines a discipline of experimentation intended to build confidence in a system's ability to withstand turbulent production conditions. Its problem is not limited to a failed component. Interactions among functioning services can produce unexpected outcomes, while unusual events can expose weaknesses in fallback settings, retries, capacity, and dependencies. These are examples of the systemic uncertainty that the formulation proposes to investigate. [AFC-W004, definition and opening discussion]

The proposed experiment begins with a measurable output that indicates ordinary behavior, then states a hypothesis that this behavior will continue in a control and an experimental group. The experimental change represents an event such as a server crash or severed connection. Investigators try to disprove the hypothesis by looking for a difference in the measured behavior. The formulation's “steady state” therefore concerns a selected observational description over a period, rather than an internal state of each component. Throughput, error rates, and latency percentiles are among its suggested outputs. [AFC-W004, “Chaos in practice”; “Build a hypothesis around steady state behavior”]

The distinction between output and internal attribute is consequential. The experiment is directed toward whether the service continues to work under the chosen conditions. Merely confirming that a disturbance was introduced does not answer that question. Conversely, finding a weakness supplies a target for further improvement. In this formulation, discovery is followed by the opportunity to address the weakness; a disturbance by itself is not a repair. The underlying choice of measurable output remains part of what the experiment asks. [AFC-W004, “Chaos in practice”; advanced principles]

The advanced principles retain the surrounding engineering work. They recommend choosing realistic events by likely occurrence or potential impact, including non-failure changes such as traffic surges. They prefer production experiments because environment and traffic affect behavior. They also call for automation of orchestration and analysis, partly because repeated manual experiments consume labor. At the same time, they explicitly recognize possible short-term customer harm and assign responsibility for containing the experiment's effects. [AFC-W004, advanced principles]

Brier: connect stated probabilities with subsequent events#

Glenn Brier’s 1950 article begins with a practical objection to forecast verification: a scoring system can reward statements that serve the score better than the forecast’s users. A forecaster may learn to report something other than the probability they judge credible. Brier investigates a verification method for probability forecasts, asking how its incentives affect what the forecaster says. His categories must be mutually exclusive and exhaustive, with their assigned probabilities summing to one. The record therefore specifies both what outcomes count and how much confidence was assigned to them. [CG-W021, p. 1]

For each forecasting occasion, his score sums the squared differences between every category’s assigned probability and its observed indicator, one for the category that occurred and zero for the others. It then

averages those sums over the forecasting occasions. A perfect forecast scores zero; certainty in a category that does not occur scores two. His rain/no-rain example counts the errors for both categories. The article’s normalization consequently gives a range from zero to two. [CG-W021, pp. 1–2, equations 1–2]

The worked example links ten forecasts with the events that subsequently occurred. Their score is 0.19. A forecaster assigning the sample’s rain frequency, 0.3, on every occasion would score 0.42; using a climatological rain probability of 0.2 would score 0.44. The sample frequency is a benchmark calculated after observing the period, whereas a working forecaster must estimate such probabilities from available information. Brier uses the comparison to distinguish recognition of an average departure from climatology from discrimination among individual occasions. Both can contribute information. [CG-W021, p. 2, table 1 and equations 3–4]

Brier also groups a larger series of eighty-five forecasts by issued rain probability and compares those groups with observed rain frequencies. The relationship shows where the forecaster’s confidence needs adjustment. He says that this relationship alone does not establish usefulness: how often different probabilities are issued also matters. His closing account reports several months of experimental six-hour precipitation forecasts at the Weather Bureau. Some initial criticism concerned misunderstanding the method or disappointment at how little confidence the forecasts justified; he also leaves substantive questions about the scoring method for further investigation. [CG-W021, pp. 2–3, table 2 and conclusion]

Nosek and colleagues: preserve the plan and make changes inspectable#

Brian Nosek and colleagues’ 2018 account concerns the relationship between research questions, analysis choices and observed results. They distinguish predictions tested by observations from explanations generated after seeing them. Both activities contribute to inquiry. The difficulty arises when an explanation discovered in the data is presented as though the same data had tested an advance prediction. Their account joins reasoning biases with research incentives and the many decisions available when analyzing a dataset. [CG-W023, “Mental Constraints on Distinguishing Predictions and Postdictions”; “Standard Tools of Statistical Inference Assume Prediction”]

Their proposed preregistration mechanism deposits the questions, design and analysis plan in an independent registry before outcomes are observed. The registry preserves the record and makes it discoverable, sometimes after an embargo. A later report can then be compared with the plan, distinguishing planned tests, amendments and newly generated questions. The authors describe the case for preregistration as theoretically strong and supported by some correlational evidence, while explicitly saying that sufficient experimental evidence establishing superiority for reproducibility was not yet available. [CG-W023, “Preregistration Distinguishes Prediction and Postdiction”]

The practical examples retain the work required when research departs from its plan. A study may recruit fewer participants than intended or reveal violated statistical assumptions. Researchers can report amendments, their reasons and what outcomes had already been seen. Other possibilities include staged registration, blinded datasets, decision trees and standing procedures. These methods have conditions: an earlier stage may reveal the outcome intended for a later test, and a decision tree can formalize a biased stopping rule. Recording that rule makes it inspectable without improving its statistical properties. [CG-W023, challenges 1–2]

For existing datasets, the relevant separation concerns information access. Data may already exist while remaining unseen by the analyst. Conversely, earlier summaries or knowledge of correlated variables can influence a nominally new analysis. The authors therefore ask who has seen what and what has been communicated. They also describe splitting a dataset: exploration develops a prediction using one part, and registration precedes testing on a sealed part. Fast experimental workflows can use a related

sequence, treating initial experiments as exploration and then repeating a selected experiment with its design and analysis recorded in advance. [CG-W023, challenges 3–5 and 7]

The record must also extend beyond the successful attempt. In their example of a research program producing one positive result in twenty tries, registering every attempt does not prevent misleading selection if only the positive result is reported. Preserved, accessible registrations and outcomes permit examination of that wider history. The authors distinguish ordinary registration from Registered Reports, which add methodological review and in-principle publication acceptance before results are known. They also retain a further difficulty: selective narrative emphasis can overstate a subset of results even when the planned quantitative outputs are all reported. [CG-W023, challenges 6 and 9; “Advancing the Motive for Preregistration”]

Van den Akker and colleagues: compare expected benefits with published results#

Olmo van den Akker and colleagues tested several expected benefits in an article first published in 2023. They compared 193 published psychology studies earning preregistration prizes or badges with 193 related non-preregistered studies published in the same year. Registered Reports were excluded. Controls were selected from Web of Science’s related-publication lists, using shared references to match subject matter. This compared observed publication groups whose researchers had chosen their own practices. [CG-W024, pp. 5427–5428]

The comparison found almost the same proportion of positive results, 0.69 and 0.68. It did not support the expected reductions in effect sizes or gross statistical reporting inconsistencies. Preregistered studies more often reported power analyses, 55 percent against 23 percent, and had larger median samples, 216 against 116. The authors also disclosed a change to their planned positive-result analysis after recognizing an asymmetry in how hypotheses had been coded; they reported both versions, each giving an unsupported predicted difference. [CG-W024, p. 5430]

Assessing the results required choices about what counted as a hypothesis test. In preregistered studies, reported results were matched to recorded hypotheses. For controls, the researchers extracted reported statistical results while excluding identified exploratory tests and checks. They acknowledge that some remaining results might not have been intended as hypothesis tests. They also discuss self-selection into preregistration and the difficulty of making causal claims from the comparison. Their evidence supports particular differences in reported planning and sample size, while leaving the expected reductions in the selected bias indicators unsupported. [CG-W024, pp. 5429–5432]

Microsoft's guidance: test a complete flow against its targets#

The Microsoft guidance distinguishes three levels of activity. Fault injection introduces a fault into a component, dependency, or system path. Chaos testing investigates a particular resilience hypothesis under disruption. Chaos engineering incorporates such testing into culture, engineering practice, and the development cycle. These definitions place a technical intervention within an experiment and the experiment within continuing organizational work. [AFC-W005, definitions]

Its full-flow account explains why component recovery is insufficient as the sole criterion. A component may recover within its target while the dependent service still takes too long to become usable. Detection, response, and the recovery of other dependencies matter to the observed result. Similarly, responsibility for testing depends on what a team controls in infrastructure, platform, and software services. A managed service can move some work to a provider while leaving the workload's interactions, fallback behavior, throttling response, and boundary assumptions to be examined. The source calls attention to these differing responsibilities rather than treating a purchased service as the end of the reliability question. [AFC-W005, scope by service type; end-to-end targets]

The guidance also keeps preparation and revision explicit. It recommends scenarios based on critical flows and failure modes, measured baselines, experiments with clear hypotheses, containment, and the ability to stop and restore. It favors beginning in lower-risk environments before proceeding to production with safeguards. Results feed new tests, recovery-plan changes, and remediation work; architectural changes can make earlier assumptions obsolete. [AFC-W005, critical flows and failure scenarios; fault injection and chaos engineering]

Monperrus: improvement must occur in the software or its engineering process#

Martin Monperrus's Principles of Antifragile Software, in the 2017 paper, asks how an error can become more than something to survive. His account distinguishes static fault tolerance from an adaptive mechanism that learns when errors occur. Static tolerance can preserve a result without becoming more capable afterward. In his proposed use of “antifragility,” learning and improvement are the distinguishing conditions. [AFC-W007, §§3.1–3.2]

His distinction between state repair and behavioral repair adds detail. A state repair changes the current program state, such as a data structure. Behavioral repair changes the program's subsequent conduct through a patch. The latter can leave a change in the code after the triggering failure, which is why he treats it as particularly relevant to improvement through encountered faults. The paper refers to existing repair research as examples. [AFC-W007, §3.2]

Fault self-injection in production enters his argument through its effect on the engineering ecosystem. Repeated disruption exercises recovery behavior, draws engineering attention to it, and creates opportunities to learn from observations. He explicitly says that the injected fault alone is not what improves the system: design principles, the engineers' thinking, and other surrounding practices matter. He also requires a balance between the dependability lost through introduced failures and gains from software improvements. Measuring that tradeoff is a problem for the proposed engineering program. [AFC-W007, §3.3]

The process discussion supplies two specific mechanisms. First, a regression test that reproduces a discovered bug can remain in the test suite after repair. The accumulated suite can support later changes by making some regressions easier to detect. Monperrus relates testing, refactoring, and frequent deployment through this retained aid. [AFC-W007, §4.1]

Second, he discusses dependency on indispensable individuals. Moving people among projects can distribute knowledge and reduce the consequences of losing one person, while producing immediate learning costs, delays, and frustration. Reducing concentrated key-person dependency can require repeated smaller costs. [AFC-W007, §4.2]

The broader relation between process and product remains expressly hypothetical. Drawing on Conway's law, Monperrus proposes that processes embodying his error-oriented principles may be better at producing software with the corresponding properties. His mention of Ariel Tseitlin's The Antifragile Organization identifies a related organizational argument. [AFC-W007, §4.3; AFC-W019, through Monperrus]

Edmondson and Cannon: seeing failure is a different competency from analyzing it#

The HBS Working Knowledge excerpt by Amy Edmondson and Mark Cannon explains why awareness of a failure may leave its causes unchanged. An error count can show that something is wrong without showing what to correct. Their examples include discussion forums in medicine and military after-action reviews, but the operative point is the work of inquiry: people need to disclose relevant knowledge, hear

different accounts, and examine the event deeply enough to alter their understanding. [AFC-W015, opening analysis discussion]

The authors identify organizational and personal obstacles to that inquiry. Revisiting a failure can threaten confidence and invite defensive explanation. Reward systems can favor rapid, decisive action over patient investigation. Habitual attribution can explain away a large failure as outside interference or a small one as an irrelevant fluke. The result can be repeated experience without corresponding learning. Their reported telecommunications case illustrates such rationalization. [AFC-W015, barriers to analysis]

Their proposed supports are correspondingly substantial. They describe forums, resources, incentives, technical expertise, diverse perspectives, and skill in managing group inquiry. Such arrangements are meant to make interpretations contestable and the findings usable elsewhere in the organization. A reported hospital example extends analysis to near misses and small errors, with formal studies, clinical teams, and logs that preserve information for further examination. These details matter to gambits engineering because learning depends on organized work and retained records, rather than on failure's occurrence alone. [AFC-W015, organizational forums and selected hospital example]

The excerpt expressly distinguishes identifying failures, analyzing them, and deliberate experimentation as relatively independent competencies, rather than a required three-step sequence. Different competencies can be inhibited by different social and technical conditions. Their treatment supplies reasons to investigate how an organization notices, understands, and acts on evidence, without making failure a necessary or sufficient cause of improvement. [AFC-W015, concluding distinction among competencies]

The practice guide: experiment design and its supporting organization#

The China Academy of Information and Communications Technology's 2021 Chaos Engineering Practice

Guide (《混沌工程实践指南》) presents experimentation as a core activity supported by strategy, people, culture, risk management, and assessment. It also describes subsequent improvements to architecture and development or operations procedures as extensions of the practice. This organization of the guide makes the relationship between an experiment and its surrounding institution explicit. [AFC-W014, printed pp. 9, 23–28]

The design account begins with a hypothesis about a possible stability weakness, informed by user feedback, test records, and understanding of the architecture. Probability, scope of effect, and seriousness enter the proposed prioritization. The system needs to be performing a business scenario for the experiment's effects to be observed; an oversimplified scenario can leave important tasks unexercised. The guide describes production traffic branches or canary versions as possible ways of limiting exposure, and suggests replaying production variables or using representative simulated data when working outside production. These are alternative arrangements for the experimental situation, each retaining work to make the scenario relevant. [AFC-W014, printed pp. 9–10]

Measurement is designed alongside the intervention. The selected passage recommends observing extremes as well as averages, supporting tasks as well as final outputs, and designated stopping indicators. Its categories include time, throughput, failures, resource use, and business feedback such as user retries or error reports. The choice of metrics is to depend on the object, scenario, and monitoring facilities. This connects what can be concluded to what the experiment is equipped to observe. The guide does not treat the introduction of a disturbance as a sufficient account of the resulting service condition. [AFC-W014, printed pp. 10–11]

Cost limits the proposed coverage. The guide says that simulating every disturbance is impractical and therefore requires selection. It proposes relevance to the hypothesis, frequency, representativeness, and

significant impact as selection considerations. Fixed and random injection are suited to different situations in its account: repeated automation is more suitable for comparatively contained, short-lived disturbances with lower recovery cost, whereas wider, longer, or more costly cases may call for designed exercises at specified times. It further distinguishes single from combined disturbances, with combinations able to expose cascading behavior missed by individual factors. [AFC-W014, printed pp. 11– 13]

The support discussion keeps possible losses visible. Production experiments can cause failure; a system with inadequate observability makes defects harder to identify and locate; a weak initial stability basis can make repeated exercises disruptive and demoralizing. The guide discusses containment and lower-risk environments as possible responses. Its culture account calls for exposing rather than hiding defects and for habits developed through practice. [AFC-W014, printed pp. 23–24]

Return on investment is explicitly difficult to assess, partly because a prevented failure is less conspicuous than a realized incident. The guide proposes separate assessment of adoption, capabilities, and benefits, with the framework adapted to the organization's people, business, and architecture. Selected benefit indicators concern response efficiency, repeated defects, monitoring and detection, transparency, and experimental efficiency. Its architectural and procedural extensions ask whether discovered problems are actually resolved, including whether recurring lower-level errors warrant changes in development or operations discipline. The framework proposes what to examine when assessing benefits. [AFC-W014, printed pp. 24–28]

Owotogbe and colleagues: what a review can tell us about the practice#

Joshua Owotogbe and colleagues' v2 review manuscript, dated 19 June 2025, brings together 96 selected sources: 49 academic sources and 47 grey-literature sources. It reports on a chaos-centered body of material covering January 2016 through April 2024. [AFC-W011, §§3, 6, 8]

Its selection and analysis procedure helps define that scope. Searches covered four academic databases and grey literature, followed by eligibility screening and quality assessment. The eligibility requirements included English and accessible full text, while exclusions concerned short items and material outside the chosen chaos-engineering focus, among other conditions. The authors used thematic coding with review and reconciliation between the first two authors. [AFC-W011, §§3.2–3.5, 8.3]

The practice account distinguishes clear objectives, small initial experiments, automation, monitoring, stakeholder participation, and documentation. Its associated problems include misaligned experiments, inadequate planning, insufficient tools, weak collaboration, and neglected containment or restoration mechanisms. The tables record sources associated with those themes. [AFC-W011, §6.3]

Its evaluation discussion is also broader than a single technical metric. Numerical observations include response behavior, throughput, resource use, errors, availability, and recovery. Other forms of evaluation concern user experience, business consequences, monitoring gaps, and team communication or decision-making. [AFC-W011, §6.4]

The review's discussion retains substantial work around the experiment: environments, telemetry, configurations, specialized knowledge, repeated analysis, and coordination among teams. Its authors identify limited empirical research on how teams acquire the relevant skills, deal with cultural barriers, or manage resource constraints. They also acknowledge that the taxonomy has not received formal external validation and that alternative terminology may leave relevant material outside the corpus. [AFC-W011, §§8.2–8.3]

Two narrower engineering accounts#

The Volcengine developer-community account separates whether a fault was injected from whether a business metric changed. It describes cases in which an injection succeeds while service metrics remain normal because protective arrangements work, and proposes fault-specific verification indicators alongside service observations. Its workflow also separates execution, result analysis, and subsequent optimization. A further passage describes a Bayesian structural time-series model for estimating what the observed series might have been without injection; this modeled counterfactual is not a simultaneous observation of both alternatives. [AFC-W020]

A selected SRE passage from Huawei Cloud’s 2023 whitepaper uses 反脆弱 in connection with controlled fault-injection exercises intended to improve reliability and recovery. It places this within organization, culture, clear processes, and an operations platform. The selected responsibilities include planning, exercise design, observation, emergency restoration, review, accumulated scenarios, and revisions to procedures. [AFC-W021, §5.2.3.1, printed p. 35]

Verhulst: where the system boundary places improvement#

Eric Verhulst proposes assured reliability and resilience levels distinguished by how components meet a contract in the presence of faults. A component may itself be a system within a larger system. He distinguishes preserving service through redundancy and repair from a wider process that updates the system, and describes aviation through a boundary including operating conditions, stakeholders, feedback, and authorities. If a technical artifact changes only through surrounding maintenance and institutional activity, the source account concerns that larger arrangement. Verhulst's proposed levels are normative, and his industry-wide characterization remains his claim. [AFC-W018, pp. 844–849]

Caulkin's account of Seddon: some recurring work is created by earlier failure#

Simon Caulkin's journalistic account of John Seddon's “failure demand” concerns a different relationship between failure and subsequent activity. A customer calls again because something was not done, was done incorrectly, or did not resolve the original need. The repeat contact is work that the service's own earlier conduct helped create. Caulkin contrasts this with the demand the service exists to meet. The distinction concerns the history and purpose of a request, rather than treating every arrival as an independent unit of useful output. [SG-W020]

His selected insurance example describes agents constrained by detailed contracts, repeated transactions to resolve one case, and a redesign intended to let an agent complete the case with appropriate access to expertise. A second example contrasts apparently successful project and activity indicators with whether distressed customers actually return to normal. These reported cases make the argument more informative than a general call for efficiency: the proposed change concerns the organization of the work that generates later demand. Caulkin does not identify the organizations. [SG-W020]

The relevant proposed reduction in work depends on meeting the original purpose more successfully. Refusing access, leaving a problem unresolved, or reclassifying a legitimate need out of the count would not establish the mechanism Caulkin describes. His argument is about reducing preventable return work through service design. [SG-W020]

How experiments enter continuing practice#

The accounts in this section describe several different places where a consequence may be retained: a revised program, a regression test, an experimental record, shared expertise, an altered procedure, or a service arrangement that avoids repeated corrective requests.

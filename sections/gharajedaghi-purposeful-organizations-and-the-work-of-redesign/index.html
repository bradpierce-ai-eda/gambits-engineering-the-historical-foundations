A connected organizational account#

Jamshid Gharajedaghi's Systems Thinking: Managing Chaos and Complexity, third edition, connects holistic inquiry, operational dynamics, purposeful social activity and interactive design. His organizational synthesis examines how interdependence, decision criteria, modularity, competence, incentives and governance affect performance. [WME-W003, 2011, preface]

Purpose and choice change the description#

In 1984, Gharajedaghi and Russell Ackoff argued that treating a social system as a machine or an organism was an inadequate guide to decisions about it. [RGC-W002]

Gharajedaghi distinguishes passive mechanical systems, organisms treated as having a unified purpose, and social systems composed of purposeful participants. In the last category, participants can choose ends as well as means. A procedure can consequently fail even when it is technically feasible: people may reject its purpose, interpret it differently or pursue another interest. The relevant organizational problem includes choice, rather than only transmitting commands to interchangeable components. [WME-W003, ch. 1, 9–22; ch. 2, 29–37]

Control, influence and context are correspondingly different relations. An actor's ability or authority to determine something differs from an ability to affect it, and both differ from conditions that the actor must accommodate. The boundary is relative to the actors and their capabilities. Describing a supplier, regulator or customer as part of an environment does not make its choices disappear. [WME-W003, 29– 37]

The book also distinguishes information about what, knowledge of how and understanding of why. Shared instructions and shared reasons are therefore different accomplishments. A member who understands only an immediate instruction can still require repeated direction when circumstances change. The account of purposeful actors makes the development of relevant understanding an organizational activity in its own right. [WME-W003, ch. 2]

Gharajedaghi's criticism of biological and cybernetic models, including Beer, is part of his argument. His own later foundation diagram acknowledges Beer alongside Ackoff, Forrester and Boulding. [WME-W003, chs. 1, 4; diagram preceding ch. 5]

Inquiry joins structure, function, process and context#

The book rejects a simple one-to-one inference from structure to outcome. The same implement can perform different functions depending on its use and setting. Conversely, one function can be realized through different structures. Process explains how an arrangement produces an outcome, while context supplies enabling and limiting conditions. Gharajedaghi therefore proposes iterative inquiry among structure, function, process and context. [WME-W003, ch. 5, 89–93]

Iteration involves examining each perspective and revisiting its relations with the others. His expanding view places the focal activity within progressively wider surroundings until further enlargement ceases to add useful insight. The practical claim is that inquiry should expose assumptions and missing relations. Gharajedaghi proposes the four perspectives as mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive. [WME-W003, 92–93, fig. 5.4]

His five social dimensions—wealth, power, knowledge, beauty and values—likewise organize the inquiry. Generation and distribution, rational and emotional commitments, and present and future effects can conflict. The dimensions direct attention toward matters that a purely financial account can omit. [WME-W003, chs. 2, 5]

Multidimensionality seeks alternatives beyond a forced choice between two unsatisfactory extremes. An arrangement might improve both production and distribution, or permit local discretion together with broader consistency. That is a design ambition and a question to investigate. The book itself recognizes difficult differences in worldviews and the need to protect minorities. [WME-W003, 38–55, 103–108]

Shared criteria can support local decisions#

The contrast between two managers makes the proposed mechanism concrete. A manager can promise autonomy while retaining unspoken preferences; unexpected decisions then provoke intervention, and a subordinate starts seeking permission for everything. Another manager repeatedly explains why decisions are made. As the subordinate learns the relevant criteria, locally proposed action can become acceptable without detailed direction on every occasion. The illustration concerns shared reasons and judgments, not the announcement of decentralization alone. [WME-W003, 98–99]

Gharajedaghi proposes nested learning and design cells that connect a manager, board, superior and direct reports. Their task is to develop common understanding of purpose, cultural defaults and decision criteria. This institutionalizes the conversations rather than expecting every individual to acquire an identical understanding through private contact with the leader. [WME-W003, 99–100, fig. 5.8]

Effective criteria must leave a degree of freedom. If they prescribe every decision, they cease to support meaningful choice. They also need internal consistency, explicit assumptions and expected consequences, and a basis for collective commitment. A production cost target and a sales revenue target can create structural conflict if each rewards conduct that makes the other's task harder. The work of aligning the criteria precedes any claim that local decisions will fit together. [WME-W003, 100–101]

Consensus is described as an agreement to act, rather than majority rule or a requirement that every person privately agree. Leadership remains necessary when discussion does not resolve the issue. The leader can formulate an explicit default, or parties can agree on an experiment with performance criteria and conditions that would count against their positions. The process retains authority, design of tests and judgment about timing. [WME-W003, 101]

Culture and learning reproduce the arrangement#

Shared images and default assumptions help reproduce an organization even when individual members change. Improvement within an existing rule and learning that questions the rule are different activities. Gharajedaghi's second-order learning asks how the organization can examine the frames and criteria through which it identifies a problem. Training in an established process is therefore insufficient when that process itself produces the difficulty. [WME-W003, ch. 3, 57–68]

Information-mediated integration requires people who can interpret and use what is communicated. Knowledge can be shared without disappearing from its original holder. Gharajedaghi also draws physical analogies involving entropy, quantum phenomena and attractors; his organizational arguments can be distinguished from those extrapolations. [WME-W003, chs. 2–3]

Development is distinguished from growth. A larger organization need not be more able or willing to serve its members and environment. Differentiation can increase specialized capability while making integration more demanding. Gharajedaghi's concern is the relationship between these changes, rather

than an assumption that scale, output or specialization alone measures development. His sweeping historical classifications remain his interpretations. [WME-W003, ch. 4, 69–85]

Operational models make delays and incentives visible#

The stock-and-flow models describe accumulations, rates, feedback and delays. A policy can improve a local indicator while worsening the condition that later drives the same indicator. Modeling is intended to make those interactions available for discussion and testing. The book explicitly makes the usefulness of a model depend on the validity of its underlying equations and assumptions. [WME-W003, ch. 6, 109–131]

The telephone-company example joins several causes. Demand had consumed spare lines previously retained for failures. A repair could then appropriate another active line, briefly satisfying one customer while generating another complaint. Different departments controlled interdependent activities, records deteriorated as more people worked on the plant, and volume-based rewards encouraged activity and overtime. The problem was not described as a shortage of worker effort alone. [WME-W003, 128–129, fig. 6.24]

The reported redesign groups related work in neighborhood units, changes rewards toward results and customer satisfaction, gives managers authority to limit new installations when plant condition and slack require it, and directs expansion toward the limiting parts of the system. Capacity, maintenance, records, local responsibility and incentives change together. The author reports resolution within three months. [WME-W003, 128–129]

The account is particularly informative about spare capacity. Eliminating an apparently idle reserve can increase later interdependence and repair traffic. Its value depends on the disturbances and demand it supports. The book also proposes preserving assumptions and expectations in a read-only log so later experience can be compared with what was expected. [WME-W003, 128–131]

Modularity groups dependencies and maintains competence#

Gharajedaghi defines a module through strong internal interdependence and relatively weak external dependence. A platform supplies a predesigned relation through which a class of modules interacts with the rest of the arrangement. The aim is to integrate related activities inside a unit while making its external interactions simpler and more explicit. [WME-W003, ch. 7, 138–140]

The proposed multidimensional architecture distinguishes inputs, markets and outputs. Input modules hold resources and competencies, including knowledge and shared services, and must renew them through research and education. Market modules identify needs and opportunities. Output modules combine external opportunities with internal capabilities in products, projects or programmes of differing duration. Their authority over work and money need not include ownership of all the capital they use. [WME-W003, 139–140, fig. 7.4]

Capability maintenance is therefore deliberately separated from the life of a particular project. The book does not propose that every temporary team independently own every specialist resource. Members of a knowledge bank also need design competence in addition to functional expertise. A simpler interface for an output group can depend on continuing work elsewhere in the architecture. [WME-W003, 139–140]

Cross-functional water-system design#

The custom water-purification design example contrasts sequential passage through functional hierarchies with a cross-functional module. In the original reported process, chemical, process, electrical and mechanical engineering each passed work up and down their own chains before transferring it

onward. Costing and customer negotiation followed. The author estimates six to eight months and roughly $500,000 for the prior process. [WME-W003, 140–141]

The proposed experiment formed a learning cell of four junior engineers, a cost accountant and a sales engineer. Each taught the others the essential concerns and variables of the work. This preparation took three months, on the members' own time, with a promised $10,000 salary increase for each participant if the shared learning and project were successful. The team then met the customer together, rather than relying on a distant serial interpretation of requirements. [WME-W003, 141]

The book reports a completed design in six weeks at $120,000 and says the experiment was repeated twice with similar results. Shared understanding and concurrent contact with the customer replaced repeated hierarchical handoffs. [WME-W003, 141]

Design and realization are separate tasks#

Idealized design is constrained by technical feasibility and operational viability, while being capable of learning and adaptation. It changes the available alternatives instead of only optimizing the inherited arrangement. [WME-W003, 133–150]

Gharajedaghi describes realization as potentially the most resource-intensive phase. It involves preparation, monitoring of constraints, trust and ownership of the change, following the inquiry and design process. [WME-W003, 144–150]

Measurement and reward are part of the arrangement being designed. Local financial centers can pull against one another, while incompatible present incentives can burden future members. The proposed social calculus incorporates chosen coefficients for need, scarcity and strategic importance. Those choices require continuing revision. They are normative and managerial decisions, not discovered physical constants. [WME-W003, 150–157]

Service, authority and internal markets#

The multidimensional design combines horizontal customer–supplier relations with vertical authority. Hiring, promotion and formal accountability do not disappear because units also purchase services. The internal market is intended to make demand and service performance visible, while the authority relation addresses matters it cannot settle. Gharajedaghi presents the two relations as complementary. [WME-W003, ch. 9, 181–207, especially 198]

Centralization requires a specific justification: indivisible functions, common measurement and information arrangements, scale effects or development of a core technology. The benefits must outweigh the disadvantages for the whole. Shared-service provision should not be confused with control, because mixing them can compromise both useful service and legitimate oversight. The control work remains assigned elsewhere. Some redundancy is also deliberately retained for flexibility. [WME-W003, 191, 195]

In his 1984 article on multidimensional modular design, Gharajedaghi argues that narrow specialization leaves an organization with too few ways to respond when conditions change. His proposal builds some redundancy into a modular design so that units can adapt and redefine their functions. [RGC-W003]

Inside–outside purchasing choices are intended to discipline internal providers, but a higher authority can override a choice by accepting the corresponding cost or loss. The account therefore retains governance even where price-like relations organize demand. Its broad assertions that an internal market eliminates bureaucratization are proposals for expected behavior, not universal effects established by the framework's definition. [WME-W003, 198]

The COM/Energy design supplies a particularly concrete cost distinction. Buying an external service does not make corporate fixed costs vanish if the organization still incurs them. The design requires the purchasing unit to bear a proportional internal charge until those costs can actually be eliminated. The comparison is between outside supply plus retained costs and the relevant internal alternative, rather than an allocated-cost figure that moves when the accounting changes. [WME-W003, ch. 13, 309–310]

Measurement itself also costs something. Gharajedaghi says its cost should not exceed the information's value and warns that overhead allocations can be mistaken for causal costs, inducing harmful cuts. The value-added coefficients of his performance centers reflect chosen strategy. These passages specify both a practical accounting issue and the limits of treating a managerial measure as a neutral description. [WME-W003, 202–206]

The cases have different evidential status#

“Mess formulation” gathers interacting difficulties into an intelligible pattern. The proposed process searches, maps and tells a story to support recognition and action. It is presented as early warning rather than prediction. The utility named XYZ is expressly fictitious, and its exaggerated scenario is a design aid. [WME-W003, ch. 8, 159–179]

The Part IV cases are selected consultant/client design synopses. Oneida is described as an ongoing fifth iteration; the Butterworth account condenses a much longer design after six iterations, acknowledging losses or misrepresentations in compression. Their detail is valuable for understanding intended arrangements. It does not convert all the proposed activities into implemented and sustained outcomes. [WME-W003, introductions to chs. 10–11, 211, 245]

Oneida's design retains dedicated expertise, boards and membership cells, together with support for meetings, material preparation, minutes and facilitation. Consensus is explicitly time-consuming. Self-reliance differs from doing everything internally: outside contractors can participate while training local counterparts, and outsourcing should avoid excessive vulnerability. Multiple employment possibilities may be desirable redundancy. The account shows how participation and local capability require supporting organization. [WME-W003, ch. 10, 211–243]

The Butterworth health-care design combines community modules with shared specialist, capital-intensive capabilities. It recognizes risk, quality and rights, and the limits of reversible experimentation. Core knowledge is maintained beyond temporary projects. The professional as learner, designer and practitioner is an integrative role that can broaden the competence required of a person. These are features of a proposed organizational design. [WME-W003, ch. 11, 245–279]

The book explicitly associates integration there with cooperation, attention from the president and cultural transformation. Flexible learning can sometimes be less immediately efficient than a perfected routine. A decision to preserve that learning has a purpose different from minimizing all overhead. [WME-W003, 273–279]

The Marriott design combines regional autonomy with shared components, core knowledge, training and maintenance. Initial subsidies may be needed. Policy teams establish criteria; where policy is absent, a local decision can become material for later learning. This is an account of how discretion and common capability are intended to fit together. [WME-W003, ch. 12, 281–289]

COM/Energy distinguishes a regulated core from entrepreneurial platforms and retains technical expertise, alliances, planning and learning networks. Its expectation of automatic or self-administered coordination is conditional on those continuing networks. Confidential supporting reports are not reproduced in the book. [WME-W003, ch. 13, 291–315]

Carrier is even more clearly bounded. The chapter says that implementation was not attempted because corporate circumstances and strategy changed. The architecture is a tentative idealized design produced by a team, not a realized success case. Its content is still informative: purchased components leave a requirement for whole-system design capability, and make, buy and alliance choices each retain relevant knowledge in different relations of control or influence. [WME-W003, ch. 14, 317–332]

The Carrier proposal further includes cross-disciplinary training, technical partnerships, models, shared design capability and policy teams. Revenue shares are proposed strategic allocations informed by benchmarks, alternatives, history and negotiation. [WME-W003, 321, 328–332, fig. 14.5]

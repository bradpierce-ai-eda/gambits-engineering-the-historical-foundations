S86 Forecasts, commitments and governing uncertain projects #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

Authorizing a project involves more than estimating its tasks. Forecasts shape the commitment, people have reasons to present particular forecasts, and governing bodies decide whether the work still serves its purpose. Exploratory projects add another difficulty: the investigation can change what the project is supposed to accomplish.

Forecasting from completed projects#

Flyvbjerg's reference-class forecasting turns attention from the internal story of a proposed project to outcomes of comparable completed projects. The procedure identifies a reference class, establishes the distribution of its outcomes and locates the proposed project in relation to that distribution. The comparison must be appropriate to the kind of project and to the decision stage: an early estimate and an estimate made after detailed design answer different forecasting questions. [MG-W004]

The outcome distribution permits a decision about tolerated overrun probability. A budget based near the middle of a distribution accepts a different chance of overrun from one based at a higher percentile. Flyvbjerg illustrates the connection with a £100 million road estimate and a 32% uplift, producing £132 million. The uplift belongs to a specified reference class and risk tolerance, rather than being a general contingency percentage to attach to every undertaking. [MG-W004]

His Edinburgh Tram Line 2 account concerns an independent review before construction. It shows how an outside-view assessment can enter an authorization process. Its historical role is the review of an estimate at that stage, not a retrospective demonstration of the line's eventual cost. Independence matters because the people assembling a project's internal case may also be committed to getting it approved. [MG-W004]

Flyvbjerg distinguishes optimism bias from strategic misrepresentation. Better information can correct a sincere but unrealistic estimate; it has a different relationship to a forecast deliberately made attractive to secure approval. His proposals therefore concern incentives, scrutiny and accountability as well as the statistical procedure. The distinction explains why installing a forecasting technique and making an institution want an accurate forecast are separate interventions. [MG-W004]

The Heilmeier Catechism in research funding#

George H. Heilmeier recalled that he read and commented on each DARPA order before deciding whether to sign it. The director’s signature was the last step before funding. He described how staff critiqued one another’s proposals and agreed to use a common set of questions. Asked how many projects survived that scrutiny, Heilmeier said the more telling question was how many had changed during the discussions. [HCT-W001, pp. 180–181]

DARPA describes using the “catechism” to assess proposed research programs. It publishes the following eight questions. [HCT-W002]

1. What are you trying to do? Articulate your objectives using absolutely no jargon.

2. How is it done today, and what are the limits of current practice?

3. What is new in your approach and why do you think it will be successful?

4. Who cares? If you are successful, what difference will it make?

5. What are the risks?

6. How much will it cost?

7. How long will it take?

8. What are the mid-term and final “exams” to check for success?

Governing the undertaking beyond its initial approval#

Too and Weaver place project management within a governance framework linking project outputs to organizational strategy. The governing board supplies direction and remains accountable for governing the organization. Project managers exercise delegated authority to carry out work. Delegating a task does not make the governing responsibility disappear, while retaining accountability does not require the board to manage each operational decision. [MG-W005]

Their framework connects four elements: portfolio management, sponsorship, a project management office and project/program support. Portfolio management selects undertakings that support strategy and reconsiders those that cease to do so. Sponsorship maintains the connection between an undertaking and the organization that expects its benefits. The other elements supply oversight information and support for delivery. The authors present this as a conceptual construction drawn from management and governance literature. [MG-W005]

Continuing review changes the meaning of authorization. A project approved under one strategy or expectation need not retain its place indefinitely. Governing information must make it possible to consider the undertaking's continuing contribution, rather than merely whether its original schedule is being followed. Too and Weaver's account thus treats delivering the specified output and selecting the right continuing commitments as connected but different responsibilities. [MG-W005]

Organizing exploration before the project is fully specified#

Lenfle examines innovation projects for which a customer, goal or decomposition of work is not yet settled. The uncertainty extends beyond the time needed to carry out known activities. Investigation can alter the potential uses, technical questions and criteria by which an opportunity should be judged. A detailed development plan presupposes answers that this kind of project is trying to obtain. [MG-W006]

He gives the undertaking a provisional research program. It organizes related technical and use questions without pretending that the final product is already known. Tests supply occasions for learning and coordination: a deadline can require a team to produce knowledge rather than a completed feature. Exploring uses and technical possibilities together also avoids a sequence in which a technical answer arrives after the question about its value has changed. [MG-W006]

Legitimacy and evaluation are part of the construction. Without a recognized undertaking and criteria appropriate to exploration, investigations can appear to be disconnected studies competing for resources. The program gives them a common provisional purpose while allowing that purpose to be revised. The Usinor history developed in S62 supplies a related account of exploratory learning. Here Lenfle's contribution is the problem of organizing and evaluating such work when the usual specification-led project representation is premature. [MG-W006]

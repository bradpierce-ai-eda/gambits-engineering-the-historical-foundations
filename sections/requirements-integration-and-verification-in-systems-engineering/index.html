Requirements as an activity#

Jeanneret Medina, Baudet and Bonnal look back on the development of requirements engineering as an area of work. In their account, the early 1990s conferences mark the emergence of an identifiable research current. They point to Pohl’s distinction among specification, representation and agreement, and contrast that account with standards that enumerate analysis, verification, validation, communication, documentation and management. [T14-W001]

NASA’s systems engineering handbook describes technical requirements definition as an iterative process. Baselined stakeholder expectations and a concept of operations supply inputs. Requirements are elaborated from the product to lower levels and address relationships with other products, operators and maintainers. The handbook’s example of a specified force with a tolerance makes a condition checkable. The specification gives the test a criterion. The observed result comes from carrying out the test. [T14-W002]

A specified unit at an interface#

The 1999 Mars Climate Orbiter investigation traces a numerical mismatch through an interface. The specification required impulse in newton-seconds. A ground software application wrote values in pound-force seconds, and the downstream navigation process treated them as the required metric values. The unit conversion is about 4.45. The report identifies the application, exchanged file and consumer, and records responsibilities on the contractor and JPL sides as they existed in the project. The case shows the practical distance between establishing an interface requirement and producing effective evidence that exchanged values meet it. [T14-W003]

Change and acceptance evidence#

Ågren and colleagues’ interviews at two automotive manufacturers address the visibility of requirements change. A current baseline tells a reader what the requirements now say. The history of changes shows which requirements moved and gives people examining the consequences a way to trace the change. The study’s proposed improvements come from interviews and a second round of inquiry. [T14-W004]

The transport prototype acceptance report gives an example of evidence at a specified configuration. A script describes the expected result, and its steps are repeated at least 15 times. The test team observes displays or physical events and keeps logs for inspection after the run. Step pass rates concern those scripts and the configuration they exercised. The report distinguishes these acceptance checks from the later field evaluation. [T14-W005]

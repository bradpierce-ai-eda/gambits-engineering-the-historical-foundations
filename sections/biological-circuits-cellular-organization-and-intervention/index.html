From components to cellular behavior#

Biological circuits provide concrete cases of organization producing a capability: a temporary signal can leave a persistent expression state, a regulatory loop can generate repeated pulses, and the allocation of cellular machinery can change what an introduced construct achieves. Systems biology examines such behavior through molecular interactions, measurements and models. Synthetic biology also constructs circuits to investigate and use it. These traditions contain pertinent historical foundations for asking how a capability is produced, what sustains it, and what an intervention changes.

The accounts below move from experimentally investigated circuits to metabolic constraints, whole-cell modeling and selected recent computational approaches. Their objects include living cells, mathematical representations of cells, and the conduct of biological research. The experiments show particular realized behaviors; the models and reviews explain the questions, assumptions and methods through which those behaviors can be investigated.

Kitano on structure, dynamics, control and design#

Hiroaki Kitano's 2002 overview identifies four aspects of system-level understanding: structure, dynamics, control and design. A list of components supplies a different kind of knowledge from a network of interactions, and an interaction diagram supplies a different kind from the behavior of that network over time. His road-map analogy makes the distinction tangible: the map helps locate possible routes, while explaining traffic requires information about movement and interaction. Control asks how a state can be changed or stabilized; design asks how systems with desired properties can be constructed. [BC-W001, p. 1662]

The research cycle he describes makes a model's assumptions available for testing. Competing explanations can guide the choice of an experiment that distinguishes them, and disagreement with observation can require revision of the model. The needed measurements depend on that purpose: broad coverage, adequate temporal sampling and simultaneous observation of different molecular properties can matter differently. Kitano's account thus joins modeling to the organization of experimental work. He presents expanded control and design capabilities as ambitions supported by continuing advances in measurement, analysis and software. [BC-W001, pp. 1663–1664]

Alon on response time and persistence detection#

Uri Alon's 2007 review explains selected regulatory patterns through their dynamics and experimental investigation. Negative autoregulation occurs when a transcription factor represses production of itself. A strong promoter permits rapid initial accumulation; as the repressor approaches its threshold, it reduces further production. Relative to simple regulation reaching the same final concentration, the circuit can respond faster. The steady concentration and the route taken to reach it are separate properties. Alon also discusses reduced variation between cells and the possibility of noise amplification when feedback contains a long delay. [BC-W002, pp. 450–451]

A coherent feedforward circuit gives a different example. X activates Y, and both activate Z. When activation of Z requires both X and Y (an AND rule), it waits for Y to accumulate sufficiently. A brief input can disappear before that happens, whereas a persistent input permits expression. Turning X off can stop new Z production promptly even though Y remains. Alon describes experimental tests in the arabinose system and contrasts OR-like regulation in flagella, where either input can suffice and the delayed

transition changes. Timing therefore depends on the regulatory signs, the input rule and molecular kinetics as well as the wiring. [BC-W002, pp. 452–453]

Gardner, Cantor and Collins construct cellular memory#

Gardner, Cantor and Collins constructed a toggle from two genes whose protein products repress one another's promoters. When one side dominates, it suppresses production of its opponent and thereby sustains its own predominance. A temporary inducer weakens the active repressor, allowing the opposing side to accumulate and establish the other state. After the inducer is removed, mutual inhibition can retain the selected expression state. Their experiment thus separates the signal used to change state from the continuing gene expression that maintains it. [BC-W003, pp. 339–340]

The two arrows are only part of the design. Their model requires sufficiently cooperative repression and appropriately balanced effective synthesis rates. Six constructs shared the same basic configuration, but one had only one stable expression state. In one construct, the two culture states persisted for twenty-two further hours without inducer, under the reported growth conditions. These particulars explain what has to be arranged for a temporary input to leave a persistent cellular response. [BC-W003, box 1 and figs. 2– 4]

Elowitz and Leibler construct an oscillator#

Elowitz and Leibler's 2000 repressilator connects three transcriptional repressors in a cycle: LacI represses tetR, TetR represses cI, and CI represses lacI. Their design model permitted either convergence to a steady state or sustained oscillation, depending on expression, repression and decay parameters. To favor oscillation, the construction used tightly repressible promoters and degradation tags that shortened repressor lifetimes. Cellular protein destruction participates in producing the temporal behavior. [BC-W004, pp. 335–337]

A separate fluorescent reporter allowed the researchers to follow individual cell lineages. They observed oscillations lasting longer than a cell-division cycle and substantial variation in amplitude and period, including skipped or delayed cycles. At least forty percent of cells met their oscillation criterion in each of three movies. A population average would conceal important aspects of this behavior as cells lost synchrony. The experiment supplied a working synthetic oscillator whose irregularity was itself a subject for further investigation. [BC-W004, pp. 335–338]

Adaptation and a programmable sensing interface#

The chemotaxis account in S05 supplies another relation between organization and response. Yi and colleagues reconstruct exact adaptation through integral feedback: an internal state associated approximately with total receptor methylation changes with the difference between a methylation contribution and activity-dependent demethylation. At a stable steady state, its rate of change is zero. Under the specified biochemical assumptions, activity returns to a value independent of the changed ligand level. The continuing receptor-modification reactions support that response. [W012]

S05 explains the four biochemical assumptions and the stability requirement in detail. This is a response that returns an activity measure after a changed input, whereas the toggle experiment selects and retains an alternative expression state. The distinction gives a reader two concrete behaviors to investigate without using persistence as a single undifferentiated outcome. Their mathematical descriptions identify different states and different conditions for the reported result. [W012; BC-W003]

The selected synthetic Notch account in S40 describes replaced extracellular sensing and intracellular transcriptional modules. Ligand binding can release a regulator and connect a chosen external cue to a

chosen transcriptional response. The sensing interface is engineered while receptor processing and cellular execution remain involved. S40 develops the sensing interface and combined-cue response programmes. This example places an intervention at the connection between a signal and an existing cellular apparatus. [GL-W060]

Ceroni and colleagues measure the host's remaining capacity#

Ceroni, Algar, Stan and Ellis examine the burden imposed by introduced gene-expression constructs. Their capacity monitor uses a gene integrated into the bacterial chromosome that produces a fluorescent protein without an inducing signal. Its production rate provides an indirect assay of expression capacity remaining as another construct draws on cellular resources. Construct output, the monitor's output and cellular growth can then be compared. An introduced function enters a cell that already has demands on its expression machinery. [BC-W006, supplementary figs. 1–3]

Their translational resource model represents ribosomes moving along transcripts and returning to a shared pool. Stronger recruitment and slower elongation can change how much machinery is occupied. In the selected supplementary analysis, changing coding-sequence length or ribosome-binding strength changes both predicted output and remaining capacity. Tests with shorter antibody-fragment constructs captured important trends, while the model overestimated burden in some conditions. The comparison makes the object of improvement explicit: obtaining a desired product while retaining more host capacity. Its diagnostic is a measured reporter response supported by a specified model, rather than a direct inventory of every resource in the cell. [BC-W006, supplementary note 4 and figs. 9, 13–15]

Orth, Thiele and Palsson on feasible metabolic flux#

Orth, Thiele and Palsson's 2010 primer explains flux balance analysis through a stoichiometric matrix S and a vector of reaction rates v. Each matrix column records what a reaction consumes and produces. At steady state, Sv = 0 balances the represented metabolites. Upper and lower reaction bounds restrict the allowable rates, including exchange with the environment. These conditions define a feasible set that can contain many flux distributions without requiring a kinetic rate constant for every reaction. [BC-W005, box 1 and figs. 1–2]

Selecting a growth or production objective adds another commitment. Linear programming chooses a feasible distribution maximizing or minimizing a specified linear combination of fluxes. Biomass production can be represented by a reaction consuming measured proportions of precursor materials. The primer illustrates environmental intervention by changing oxygen-uptake bounds, and discusses alternative optima when several pathways attain the same objective. Standard FBA describes steady-state fluxes; it does not determine metabolite concentrations or automatically include regulatory dynamics. Feasibility, the selected objective and the additional biology needed for prediction remain distinct elements of the method. [BC-W005, From constraints to optimizing a phenotype; The many uses of flux balance analysis]

Karr and colleagues integrate a whole-cell model#

Karr and colleagues' 2012 model of Mycoplasma genitalium divided cellular function among twenty-eight process models. Different mathematics served different processes, including flux balance for metabolism and stochastic descriptions for degradation. Integration used shared cellular variables and an approximation of process independence over short intervals. Submodels advanced using values determined at the preceding step. The model's architecture therefore included an explicit computational means of reconnecting processes initially developed separately. [BC-W007, pp. 389–391, fig. 1]

The study also used discrepancies to direct experiments. In its examination of twelve gene-disruption strains, two-thirds of predicted growth rates were consistent with the measurements. The lpdA disruption was particularly informative: a colorimetric assay initially failed to detect growth, while an alternative doubling-time measurement indicated viability. The authors interpreted the combined evidence as severe but noncritical impairment of growth. The authors investigated such disagreements to refine parameters and functional assignments. Their whole-cell construction supplies a historical example of connecting heterogeneous representations to experimental correction. [BC-W007, Model-Driven Biological Discovery]

Maintaining processes and realizing their relations#

The cellular-resource account has a useful neighboring question in the Rosen material in S26: how are the processes on which operation depends themselves produced and repaired? Louie's reconstruction distinguishes a transformation's output from production of the machinery performing that transformation. Siekmann examines the proposed closure of these productive relations and disputes what can be inferred about their biological realization from the abstract maps. The disagreement concerns an identified step between a formal construction and the organized material process it is intended to describe. [W104; W103]

S40 develops other source-specific accounts of maintained organization. Chemical organization uses closure and feasible-flux conditions for reaction sets. Hopfield's kinetic proofreading adds a driven intermediate stage that gives an incorrectly bound substrate another opportunity to dissociate. The spatial-proofreading account instead examines gradients and movement, including the work needed to maintain the spatial difference. These accounts locate particular requirements inside their mechanisms: productive closure, sustained chemical driving, or a maintained gradient. Their full expositions and qualifications remain in S40. [W077; TA-W017; TA-W018]

Development adds a further historical route. Waddington's canalization account in S28 concerns a fairly definite developmental outcome despite minor variations along its course. His argument distinguishes a trigger selecting a path from conditions participating in its maintenance. The niche-construction accounts consider changes to environments that can outlast the activity producing them. Those sources direct attention beyond an instantaneous input-output relation to the history and persistence of the conditions under which a biological response occurs. [W032; TA-W020]

Zuidberg Dos Martires and colleagues on representing reaction conditions#

Zuidberg Dos Martires and colleagues' 2024 position paper argues that knowledge representation and automated reasoning can help connect molecular knowledge to system-level questions. Explicitly listing every combination of a protein's site states can generate an enormous microstate space; representing only whole components can omit distinctions that observations resolve. The authors favor descriptions separating reactions from the site-specific conditions on which they depend. The representation preserves locally meaningful detail without beginning by enumerating every complete configuration. [BC-W011, §§3–4]

Their signaling illustrations distinguish deterministic Boolean simulation from probabilistic reasoning. Adding probabilities can express uncertainty about reactions but introduces a harder inference problem. They propose knowledge compilation and related reasoning methods to address it, with model structure supplied by biological knowledge and parameters learned from data. Their claim that this integration is necessary for precision medicine is their position; the quantitative programme remains a proposed direction alongside the worked illustrations. S42 provides the separate Darwiche–Marquis account of choosing a compiled representation for the operations it must support. [BC-W011, §5; TA-W008]

Soltani and colleagues distinguish an association graph from an assembly#

Soltani and colleagues' 2026 methodological review distinguishes several meanings of an edge in a protein-interaction network. Direct binding, recovery in the same purified complex, co-expression and shared localization supply different evidence. Proteins can be associated without touching, and interactions observed at different times can enter the same static graph. A dense cluster can therefore fail to describe a physically possible assembly. [BC-W010, §§2, 4]

Their proposed response brings cellular conditions and molecular interfaces into detection and validation. Compatible locations and binding surfaces qualify a candidate; a plausible pairwise interface still does not show that every proposed subunit can assemble simultaneously. They also identify a risk of circular evaluation when functional annotations help generate a prediction and are reused to validate it. Their recommendations concern evidence integration and comparable tests. [BC-W010, §§4.5–4.6, 6, 8, 11]

Li and colleagues on learned flows and biological trajectories#

The flow-matching survey by Zihao Li and colleagues describes learning a vector field that transports a simple probability distribution toward a distribution of biological data. In the continuous formulation, generation follows a learned ordinary differential equation from an initial sample. The flow parameter orders the generation process; its biological interpretation depends on the modeling task. Geometric choices can matter when ordinary straight-line interpolation passes through states unsuitable for the represented data. [BC-W009, Background; Flow Matching Methods]

Selected applications concern cell trajectories and spatial transcriptomics. The review discusses reconstruction from sparse, noisy observations and methods that retain aspects of expression geometry or tissue context. A generated path links modeled observations under learned assumptions; testing a biological transition requires evidence appropriate to that interpretation. The survey's evaluation discussion identifies heterogeneous tasks, datasets and constraints as obstacles to comparable assessment. These passages explain choices in building a computational representation, including the space in which movement is modeled and the conditions by which generated samples are judged. [BC-W009, Dynamic Cell Trajectory Prediction; Cellular Microenvironments from Spatial Transcriptomics; Evaluation Tasks and Datasets]

Li and colleagues on cellular predictors and research agents#

Krinos Li and colleagues' 2025 virtual-cell survey separates direct cellular predictors, called oracles, from agents organizing scientific tasks. Their cellular-modeling discussion includes representation, perturbation prediction and gene-regulation inference. The agent discussion concerns planning, tool use and the division of research work. A predictor of cellular response and an agent arranging an analysis occupy different places in the resulting research process. [BC-W008, §§2–3]

The distinction makes their unresolved questions concrete. Predictors must generalize to unfamiliar cellular contexts and make uncertainty intelligible. Agents must sustain coherent work across tool calls and experimental stages, with suitable evaluation of the completed task. In its single-agent architectures, one model manages the workflow, sometimes switching roles through prompts. Multi-agent architectures divide responsibilities among specialized planners, analyzers or executors that coordinate through dialogue or shared memory. Its taxonomy and surveyed examples concern selected approaches to virtual-cell research; the evaluations it calls for remain part of establishing what these approaches can reliably accomplish. [BC-W008, §3.1 and Conclusions and Future Directions]

Reading routes through the biological foundations#

For a reader interested in building a particular response, the switch and oscillator accounts supply constructions to study alongside Alon's timing mechanisms. The capacity-monitor account adds a way to examine effects on the host. The metabolic primer makes environmental supply and chosen objectives explicit, while the whole-cell model supplies a worked architecture for relating several processes and confronting their combined predictions with observations. The recent accounts address the representation of reaction conditions, evidence for assemblies, learned biological representations and the conduct of research with AI.

The neighboring sections retain further detail: S05 on adaptation and robustness; S26 on production, repair and realization; S28 on development and environmental legacies; S40 on closure, proofreading and synthetic Notch; S42 on representations and reasoning; and S47 on linked flow accounts. These routes allow the particular intervention or modeling question to determine which historical account the reader studies next.

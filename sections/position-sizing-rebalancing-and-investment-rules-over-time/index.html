S90 Position sizing, rebalancing and investment rules over time #

Investment rules act repeatedly. A fraction of current capital determines a changing monetary stake; market movements alter portfolio weights; a trigger determines when those weights are restored. The accounts here develop these temporal arrangements and then consider uncertainty about the return distribution used to choose an allocation.

Sizing for repeated participation#

Kelly's 1956 paper attaches a value to information through a betting construction. A noisy channel supplies information about outcomes, giving the bettor an advantage without certainty. Betting all capital can maximize expected wealth in the simple favorable game, yet a single loss eliminates the capital required to continue. Repeated participation changes the significance of a favorable expected payoff. [MG-W016]

In his even-money example, q is the probability of winning and p the probability of losing. Betting a fraction ℓ of current capital multiplies wealth by 1 + ℓ after a win and 1 − ℓ after a loss. Over many independent repetitions, the logarithmic growth rate is q log(1 + ℓ) + p log(1 − ℓ). Maximizing this expression gives ℓ = q − p. Both the multiplication and the objective matter: expected wealth and long-run logarithmic growth need not favor the same fraction. [MG-W016]

The monetary stake adapts even when the chosen fraction stays constant. It rises after gains and falls after losses because it is applied to current wealth. Kelly connects the resulting maximum growth to information rate in his specified channel model. The known probabilities, odds and nonterminating comparison define the result; the investor's valuation of a finite-horizon outcome is another question, which Kelly explicitly recognizes. [MG-W016]

Maintaining weights rather than holding quantities fixed#

Willenbrock distinguishes a constant-weight portfolio, restored through rebalancing, from a buy-and-hold portfolio whose weights drift. After relative gains, an asset occupies more of the portfolio; after relative losses, less. Restoring weights sells some of the relative gainers and buys some of the relative losers. Maintaining the allocation is an ongoing rule of action, rather than a property secured by the initial purchase. [MG-W017]

His diversification-return analysis distinguishes arithmetic returns from compounded, or geometric, returns. It compares portfolio geometric return with the weighted geometric returns of the component investments. The difference uses that specified baseline. In his 2000–2009 illustration, a portfolio initially split equally between the S&P 500 total-return index and a long-Treasury total-return index is rebalanced annually. Its geometric return is 4.44%, against a 3.32% weighted component baseline, a difference of 1.12 percentage points. [MG-W017]

Willenbrock argues that rebalancing is operative in this increment and examines why attributing the entire effect to a reduction in variance is misleading. Buy-and-hold also has a changing composition: its successful holdings become more influential without corrective trades. The decomposition and the historical example explain these different mechanisms; the stated difference is not a comparison establishing that rebalancing always beats buy-and-hold. [MG-W017]

Deciding when to rebalance#

陆靖昶 and 朱坤 distinguish calendar rules from tolerance-band rules. A calendar rule checks an elapsed period; a band rule checks how far actual weights have moved from targets. The observation controlling action differs. A portfolio can reach a calendar date with little drift, or cross a band long before that date. Defining target weights is therefore only one part of specifying the allocation process. [MG-W018]

Their practitioner account relates rebalancing to maintaining intended exposure and discusses frequency, turnover and market movement. Repeated restoration during a trend and restoration amid reversals need not produce the same comparison. The method consists of observing actual weights, testing the chosen trigger, trading when called for and continuing to monitor drift. More frequent trading is not itself a definition of better maintenance. [MG-W018]

Allocating when the distribution is uncertain#

Li's 2023 Wasserstein-Kelly formulation addresses uncertainty in the distribution used for growth optimization. Historical observations supply an empirical distribution, but treating that distribution as exact can make a chosen allocation sensitive to sampling and misspecification. The formulation constructs a neighborhood of distributions around the empirical one and chooses an allocation against the worst expected log growth within that neighborhood. [MG-W019]

This changes the optimization problem rather than merely replacing one return forecast with another. The size and form of the permitted departures, together with restrictions on possible returns, determine which adverse distributions enter the comparison. Support assumptions matter because historical observations alone may not include outcomes that the investment can experience. The selected formulation explains how distributional uncertainty enters the decision alongside the growth objective. [MG-W019]

The temporal rules developed here specify different objects: a fraction of current wealth, a target allocation and trading trigger, or a set of distributions over which an allocation is evaluated. Each account makes its object and objective explicit. Financial allocation complements the histories of retained choice and investment timing in S34 and S61, where the decisions concern options and commitments of a different kind.

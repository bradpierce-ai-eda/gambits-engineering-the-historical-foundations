S53 Absorbing new resources and capabilities #

Acquiring equipment, finance or outside knowledge can leave substantial work between the acquisition and a useful result. People may have to learn unfamiliar operations, establish relationships, develop supporting services or change how decisions are made. The historical accounts below examine that work and the order in which it develops. Their interventions include organizing training, combining investments, distributing knowledge, adapting institutions and managing the pace of expenditure.

Baldwin's account in S07 explains complementary investments and transition costs; S14 develops changes in work design and information processing. Miller's overload discussion in S21 and the moving-constraint account in S44 address limits on increasing input. The sources here examine how the receiving capabilities themselves develop, including situations in which beginning an activity helps create the conditions for its further expansion.

Rosenstein-Rodan: training and investment beyond a single enterprise#

P. N. Rosenstein-Rodan's 1943 proposal for industrialization in Eastern and South-Eastern Europe treats training as an investment problem. A firm paying to prepare workers may lose them to another employer. The benefit of their acquired skills can exceed the return available to the firm that bears the training cost. He therefore considers training from the standpoint of the group of industries being created. Transport and housing enter the provision for bringing people into industrial work, alongside instruction itself. His proposed large investment unit would include this preparation among its capital costs. [AB-W003, pp. 204–205]

Complementarity also concerns the market for what new enterprises produce. In his hypothetical example, workers employed by one large shoe factory will spend their wages on many products, leaving the factory unable to rely on their demand alone. A group of industries producing the goods those workers require can generate demand for one another's output. The argument for coordinated investment thus includes relations among production, income and consumption. It asks why a project that looks unattractive by itself might contribute to a more valuable combined undertaking. [AB-W003, pp. 205–207]

Rosenstein-Rodan also questions a different proposal: build transport and power facilities and expect other industries to follow automatically. The region's existing transport provision and its intended productive activities affect what infrastructure is needed, where and in what quantity. His coordinated industrial programme is a proposed response to a particular reconstruction problem; the paper explicitly leaves detailed institutional implementation for later work. Training and complementary demand receive explanations, while the task of establishing the proposed investment organization remains to be worked out. [AB-W003, pp. 208–209]

Hirschman: developing the ability to invest#

In The Strategy of Economic Development (1958), Albert Hirschman distinguishes available savings and investment opportunities from the ability to connect them. Recognizing a project, obtaining cooperation and carrying it through require abilities that cannot be assumed to appear whenever finance becomes available. He argues that these abilities develop partly through practice in existing productive activities. An expanding sector can train people and create experience useful for further expansion. This makes the ability to undertake projects part of the development process itself. [AB-W004, pp. 35–38]

His schematic account allows the limiting factor to change. Initially, available or mobilizable savings may exceed what the existing productive sector can turn into investments. As that sector expands, its investing

abilities can increase until finance becomes the tighter restriction. Hirschman cautions that this transition need not occur at one neat date: skills and savings move imperfectly between sectors, so different activities can encounter the change at different times. He prefers investigating this developing ability to treating every difficulty as an obstacle to an assumed normal state in which all available finance would be absorbed. [AB-W004, pp. 36–39]

This interpretation also changes the role assigned to external capital. In his first situation, an outside investment can matter through skills, organization and ways of conducting an enterprise, as well as the money supplied. In a later situation, domestic managerial and entrepreneurial abilities may already outrun available savings, making finance itself more important. These are distinctions within his proposed account of development, with different implications for what a capital transfer must actually provide. [AB-W004, pp. 38–39]

Hirschman: choosing a sequence that elicits a response#

Hirschman's investment discussion compares expanding infrastructure before productive activity with allowing productive activity to advance far enough to create pressure for additional infrastructure. More transport or power capacity can reduce costs and attract investment. Conversely, existing producers facing costly shortages can press authorities to expand those services. The first route invites an activity through improved conditions; the second attempts to elicit action through a difficulty people already experience. His comparison depends on entrepreneurial response and on the responsiveness of the authorities responsible for infrastructure. [AB-W004, pp. 86–93, figs. 3–4]

His diagram separates the resources needed for a static least-cost combination from the decisions through which a country might reach a larger productive scale. He assumes, for that comparison, that infrastructure and directly productive activity cannot both be expanded at once. Infrastructure built ahead of demand supplies spare capacity whose value depends on subsequent use. Production expanded first can make the need for an additional road or power supply concrete enough to generate a decision that previously attracted little support. Those assumptions and induced responses, rather than a preference for imbalance as such, do the explanatory work. [AB-W004, pp. 86–90, fig. 3]

There is a technical minimum. Access and some supporting services must exist before an activity can operate at all. Prolonged neglect can also make expansion self-defeating: another factory may claim scarce services at the expense of existing firms, leaving little gain in aggregate productivity. The absence of an absolute barrier to building the new factory can conceal that displacement. Hirschman's discussion therefore distinguishes a shortage capable of provoking a useful response from one that continues to damage the productive system. [AB-W004, pp. 94–97]

He also allows experience to change the comparison. Investors who expect infrastructure providers to respond, and providers who anticipate growing demand, may act before an actual shortage or excess appears. Under those conditions, his alternative inducement curves permit coordinated expansion to sustain further investment. The same account that explains the possible usefulness of an uneven sequence also explains how learned expectations can reduce reliance on the difficulties that initially elicited action. [AB-W004, pp. 91–93, figs. 4a–4b]

Dierickx and Cool: expenditure flows and accumulated capabilities#

Ingemar Dierickx and Karel Cool's 1989 article distinguishes resources that can be traded from assets that develop through a history of activity. Trust, firm-specific knowledge and a reputation for reliable quality are among their examples. Current expenditure can purchase inputs to that history without immediately producing its accumulated result. Their stock-and-flow description makes the distinction

explicit: additions build an asset stock, while erosion diminishes it. A current research budget and accumulated technical know-how therefore describe different quantities. [AB-W002, pp. 1505–1506]

They call one possible property of accumulation time compression diseconomies. Increasing expenditure over a shorter period may not reproduce what a longer process would have developed. Their examples include crash research programmes and attempts to compress education. They also identify interconnectedness: building one asset can depend on another stock already in place. A service network, for example, can provide customer knowledge that contributes to technological development. These are proposed properties to investigate in particular accumulation processes, and the authors explicitly say that a process need not possess all of them. [AB-W002, pp. 1507–1509]

An accumulated advantage also needs maintenance and can be displaced by a different approach. Their Canon–Xerox illustration describes design capability substituting for a service network on which an earlier competitive position depended. The article's question is the sustainability of competitive advantage. Its conceptual examples explain why buying more inputs, accelerating expenditure, preserving an existing asset and developing a substitute are different strategic actions. [AB-W002, pp. 1508–1510]

Cohen and Levinthal: prior knowledge and the organization of reception#

Wesley Cohen and Daniel Levinthal's 1990 account names a firm's ability to recognize useful external knowledge, assimilate it and apply it commercially as absorptive capacity. Prior related knowledge helps people understand and connect new information. Their argument joins existing knowledge to effort and practice: information can be available without being intelligible or usable to its recipients. Research, technical training and experience in production can contribute to this capacity through different activities. [AB-W001, pp. 128–131]

Organizational reception involves more than employing several knowledgeable individuals. Someone encountering an outside idea must connect it with people who can evaluate or use it internally. A specialist at the boundary may translate unfamiliar material, but recipients still need enough related understanding to act on what is communicated. Where technical change is rapid and uncertain, the authors argue for a broader distribution of people able to notice relevant developments. Shared understanding supports communication, while excessive uniformity can restrict the different associations through which something new is recognized. [AB-W001, pp. 131–135]

They also explain why purchasing expertise may leave integration work unresolved. An external consultant or newly hired specialist can bring knowledge without possessing the firm's understanding of its own needs, routines and complementary capabilities. Maintaining some internal expertise helps participants interpret outside work and connect it with those particulars. Similarly, participation in a joint research venture leaves a firm with internal work if it is to use the venture's output. The authors distinguish knowledge related to ordinary activity, for which capacity may develop as a byproduct, from unfamiliar domains requiring deliberate preparation. [AB-W001, pp. 135, 148–150]

Their empirical discussion examines an indirect implication of this account: how learning conditions affect firms' research expenditure. It combines reported characteristics of technical knowledge with manufacturing business-unit data, rather than directly observing a completed absorption process. The R&D evidence reported in the article derives from their earlier work. Greater relevance of basic rather than applied science was associated with more research spending, as predicted when less targeted knowledge requires more internal effort to absorb. These spending patterns do not by themselves establish the causal effectiveness of the organizational practices described. Their closing discussion retains the difficulty of valuing indirect benefits when deciding how much to invest. [AB-W001, pp. 139– 150]

Noblet and Simon: making organizational practices observable#

Jean-Pierre Noblet and Eric Simon's 2008 study attempts to make absorptive capacity examinable through what firms do. They interview leaders of ten innovative companies, largely small and medium enterprises, using two appointments separated by two to four weeks and about thirty hours of interviews in total. Their literature-derived grid distinguishes acquiring, assimilating, transforming and exploiting knowledge. Indicators include alliances, shared understanding, research activity, changed routines and management support. They analyze meaningful passages in the interviews, with interpretation checked by another researcher. [AB-W007, pp. 9–12]

The reported practices give those categories substance. One leader describes joint research followed by a report and discussion that can change working practices. Others identify employees who scan external information or help colleagues find an outside application when their own work is blocked. The body of the paper emphasizes managerial openness, contact with final customers and trust. These concern willingness to reconsider an activity, opportunities to learn what a product must do, and relationships through which people exchange knowledge. [AB-W007, pp. 12–16]

Measurement itself becomes a practical issue. Interviewed leaders challenge patent counts as an adequate indicator for their firms because confidentiality, retained know-how and filing costs affect whether they patent. Noblet and Simon offer an exploratory operationalization based on leaders' accounts and call for further development of the instrument. Their practices and proposed indicators help identify what an investigation could observe, while the small selected sample and self-reports limit conclusions about their effects on growth or innovation. [AB-W007, pp. 13–16, appendices 1–2]

Picoli and Takahashi: similar equipment, different incorporation into work#

Florindo Rhaoni Picoli and Adriana Takahashi's 2016 study compares two public schools in Curitiba that received computer laboratories through Paraná Digital. Both received twenty computers in 2007 and had access to training and online manuals. The researchers collected interviews, documents and nonparticipant observations in 2013, reconstructing implementation since the programme began. They deliberately selected schools with contrasting levels of use and broadly comparable staffing, enrollment and location. [AB-W008, pp. 8–12]

At Professora Luiza Ross, earlier experience with a school computer laboratory helped staff approach the new equipment, although its Linux system differed from the software many already knew. Formal meetings and training were joined by informal exchanges during breaks. Staff discussed successful and unsuccessful experiences, tried the technology in practical situations and adapted how they worked. The authors associate these interactions with the transformation and use of knowledge obtained through the programme. [AB-W008, pp. 9–11]

At Santo Agostinho, staff also had some personal computer experience and access to the programme's support. The researchers nevertheless found little exchange about classroom use or change in teaching routines. The reported use was student internet research outside classes, without pedagogical guidance. The source's comparative coding locates the difference beyond initial introduction and understanding, in the sharing, experimentation and incorporation of knowledge into work. The knowledge-sharing account in S42 gives a neighboring treatment of support after transfer. [AB-W008, pp. 11–16; W088]

Picoli and Takahashi distinguish their observed knowledge-flow processes from a stronger claim about organization-wide learning. They say the latter would require deeper investigation of changes in culture, values and practice. Prior laboratory experience, space and other institutional differences also remain part of the comparison. The evidence develops an account of how people came to use supplied

technology; computer-use totals and staff reports do not themselves measure improvements in students' learning. [AB-W008, pp. 13–17]

Zhang and colleagues: imported technology and delayed innovation effort#

Zhang Jie, Chen Zhiyuan, Wu Shufeng and Sun Wenhao's 2020 article investigates the relation between foreign technology introduction and firms' own innovation effort. Its firm-level analysis uses the 2008– 2014 National Enterprise Innovation Survey database. The principal outcomes are spending on science and technology activity per science and technology worker, including a measure net of government funding. Technology-introduction expenditure is excluded from those outcomes. The study therefore examines changes in further innovation inputs rather than directly measuring inventions, productivity or operational mastery. [AB-W009, pp. 93–95]

The authors address reverse causation using an instrument based on government catalogues encouraging technology and product imports, matched to industries and lagged to allow for implementation. Their instrumental-variable estimates do not show a statistically significant contemporaneous increase in the spending outcomes. More consistent positive estimates appear for outcomes two years ahead. They interpret the delay through the work of learning, digestion and assimilation following a technology purchase. The estimates rely on the policy instrument's assumptions, while the learning process remains the authors' explanation of the observed delay. [AB-W009, pp. 94–98, tables 2–3]

Their theoretical model and subsequent comparisons also distinguish firms by position relative to industry leaders. The more consistent positive spending responses occur in the middle portions of their innovation-gap distribution. The reported tables are less uniform than a simple middle-only rule: one measure in the most distant group has weakly significant positive estimates. The model and the data comparisons make the initial capabilities and competitive setting relevant to the question, while the estimated delay and group boundaries remain properties of this investigation. [AB-W009, pp. 98–103, table 4]

Polterovich: institutions can change with acquired capabilities#

Victor Polterovich's 2009 account of building a national innovation system argues for intermediate institutions that help relax constraints on further development. His technological borrowing includes choosing a technology, obtaining rights, adapting it to local conditions, constructing prototypes and testing. The receiving activity can itself modify or improve what was acquired. Domestic scientific work remains important for adaptation, so borrowing and new knowledge creation are not mutually exclusive phases in his argument. [AB-W010, “Problem statement: seeking a strategy of intermediate institutions”; “Absorptive and innovative capabilities of a country”; “Transition to innovative development”]

A proposed institutional sequence changes as firms become able to do more. Polterovich reports the establishment of Korea's KIET in 1976 to support learning in semiconductor technologies and coordinate research. In the historical account he cites, the growth of corporate research led to a reorientation of that institution eight years later toward basic research supporting innovation. He uses the example to explain why an organization built to compensate for a missing capability can require a different role once that capability develops elsewhere. This is his report from the cited development literature. [AB-W010, “Main agents of technology imitation: the state and large firms”]

His account also examines interdependent investment. Refineries can hesitate over costly modernization while domestic equipment producers hesitate over supplying machinery without assured demand; downstream production may require changes as well. He proposes repeated interaction among public authorities, business, workers and consumers to coordinate expectations and investments. Indicative planning is an intermediate institution in this argument, capable of changing or receding as other

relations become adequate. His prescriptions concern catching-up development and its institutional conditions, and he expressly rejects a universal recipe for dividing resources between basic research and borrowing-based modernization. [AB-W010, “Escaping the trap: increasing absorptive capability through interactive planning”; “Transition to innovative development”; “Situation in Russia: approaches to modernization”]

Collier and colleagues: investing resource revenues requires an investment process#

Paul Collier, Frederick van der Ploeg, Michael Spence and Anthony Venables examine the use of resource revenues in capital-scarce economies. Their 2009 account distinguishes a high return on small additions to existing capital from the returns obtainable when investment expands rapidly. A spending increase can run into construction, skills and other domestic supply constraints. Prices then rise and other activities can be displaced. Some equipment can be imported, but productive, human and managerial capabilities are not all obtainable through an international purchase. [AB-W005, section 4.1]

The response they discuss includes changing the supply conditions. Investment in infrastructure and skills can support private production; administrative barriers, equipment-import delays and earlier underinvestment can otherwise restrict the response to new demand. They also distinguish technical and informational limitations from incentives that favor diversion or poorly chosen projects. A larger budget does not settle which of these difficulties a spending system faces. [AB-W005, sections 4.1, 4.5]

Their account separates ownership of the funds, control of aggregate spending and choice of individual projects. Transfers to citizens, public spending, domestic lending and foreign-asset accumulation allocate those functions differently. Passing project decisions to private actors can use their information and incentives, but the argument still requires channels that connect savers with productive investments. Their discussion of public expenditure separately examines honesty and efficiency, scrutiny before and after spending, and enforcement through authority, citizens, peers and professional conduct. [AB-W005, sections 4.2–4.5, tables 1–2]

Revenue volatility adds a different timing problem. A buffer of liquid foreign assets can protect spending from abrupt revenue changes, yet a fund large enough to stabilize everything can postpone valuable domestic consumption and investment. The authors consider allowing investment flows to vary while the accumulated capital stock changes less sharply. They also discuss designing construction-sector employment and financial commitments to withstand contractions. The capacity to manage an investment programme includes its response to lower revenues as well as to an initial expansion. [AB-W005, sections 5.1–5.3]

Their conclusion about a liquidity fund is consequently conditional on its role in the investment process. It can buy time for adjustment. It can also become counterproductive if saving overseas substitutes for developing the ability to invest effectively at home. Their proposed task is to restructure that process for a higher and more variable level of investment, with buffering as one instrument in the adjustment. [AB-W005, section 5.3]

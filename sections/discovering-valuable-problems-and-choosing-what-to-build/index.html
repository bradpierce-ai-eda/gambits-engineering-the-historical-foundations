S87 Discovering valuable problems and choosing what to build #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

Choosing what to build involves representations of needs, technical possibilities and a firm's offering mix. A customer can report a present difficulty without imagining a new product, while a technically attractive concept can fit poorly with other products. The accounts here develop ways of representing product decisions and finding users whose experience contains information about emerging needs.

Representing the product decision#

Krishnan and Ulrich organize product-development literature around decisions. Their 2001 review compares the concerns of marketing, organizational research, engineering design and operations management. A product can appear as a bundle of attributes, an organizational undertaking, an interacting assembly or a flow of development activities. These representations make different relationships and measures prominent. Stating the decision helps identify which representation supplies useful information. [MG-W008]

The review distinguishes decisions within a development project from decisions that establish its setting. Concept development, architecture, variants, shared components, prototyping and launch occur within a context of product strategy, project selection and development organization. Choices about an individual component can therefore depend on a product-line decision that the local design problem takes as given. Optimizing one project does not settle which projects should exist. [MG-W008]

Their launch-sequencing discussion illustrates the connection. An earlier introduction can earn profit sooner, yet a new offering can displace sales of another product. Likewise, offering variants and sharing components can change both market coverage and development work. Their review locates these relationships across research traditions, rather than reducing product-development performance to minimum lead time. Its decision map helps a reader distinguish the object being chosen, the objective used to compare alternatives and the related decisions outside the immediate project. [MG-W008]

Finding needs ahead of the market#

Von Hippel's lead users have two characteristics: they encounter needs ahead of a significant market trend and expect substantial benefit from solving those needs. Those conditions make them informative in different ways. Being ahead provides experience with a problem others may face later; expecting a large benefit supplies a reason to search for or construct a solution. An early purchaser is not necessarily a lead user, since buying a new offering does not itself establish either condition. [MG-W007]

The inquiry begins with a consequential trend and seeks users who are already confronting its demands. It then examines their needs, adaptations and solutions, before assessing which findings transfer to the intended market. Von Hippel argues that typical users can be constrained by their experience of familiar products and attributes. Lead-user inquiry instead finds people for whom an emerging problem is already practical experience. [MG-W007]

The search can extend beyond the producer's existing customers and industry. Users facing demanding conditions elsewhere may already have addressed a related need: the paper discusses such connections as lightweight materials in aerospace and automotive uses, and controls for commercial buildings and homes. Transfer is a further question, however. A demanding user's solution provides something to inspect and evaluate, rather than a substitute for understanding the target users. [MG-W007]

The IBM component-insertion machine illustrates a user-developed solution moving into supply. In von Hippel's account, the user developed the design and supplied drawings with an order to a manufacturer. Permission to sell the machine more widely followed. The sequence matters: an experienced user's work

supplied a concrete design before the supplier treated it as a general commercial offering. Information about need and information about a possible solution had been produced together in use. [MG-W007]

Lead-user inquiry therefore includes both discovery and evaluation. Finding an inventive user can reveal a possibility; assessing its applicability determines whether that possibility belongs in a proposed product. Product-line decisions remain another level of choice. Krishnan and Ulrich's review concerns how those decisions relate, while von Hippel supplies a particular route for obtaining information about needs and solutions. The histories of inventive and associative methods elsewhere develop further ways of generating concepts.

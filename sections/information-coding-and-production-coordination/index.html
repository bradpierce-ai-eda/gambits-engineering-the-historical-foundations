Shannon: recover a selected message from alternatives#

Claude Shannon's 1948 A Mathematical Theory of Communication begins with a particular engineering problem: reproduce at one point a message selected at another. A system must be prepared for the possible selections, rather than only the message that happens to be sent. Its source, transmitter, channel, receiver and destination have different roles. The transmitter makes a suitable signal from the message; the receiver reconstructs the message from that signal. [W025, introduction]

The paper expressly sets semantic aspects aside for this engineering problem. That choice permits analysis of the alternatives and the transmission process without requiring the theory to establish what a recipient understands, believes or values. For equally likely alternatives, a logarithm of their number gives a convenient measure; base two expresses it in bits. Four equally likely messages correspond to two bits in that counting sense. [W025, introduction; arithmetic illustration]

The opening also makes coding a material and procedural operation. A telephone transmitter converts sound pressure into current; a telegraph code supplies a pattern of dots, dashes and spaces; more elaborate systems sample, quantize, encode and interleave. The abstract channel is one part of the process. The source's framework explains how differently constituted communication arrangements can be analyzed under common specified questions. [W025, introduction, communication-system description]

Reliable transmission is a capacity result with a coding obligation#

In the selected noisy discrete-channel result, Shannon relates a source's entropy rate to channel capacity. Below the relevant capacity, suitable coding can make the frequency of errors or remaining equivocation arbitrarily small under the model. The result is stronger than saying every bit must travel perfectly in isolation. A receiver can use a suitably organized longer signal to recover what was sent despite particular corruptions. The construction depends on the channel/noise model and the rate being considered. [W025, theorem 11]

Shannon's following discussion is unusually important for interpreting that accomplishment. The proof does not generally supply a straightforward practical method for constructing the ideal code. Appropriate redundancy must combat the particular noise structure, and approaching the ideal generally introduces delay. A longer sample gives the receiver a better basis for deciding which original signal caused the received one. The selected passage thus presents reliable transmission together with the work of coding, decoding and waiting that makes it possible. [W025, §14]

Redundancy serves two distinct roles. Statistical redundancy in a source can permit more compact representation; suitably retained or introduced redundancy can also help recover a message when the channel is noisy. Shannon's English-language example concerns contextual constraints assisting reconstruction, rather than establishing that all repetition should be removed. An arrangement that optimizes one objective can change the other. The desired rate, tolerated error and available delay must remain specified before a coding improvement can be assessed. [W025, §14]

Exchanging information to coordinate production#

In a 1999 conference paper, Stanley Reiter compares two tractor makers. One buys a gear train designed specifically for its tractors. The maker and its supplier exchange information and negotiate so that the gear trains arrive when the assembly schedule requires them. The other makes the gear train in a

separate unit of the same firm. Reiter observes that this unit and the tractor plant still face the same scheduling problem and may settle it by the same exchange of information. He contrasts this with divisions of an integrated petrochemical company exchanging intermediate products at transfer prices set to outside market prices. The examples distinguish the channel by which production decisions are coordinated from the ownership of the units making them. [DP-W001]

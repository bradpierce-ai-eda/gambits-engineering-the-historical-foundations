Making something available differs from making it usable#

Physical records, symbolic systems, programs, classifications, standards and assurance arrangements make information available for action. Their design shapes which operations a person can perform and which distinctions a representation preserves.

Pattee: symbols require physical realization and interpretation#

Pattee's selected discussion relates dynamical laws, constraints, memory and symbolic control. He argues against accounts that detach symbols from physics and against accounts that assume real-time sensorimotor dynamics explain every aspect of life. A relatively persistent memory structure can affect a time-dependent process through physical mechanisms that read or respond to it. The symbolic description and the dynamics are distinguishable, but the connection needs an implementation. [UX-W012, discussion of laws, constraints and memory]

This account does not classify every useful physical constraint as a symbol. A structure can constrain motion without serving as a record interpreted within a symbolic-control system. Pattee's discussion of flexible mechanical constraints, switches and molecular mechanisms asks how stored alternatives can enter dynamic operation. The relevant material is the connection between a comparatively rate-independent record and rate-dependent execution, not a free-standing informational cause outside physical processes.

Pattee’s epistemic-cut argument relates descriptions to operations and connects symbolic control with his account of biological origins. [UX-W012]

Etxeberria and Moreno’s reconstruction of Pattee’s hierarchical-control account [UX-W014] distinguishes structural hierarchies from control hierarchies and discusses constraints, records and symbols in that setting. Its usefulness is the distinction between assembling levels of material organization and specifying how a record controls a process. A higher descriptive level does not become a controller merely by being drawn above another level. [UX-W013, selected manuscript pp. 2–4 and concluding caveat]

Darwiche and Marquis: choose a representation for the operations required#

A Knowledge Compilation Map examines propositional theories compiled in advance into representations suited to later reasoning. Darwiche and Marquis compare languages along two dimensions: succinctness and the queries or transformations they support in polynomial time. A language can be convenient for one operation and unsuitable for another. The choice is therefore application-dependent rather than a contest to find one universally smallest or fastest representation. [TA-W008, printed pp. 229–236]

Their framework treats several classes as restrictions on directed acyclic representations in negation normal form. Conditions such as decomposability, determinism and smoothness impose different structures. Those restrictions affect which operations are tractable and how large a representation may need to be. A more concise language can leave an operation hard; a less concise compiled form can make repeated queries manageable.

The authors propose identifying the needed operations before choosing a target language. The relevant saving is not simply deleting information. An equivalent representation can reorganize the information so that a particular query is easier. Conversely, having a small equivalent representation in principle does not establish that it can be constructed efficiently from the original input. Compilation and representation size remain separate obligations.

Jones, Gomard and Sestoft: move known computation to an earlier stage#

The opening of Partial Evaluation and Automatic Program Generation explains specialization using a program with some inputs already known. A partial evaluator produces a residual program for the inputs still unknown. When given those remaining inputs, the specialized program is intended to return the result the original would have returned from the complete input. The relation is semantic, while its efficiency depends on what can actually be evaluated in advance. [TA-W009, selected printed pp. 1–4 and staging passages pp. 6–9]

The authors' power-function example is concrete. Knowing the exponent in advance can allow recursive control and exponent-dependent work to be unfolded or precomputed. Knowing the base instead, while leaving the exponent unknown, need not yield a significant speedup in the same example. Advance information has to match the structure of the computation; any known input is not equally useful.

Their term binding-time engineering concerns changing when subcomputations occur. It retains the earlier computation, the specialized code and the circumstances in which the preparation remains valid. Correct specialization is not by itself proof of faster execution or lower total cost. [TA-W009]

Necula and Rahul: a smaller oracle can guide a trusted checker#

The selected 2001 oracle-based checking paper describes a variant of proof-carrying code. Trusted inference rules are represented as a logic programme, and a nondeterministic interpreter checks the property. A supplied bit stream resolves choices that would otherwise require search. The oracle guides the checker; it does not replace the rules that determine whether the proposed steps are acceptable. [TA-W011, PDF pp. 1–3]

The article contrasts this with carrying an explicit proof syntax for every inference. Its proposal can exploit directed or simple checking procedures without charging the full proof-size overhead of a very general representation. In the reported type-safety examples for code compiled from Java, the authors report oracle sizes averaging twelve percent of code size and a factor-of-thirty reduction relative to an earlier proof representation.

The retained trusted components are decisive: the policy, verification-condition generation, inference rules and checker must be appropriate. Cryptographic evidence of who supplied code differs from evidence about what the code does. A compact oracle can reduce transmitted proof information while verification still requires execution of the trusted procedure. Safety remains relative to the specified property, not unrestricted harmlessness. [TA-W011]

Necula introduced Proof-Carrying Code in 1997; the account above follows the later paper. [TA-W010]

Edmonds: greedy choice depends on an independence structure#

Edmonds considers finite linear-objective combinatorial optimization and asks why certain problems admit efficient algorithms. In a matroidal problem, feasible independent sets satisfy a structure that permits a greedy procedure to select weighted elements while preserving feasibility. A simple sequence of choices can be globally justified under these conditions, rather than relying on a general belief that local decisions are good. [TA-W012, printed pp. 127–130]

The representation of the feasible family matters: the source explicitly asks how membership is determined. An independence test or oracle is part of the algorithmic setting. Arbitrary constraints do not necessarily have the exchange property behind the characterization.

The selected characterization explains how an independence structure supports a computational method. [TA-W012]

Liskov and Wing: an interface must preserve behavior over time#

Liskov and Wing's selected account of behavioral subtyping asks when an object of a subtype can be used while reasoning with the supertype's specification. Matching method names or signatures is insufficient. Invariants and the allowed evolution of state matter. The source includes ways of preserving history properties directly through constraints or indirectly by explaining additional methods through operations already available at the supertype. [W087, selected §§1–2 and 5.2–5.3]

Aliasing makes the issue substantive. The same mutable object can be accessible through more than one name, with one user reasoning through the supertype and another invoking subtype methods. Effects of the additional method can later be visible through the first name. A contract that considers only an isolated call can therefore miss behavior that undermines the first user's reasoning. [W087, printed pp. 1811–1815]

The stack-and-bag discussion explains how an additional operation can preserve or violate what remains fixed in the abstract history. The account thus concerns substitution under a stated behavioral contract, not an absence of knowledge about implementation. The contract itself must specify the relevant guarantees and be justified against the implementation. Liveness and certain cross-object safety properties lie outside the stated scope. [W087, selected printed pp. 1822–1829 and 1836–1837]

Tonet and da Paz: knowledge sharing continues after initial transmission#

Tonet and da Paz propose four phases of workplace knowledge sharing: initiation, implementation, support and incorporation. Their account attends to the source, recipient, knowledge and context rather than treating sharing as the delivery of a detachable message. Support after initial transfer matters when the recipient encounters difficulties using what was received. [W088, selected four-phase model and support discussion]

The sequence helps distinguish access from incorporation into practice. A proposal can fail because participants lack the relevant understanding, opportunities or contextual support, even after information has been supplied. The model offers questions and guidance for examining the process.

Buckland: stored things are not identical to knowledge#

The conceptual discussion in Buckland's Information as thing distinguishes information as a process, knowledge and evidence treated as a thing. Storage and retrieval systems directly handle objects or representations: documents, records, data and other things regarded as informative. Calling them information does not guarantee that a user has learned from them or that their content is true. What counts as informative is situational and can depend on agreement about evidence. [GL-W020, situational information and the topography of information-related activities]

His topography of information-related activities distinguishes the constraints of museums, archives, printed libraries and coded-data systems. Selection, representation, identification, location and physical access are not the same task in each setting. A convenient representation can support one activity without preserving every physical or evidential property of its subject. The account is conceptual classification.

Potts: conventions and context both contribute to meaning#

Potts's exposition distinguishes conventional linguistic content from context-dependent interpretation. Indexicals require a setting to determine what is referred to; implicatures involve what a speaker conveys beyond a literal formulation. Compositional rules can support interpretation without eliminating the situation in which an utterance is used. [GL-W014, selected body on semantics and pragmatics]

This supplies a substantive qualification to any picture in which a message is wholly self-contained. The recipient's context and conventions can carry information not repeated in the words.

Star and Lampland: standards coordinate while retaining local work#

The selected pages of Reckoning with Standards examine standards as arrangements embedded in activities, rather than neutral descriptions floating above them. A standardized category, measure or procedure can enable work across settings while fitting some local circumstances better than others. The work of interpreting, fitting and maintaining the standard does not vanish when the written form is shared. [TA-W025, pp. 3, 6–7 and 10–11]

The chapter's account directs attention to classifications and their consequences for those who must use them. Uniformity can facilitate coordination and also obscure variation or make accommodations necessary.

Star and Ruhleder followed the Worm Community System, built so geographically dispersed geneticists could share information. Some laboratories needed to obtain and configure windowing software and learn how to reach a remote machine before they could use it. The instructions assumed that users already knew how to obtain help with those steps. Even with favorable feedback on the prototype, many biologists struggled to get to routine use. During the study, many respondents turned to the World Wide Web for information exchange. [TA-W024, case study and discussion of signing on]

Archives: context is part of description#

The introduction to Records in Contexts–Conceptual Model, version 1.0, frames archival description through records and their relations to contexts. It supplies a descriptive model for recording relationships rather than only a flat list of isolated documents. The relevant contribution is an explicit structure for representing provenance and context. [GL-W023, 2023 version 1.0, selected §§1.1–1.2]

Traceability: a documented chain still carries uncertainty#

The CNAS 2021 guidance defines metrological traceability through a measurement result's relation to a reference by a documented chain of calibrations, with uncertainty contributions retained. The account concerns traceability requirements and definitions. [TA-W030, cover, foreword and printed pp. 2–3]

The NIST FAQ and policy make the responsibilities explicit. The provider supports a traceability claim; the user assesses its validity. NIST's provision of its own services does not certify every other measurement claimed to be traceable to it. Traceability also does not guarantee fitness for a purpose: the uncertainty may still be too large for the intended use. A certificate cannot substitute for the user's accuracy requirement. [TA-W031; GL-W101, policy §3.2 and FAQ]

NIST attributes its formal definition to VIM. [TA-W031; GL-W101]

Role-based access: permissions are prepared through assignments and constraints#

Sandhu, Ferraiolo and Kuhn's 2000 NIST model organizes permissions through roles rather than treating every authorization only as an isolated individual grant. The proposal provides levels of RBAC capability, including hierarchical and constrained forms. The four levels describe the capabilities distinguished in this 2000 proposal. [TA-W032, selected PDF pp. 1–3 and 8]

The selected constrained model distinguishes restrictions on assigning users to roles from restrictions on activating roles in sessions. Separation of duty seeks to prevent one person from possessing excessive

combined authority. It retains the possibility of deliberate collusion among multiple people. Administrators must still assign roles, maintain permissions and enforce the runtime rules.

Internal control: separation, authorization and review remain different functions#

The selected 2001 GAO exposure draft discusses segregation of duties, authorization, records and review. Dividing incompatible responsibilities can make some errors or fraud harder for one actor to carry out unnoticed, while authorization specifies who may act and documentation supports later examination. The source also recognizes that collusion can undermine separation. [TA-W033, historical draft, printed pp. 38–45]

This is historical prescriptive guidance. A formal division of duties does not establish that staff actually perform them, that records are complete or that review is competent. The arrangement's operation remains part of what has to be assessed.

The archived AU319 standard distinguishes control activities and monitoring in a financial-statement-audit setting. Monitoring concerns whether controls continue to operate appropriately and whether needed changes are made. [GL-W044, selected §§.21–.22 and monitoring paragraphs]

The IFAC guidance similarly addresses evaluating and improving controls as part of organizational activity. Its overview and appendix do not equate a passed reporting control with effective operational control. [GL-W048]

Zero trust: the boundary is not an unconditional authorization#

The abstract of Zero Trust Architecture describes an approach that does not assign implicit trust merely from network location or ownership. The relevant shift is toward explicit decisions about access to resources. [GL-W045]

Journalism verification: source and content are separate objects of checking#

The selected UNESCO training module sets out work with a source, image, metadata and location evidence. Establishing who supplied an image differs from establishing what it depicts, where it was made or whether its surrounding claim is accurate. A verification workflow distributes those questions across different evidence rather than expecting a single cue to answer all of them. [GL-W047, selected module 6, PDF pp. 100 and 102–103]

The EXIF discussion retains a specific limitation: metadata may be absent, altered or otherwise insufficient. The handbook is a professional curriculum. Interpretation and source assessment remain work performed within the workflow.

Mapping and classification: the useful omission is relative to a purpose#

The USGS cartographic orientation treats generalization as a way to make geographic information useful at a selected scale and purpose. Selection and simplification can remove visual or informational detail that a particular map cannot usefully present. The textbook warning makes the condition clear: what is omitted may be important for a different task. [GL-W066, institutional orientation and selected sample passage]

The UBC guide to the Brian Deer Classification System describes a specialized original scope and local adaptations at different institutions. A classification can fit a collection's communities and relationships better when it can be adapted. The same flexibility can create differences among collections that matter for cross-collection use. [GL-W072]

The guide traces the classification system through earlier accounts and describes the work of maintaining categories suited to a local setting.

Shaw and the proof-theory entry: explicit levels of description#

Shaw's selected chapter opening distinguishes source, data, generative, structure and meta levels in knowledge and expert systems, drawing on Klir and applying the account to personal constructs. Observations, a model that generates them, a structure organizing that model and questions about the modeling arrangement are not interchangeable. The framework helps identify which level a proposed knowledge representation is meant to address. [SG-W034, selected PDF pp. 1–5]

Shaw reconstructs and applies Klir's epistemological hierarchies. [SG-W034, selected chapter passages]

Rathjen and Sieg's selected proof-theory reconstruction examines formal proof, foundations, reverse mathematics and feasibility. It helps distinguish formal derivability from questions about the resources or strength needed to establish a result. [GL-W087]

Using representations in practice#

The section provides source-specific explanations of preparation, representation, interpretation and assurance. Their guarantees differ: semantic equivalence, behavioral contracts, formal tractability, measurement traceability, archival context and professional procedure answer different questions.

S98 Goal-setting: choosing aims, directing effort and revising commitments #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

A goal can specify what an engineer is trying to construct, what an athlete attends to during performance, or what a manager rewards. It can also express an aim that someone accepts, revises or relinquishes. These uses bring different problems into view: constructing an adequate target, sustaining effort, learning how to act, recognizing a useful opportunity, and deciding whether pursuit should continue. Historical accounts of goal-setting address each of these problems through different models, procedures and kinds of evidence.

Constructing and adopting aims#

桑原哲郎 and 岸野哲's 1977 account begins before the target is fixed. An engineering problem does not always arrive with a satisfactory objective function awaiting optimization. The target itself must be constructed through inquiry into the system and its environment. They present this inquiry as a way of discovering causal regularities rather than restricting the goal to improvements under conditions already fixed. Their proposed reasoning follows effects from the system into its surroundings and the responses that return to it. Understanding those relations can change what it would be purposeful to accomplish. In this account, abstraction is part of discovering the target, rather than merely simplifying a target already supplied. [GS-W02]

The authors distinguish a creative phase and three execution phases. The creative phase abstracts the object, considers its wider relations and makes a judgment about purpose. The first execution phase introduces concrete conditions and asks whether the proposed account is effective; inadequacy can require returning to abstraction. The second works toward consistency between system and environment, including conditions left unexplained earlier. The third introduces economic and feasibility conditions and establishes the functions to be realized and the constraints under which conventional engineering design can proceed. Their table and diagrams distinguish phases by the determinacy of the target and the kind of judgment being made. The result is a proposed architecture for reasoning about an engineering objective, with revision built into its construction. [GS-W02]

Sheldon and Elliot's 1999 self-concordance model asks why people pursue the personal goals they have adopted. A person may act because the activity is enjoyable, because its importance is personally accepted, because failure would provoke guilt or self-reproach, or because other people demand it. Personally accepting an unpleasant but valued task differs from enjoying it, and both differ from acting under pressure. Self-concordance concerns these reasons for pursuit. It is therefore possible to specify the same outward achievement while having substantially different relationships to that achievement. [GS-W04]

Their model connects concordance with sustained effort and goal attainment. It then connects attainment with well-being through experiences of autonomy, competence and relatedness. A further relationship matters: concordance is expected to modify what attainment provides. Achieving a personally concordant goal should yield more need-satisfying experience than achieving a goal pursued for less personally accepted reasons. The account thus concerns both persistence toward an end and the experience of having reached it. [GS-W04]

In the first study, 169 students listed ten personal goals and rated their reasons for pursuing them. Repeated assessments over a semester followed effort and progress. The concordance index added intrinsic and identified reasons and subtracted external and introjected reasons. The reported associations were consistent with effort mediating attainment. The model begins with selected goals and commitment; its explanation concerns differences among adopted pursuits rather than the prior deliberation through which every possible end might be compared. [GS-W04]

Harald Seelig's operational account makes the component reasons especially tangible through exercise. Interest in the activity, acceptance of its value, self-reproach for failing to exercise and pressure from others can be examined separately. He explains that the difference index is a simplified global measure: inspecting the component profile gives more detail than the resulting score alone. The profile preserves distinctions between different combinations of reasons that a single total can conceal. [GS-W05]

Д. А. Леонтьев's account of self-regulation also gives the adoption of goals an active role. An aim may be supplied externally, but accepting it still involves the actor's selective judgment and responsibility. He contrasts the absence of personal criteria for selection with rigidity that makes an accepted goal impossible to relinquish. These are different problems: being unable to choose among demands and being unable to revise a choice once made. His discussion places both within the structure of activity and its correction. [GS-W10]

Directing attention, effort and learning#

In their 2002 retrospective, Locke and Latham explain four pathways through which goals affect task performance. Goals direct attention toward relevant activities, mobilize effort, prolong persistence and prompt the use or acquisition of task knowledge. Specificity reduces ambiguity about what counts as the required performance; difficulty supplies challenge. Knowledge, commitment and information about progress affect how those processes operate. The account explains why merely announcing a target can differ from giving someone a target that is understood, valued and connected to workable action. [GS-W01]

A particularly consequential distinction concerns performance and learning goals. A performance goal specifies the score or result to achieve. A learning goal concerns discovering or acquiring strategies for the task. When a task is unfamiliar and complex, a demanding score can draw effort toward immediate performance while the performer still lacks an effective method. The relevant work may instead be systematic strategy acquisition. Locke and Latham's discussion therefore distinguishes applying available knowledge from learning what action the task requires. Their account of commitment likewise includes the goal's importance and the person's perceived capability, rather than treating difficulty alone as sufficient motivation. [GS-W01]

Díaz-Ocejo and Mora-Merida's review distinguishes outcome, performance and process goals in sport. Winning a race is an outcome goal. Improving one's own previous time is a performance goal. Directing attention to a technical action is a process goal. The distinctions concern the content of the target and what the athlete can attend to or influence during execution. A process goal specifies an action to attend to, whereas a learning goal specifies knowledge or a strategy to acquire. [GS-W16; GS-W01]

The review reports more consistent support for goal-setting interventions in motor performance than for a uniform ranking of specificity, difficulty or proximity. Comparisons differ in who sets the target and how its difficulty is determined. Its discussion of proximity also raises flexibility: a commitment to a number of training sessions during a week permits adjustments that a rigid daily requirement may prevent when injury or another interruption occurs. The practical question is how the target organizes training under changing conditions, as well as how challenging it sounds when first stated. [GS-W16]

Frutuoso's sport-practice review develops a concrete coach–athlete account. Written goals are coupled with a strategy, a deadline, periodic evaluation and feedback. Goals are developed with the athlete, and the target actually pursued is compared with the one formally stated. Competition and training targets are distinguished; simultaneous aims need priorities and compatibility. Revision is part of the proposed practice. The inventory he describes elicits athletes' perceptions of goal-setting, while his recommended package brings formulation, execution and evaluation together as a coaching practice. [GS-W17]

From a desired future to situated action#

Oettingen, Pak and Schnetter's 2001 fantasy realization account distinguishes imagining a desired future from relating it to an obstructing present. Indulging in the future elaborates its attractive aspects; dwelling on the present elaborates difficulties. Mental contrasting brings the desired future and the present obstacle into relation. The proposed effect is expectancy-dependent commitment: determination strengthens when success appears feasible and weakens when it appears unlikely. The procedure is consequently concerned with selective commitment, rather than simply making every wish more motivating. [GS-W06]

Their first experiment involved 136 female students who named an important interpersonal problem, rated the importance and likelihood of a happy outcome, and listed positive aspects of that future and obstacles in the present. Contrasting instructions alternated elaboration of future and obstacle; comparison instructions elaborated one side alone. Immediate planning and perceived responsibility served as commitment indicators. The authors' general discussion reports the predicted expectancy-dependent pattern across their four experiments. Commitment here means determination to pursue the future, rather than a binary choice or a ranking of competing ultimate values. [GS-W06]

Gollwitzer and Sheeran's account of implementation intentions begins with a different difficulty: a person can intend an outcome yet fail to initiate the necessary action. Forgetting, missing an opportunity, initial reluctance and distraction pose different obstacles. Constructing an implementation intention involves identifying an instrumental response, choosing a feasible situation in which to perform it, and linking situation and response in an if–then plan. The selected response must serve the goal; the cue must be one the person can actually encounter and recognize. [GS-W07]

Their explanation has two parts. Specifying the situation makes its cue more accessible, and linking that cue to the response facilitates initiation without renewed deliberation at every encounter. Deliberate preparation is intended to make later action less dependent on a fresh decision. In a coupon-collection example discussed by Gollwitzer and Sheeran, location cues are connected with collecting the coupon; the account examines cue recognition and subsequent action, rather than intention alone. Their discussion also considers the strength and activation of the underlying goal. A pursuit already proceeding easily need not gain in the same way as one hindered by an initiation problem. [GS-W07]

Wang, Wang and Gai's 2021 meta-analysis evaluates mental contrasting with implementation intentions, or MCII, as a combined intervention. A desired future is related to an obstacle, and an obstacle-linked response plan is constructed. Included comparisons encompass MCII against no treatment and adding MCII to an intervention against that intervention without MCII. The review analyzes 24 independent samples or effect sizes from 21 reports, with 15,907 participants. Its random-effects estimate for goal attainment is g = 0.336: an average advantage of about one third of a standard deviation over the included controls. The 95% confidence interval of 0.229–0.443 expresses uncertainty around this pooled estimate. Heterogeneity I2 = 59.321% indicates substantial variation among study estimates beyond sampling error. [GS-W08]

The review's publication-bias diagnostics give different indications. Its trim-and-fill procedure estimates how the pooled result would change if apparently missing studies were included, yielding an adjusted g of 0.242. This is a sensitivity estimate based on that procedure's assumptions. Its pooled comparison evaluates the specified intervention against the included controls. Most comparisons do not isolate whether the combination outperforms each component separately. The delivery-mode analysis also has a different basis: it excludes two large online-course samples totaling 13,909 participants. Differences among delivery modes in that reduced collection are comparisons across studies, rather than randomized assignments to delivery modes within the full participant sample. [GS-W08]

Feedback and the people who manage goals#

野上真 and 古川久敬's 2010 review examines managerial action across goal formulation, execution and achievement appraisal. It distinguishes communicating or emphasizing demands, providing support through advice and feedback, and incorporating performers' views into decisions. These activities can occur at different points in the work. A manager can consult someone about the target, advise on the method, discuss progress during execution or involve the performer in appraisal. Each changes a different part of the goal-setting process. [GS-W03]

The review considers learning, satisfaction and organizational commitment alongside productivity and motivation. Advice about performing an unfamiliar task supplies something different from a report of the distance still remaining to a numerical target. Participation in devising a strategy also differs from participation in choosing the number. The authors find more developed evidence for support than for achievement demands and identify combinations across phases as a research question. Their summary table collects reported positive findings; their proposed complementarity among managerial actions is an interpretation and direction for research. [GS-W03]

Locke and Latham's discussion of participation similarly distinguishes a reasoned assignment from a terse command. Their account of the Erez–Latham collaboration examines what information and rationale accompany assignment, as well as whether the performer participated in setting the goal. Participation in developing task strategies can supply useful knowledge even when choosing the target is not the decisive motivational difference. Progress feedback gives the performer a basis for altering effort or strategy. The comparison therefore involves the interaction and information surrounding the target, as well as its nominal source. [GS-W01]

Леонтьев separates receiving feedback, comparing the actual state with a desired criterion, and correcting action. Information can be available without being received; an error can be recognized without producing a change of conduct. His conceptual structure distinguishes failure to begin acting from failure to notice a discrepancy and from failure to respond after recognizing it. Correction may concern the route being taken or the goal itself. These distinctions make it possible to describe several different failures that would otherwise appear simply as failure to attain a target. [GS-W10]

In Frutuoso's coaching account, periodic evaluation and feedback return attention to the goal actually being pursued. A formal agreement between coach and athlete does not by itself establish that training is organized around that agreement. Comparing stated and pursued aims, reviewing progress and revising the plan give the target an ongoing role in the working relationship. The accounts of participation and organizational strategy in S92 develop related questions about interpreting objectives and taking part in decisions. [GS-W17]

Revising, relinquishing and replacing goals#

Wrosch and colleagues' 2003 account addresses self-regulation when a goal becomes unattainable. Disengagement has two components: reducing effort and relinquishing commitment. Stopping an activity while remaining intensely committed to its outcome differs from releasing the commitment itself. Reengagement has its own components: identifying alternative goals, investing them with value and beginning their pursuit. The distinction permits someone to develop a new pursuit while the old commitment is still being relinquished. [GS-W09]

Participants rated how readily they could reduce effort and release commitment to an unattainable goal, and how readily they could find, value and pursue alternatives. The authors consider lost opportunities, changed life circumstances and competing demands on limited time and energy. These contexts give the problem a practical form: continuing to pursue one aim can interfere with pursuits that remain available,

while the absence of an alternative can make disengagement harder. Their framework distinguishes capacities for releasing and replacing goals rather than requiring a fixed sequence in which one must be completed before the other begins. [GS-W09]

The three samples comprised 115 undergraduates, 120 younger and older adults, and 45 parents of children with cancer or medically healthy children. Associations connected goal-adjustment capacities with aspects of subjective well-being. The authors identify the cross-sectional design and possible cohort differences as constraints on causal interpretation. The studies relate adjustment capacities to well-being; they do not supply a test for deciding that a difficult undertaking is objectively unattainable. [GS-W09]

Леонтьев's discussion of flexibility distinguishes changing a route from changing the desired destination. Recognizing a discrepancy can prompt either response. A goal that cannot be reconsidered can organize repeated action around a criterion that circumstances no longer support, while indiscriminate acceptance of new demands creates a different difficulty. His conceptual account places revision within self-regulation itself. The backward-planning accounts in S94 examine how means are derived from intended results; these adjustment accounts concern the possibility that the intended result will also change. [GS-W10]

Organizational objectives and the debate over targets#

Doran's 1981 formulation of SMART concerns making managerial objectives usable and communicable. Specific identifies an area for improvement. Measurable calls for quantity or an indicator of progress. Assignable identifies who will do the work. Realistic relates the intended results to available resources. Time-related specifies when results are to be achieved. The original A therefore concerns assignment of responsibility. His illustrative objective calls for developing and implementing an inventory system by December 31 of a designated year to reduce inventory costs by $1 million. The work is to take no more than 200 work hours and $15,000 in initial out-of-pocket expenditure. The example connects the intended result with a deadline and specified resources. [GS-W11]

Doran also allows abstract objectives and does not require every objective to satisfy every criterion or be quantified. Pairing the objective with an action plan matters to his account: writing an intelligible result and organizing its accomplishment are connected managerial tasks. He discusses managers' anxiety about commitments involving variables they cannot control and senior managers' role in communicating objectives. The proposal addresses formulation and coordination. [GS-W11]

Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky and Bazerman's 2009 critique asks what targets cause people to attend to and what selected performance measures leave out. They discuss a proofreading experiment in which explicit grammar or content targets are contrasted with an instruction to do one's best. Concentration on a specified dimension can leave the other dimension unattended. Their discussion of multiple goals likewise raises competition between quantity and quality: adding a second objective changes the demands rather than automatically resolving the allocation of attention. [GS-W12]

The critics also distinguish using dishonest means to attain a goal from falsely reporting that it has been attained. A target can organize conduct leading to a result, and a reward can depend on a report about that result; the two create different opportunities for misconduct. Corporate incidents illustrate their argument for examining risk and conduct alongside output. In their subsequent rejoinder, they distinguish using case histories to identify consequential questions from using them to prove causality and argue for studies that permit and measure broader consequences. [GS-W12; GS-W14]

Locke and Latham's reply contests inference from anecdotes, selective citation and the characterization of earlier research. Their stated position is that proponents had already described many of the pitfalls presented as neglected. The disagreement thus concerns both evidential standards and what the existing theory actually asserts. The proponents' earlier retrospective develops conditions involving knowledge,

commitment, rationale, participation and feedback; the critics ask whether investigations adequately capture consequences beyond the chosen performance score. [GS-W13; GS-W01; GS-W14]

文鹏, 任晓雅 and 陈诚's 2017 review separates target attributes, the setting process and target content. Difficulty and specificity concern attributes; participation and rewards concern how goals operate organizationally; result-oriented and process-oriented formulations concern content. The authors also distinguish violation of ethical norms from violation of an organization's internal rules. Conduct can serve an organization's immediate interests and still violate broader moral expectations. That distinction prevents organizational compliance from silently becoming the definition of ethical behavior. [GS-W15]

The review develops three proposed explanations. Goal shielding concentrates attention on an important target while other goals receive less attention. Organizational targets can thereby signal what is valued and where effort should go. Moral disengagement concerns justifying misconduct: treating the result as overriding other considerations can make deception seem acceptable, or an imposed target can encourage an actor to shift responsibility to the person who set it. The depletion account treats self-control as a limited resource. Demanding goals require effort and increase cognitive load; reduced self-control can then make conduct less consistent with internal standards. [GS-W15]

The review also examines studies involving unverifiable performance reports, nearly attained targets and moral justification. It records inconsistent findings and limited organizational-field evidence. The contrast between result-oriented and process-oriented content is presented as theoretically proposed, with direct tests still lacking in that comparison. The organizational debate extends from how an objective is written to what conduct is observed, what outcome is rewarded and what counts as an adequate explanation of the result. [GS-W15]

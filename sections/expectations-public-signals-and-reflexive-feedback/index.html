S59 Expectations, public signals and reflexive feedback #

An announcement can matter because of what it reveals, because people can see that others have received it, or because it changes the consequences of acting alone. Prices, votes and other observable actions can themselves become information. The following accounts examine these processes through communication, investment decisions and the interaction between financial markets and institutions. They ask what participants can observe, what they infer, how they are evaluated, and how their responses change subsequent events.

Chwe: knowing that others received the message#

Michael Suk-Young Chwe's 1998 article starts with two coworkers who usually leave the bus together for a drink. On a crowded journey they become separated. A mutual acquaintance calls to invite them off at another stop. Each would enjoy joining the acquaintance, but mainly wants the other's company. Both may hear the invitation and nevertheless remain on the bus: neither is sure that the other heard it and will get off. The message has reached both people without supplying the assurance each needs to act. [EF-W003, pp. 49–50]

Knowing that the other heard is not always enough. One coworker may see the other look up but remain unsure whether the other has noticed that the invitation was mutually received. Chwe uses this progression to explain common knowledge: everyone knows something, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and the corresponding further levels hold. In the example, mutually observed eye contact supplies an immediate way to establish the relevant shared awareness. The communication problem includes what people know about one another's reception of the message. [EF-W003, pp. 49–50]

The dependence on joint participation matters. Chwe changes the example so that one coworker's partner is calling from the street. That coworker will get off regardless of what the other does. The remaining coworker then needs to know that the invitation was heard, but need not obtain the same assurance about mutual awareness. The original difficulty came from each wanting to participate only if the other participated. [EF-W003, p. 50]

Chwe applies this argument to the physical organization of public encounters. In inward-facing circles, people can observe others receiving a communication and reacting to it. His discussion of circular seating at Fort Worth's city hall emphasizes the audience's visual contact with one another as well as with the council. His reconstruction of revolutionary festivals similarly distinguishes the circle's symbolic meaning from the opportunity it gives spectators to see one another. Removing partitions or arranging sight lines can change this opportunity even when the spoken content remains the same. [EF-W003, pp. 58–59]

Publicity also enters his account of advertising. A buyer may value a product more when others use it, as with compatible computers or goods consumed together. Advertising on a conspicuously popular programme can inform viewers that many other viewers have encountered the product. Chwe compares advertising for 119 brands over three representative months of a network year. The eighteen brands classified as social goods appeared before larger audiences and paid substantially more per viewer than the other brands. The comparison is suggestive: the available brands were not a representative sample, classification was partly judgmental, and the social group was dominated by products aimed at men, particularly beer. [EF-W003, pp. 59–63, tables 1–2]

Chwe also addresses the apparent implausibility of indefinitely repeated beliefs about beliefs. People need not consciously recite an infinite chain. They can rely on familiar situations, recursive recognition or practical heuristics for judging shared awareness. He explicitly distinguishes such supports from a failsafe communication procedure. Common knowledge helps explain coordinated action without guaranteeing it. [EF-W003, p. 66]

Informational cascades: more followers need not mean more evidence#

Sushil Bikhchandani, David Hirshleifer and Ivo Welch's 1992 article examines a different reason for doing what others do. Participants decide sequentially whether to adopt an action. They observe predecessors' choices, but not their private information. Each also receives a private signal about whether adoption is worthwhile. The model's payoffs do not require a taste for conformity or a reward for agreeing with other people. Earlier actions matter because they may reveal information. [EF-W005, pp. 994–996]

In the article's elementary example, adoption has a known cost and an uncertain benefit, with two possible values. A signal is more likely to favor adoption when adoption is valuable. The first person's choice reveals that person's signal. The second combines this information with a new private signal, using a random tie-break when the two indications balance. After two preceding adoptions, the third person can find adoption preferable even after receiving an unfavorable signal. After two rejections the corresponding reversal occurs. With one adoption and one rejection, the third person's own signal again determines the choice. [EF-W005, pp. 996–997, table 1]

The consequential step is the loss of information in subsequent actions. Once someone chooses the same action regardless of the private signal received, observing that choice tells later participants nothing about that signal. Later people can then face the same informational balance and make the same choice. Many identical decisions may therefore rest on the information conveyed by only a few early decisions. They are not necessarily many independent confirmations of the action's merit. [EF-W005, pp. 998–1001]

The authors compare this with a setting in which previous signals remain observable. An individual may still choose against a private signal, but the signal enters the common information pool. Enough contrary signals can eventually reverse behavior. In the actions-only setting, signals arriving after the cascade begins remain hidden behind identical choices. The difference concerns what an observed action preserves about the reasoning or evidence behind it. [EF-W005, p. 999]

This also explains the fragility of a cascade. A public disclosure less informative than one person's private signal can make a long-established cascade unravel, because it need only counter the limited information that originally started the process. The crowd's size has not continually deepened its evidential basis. The article distinguishes disclosure before a cascade from disclosure afterward. An early noisy disclosure can change predecessors' choices in a way that reduces what later people learn from those choices; after a cascade has begun, new public information can add evidence without destroying information that later actions were still transmitting. These are results about the specified sequence and information structure. [EF-W005, pp. 1004–1007]

Scharfstein and Stein: the reputation gained by following others#

David Scharfstein and Jeremy Stein's 1990 model makes evaluation by other people part of the decision. Two managers consider investing in a cost-saving technology, with the first deciding before the second. Each receives a signal about the investment's eventual profitability. The outcome later becomes observable even if neither invests. Investment is available at a fixed price, so the model isolates reputation and inference without also determining a market-clearing asset price. [EF-W004, pp. 465–468]

Managers differ in their ability to obtain useful signals, but initially neither they nor the labor market know which managers have that ability. In the central case, capable managers receive correlated informative signals; other managers receive independent noise. An evaluator can therefore learn about ability from both the investment's eventual success and the similarity of the managers' decisions. Two managers making the same unsuccessful choice may have encountered an adverse circumstance that neither could have foreseen. A manager making an isolated unsuccessful choice has less of that explanation available. The authors call this sharing the blame. [EF-W004, pp. 466–469]

The second manager consequently has a reason to imitate beyond learning whether the investment will be profitable. Agreement can make that manager appear to have received the kind of information a capable colleague received. In the authors' herding equilibrium, the second manager follows the first even when a private signal points elsewhere. The information possessed by both firms need not be reflected in investment. Reputation affects future compensation, and maximizing that compensation can conflict with making the best present investment. [EF-W004, pp. 469–473]

This is a model of incentives within an evaluation system. Its managers need no explicit agreement to suppress private information. Nor does it require evaluators to be unaware of the incentive to imitate: managers' attempts to influence the inference about their ability occur within the equilibrium being analyzed. The definition of ability, what evaluators observe, the sequence of decisions and the dependence between informative signals all enter the original construction. [EF-W004, pp. 468–473]

The paper also develops countervailing incentives. Giving managers a material stake in profits can make sufficiently costly imitation unattractive. Rewarding relative standing can make merely matching another manager less appealing, because imitation prevents becoming the top-ranked performer. A broader conception of ability can reward finding a useful alternative rather than forecasting which of the same two choices will succeed. These changes affect what a manager gains by preserving a distinct judgment. [EF-W004, pp. 475–476]

The authors apply the argument to committee procedure. Sequential public voting may allow later members to echo the first speaker instead of contributing their information. Simultaneous written votes are one proposed response, although they can reduce valuable exchanges of ideas. Another is to let those with stronger reputational concerns speak first; under the conditions discussed, this can mean hearing younger members before established senior members. [EF-W004, pp. 477–478]

A later exchange clarifies what counts as herding#

Marco Ottaviani and Peter Sørensen's comment challenges the original claim that correlated prediction errors are necessary for reputational herding. They connect reputational imitation with statistical learning from predecessors' choices. An observed decision can change both the probability assigned to a favorable state and the reputational payoff associated with acting in that state. Relaxing a restriction on prior probabilities allows cases in which a later manager disregards private information even without the original form of conditional signal correlation. Their account gives learning and evaluation joint roles in the resulting decision. [EF-W009, opening argument; EF-W010, pp. 705–706]

In their 2000 reply, Scharfstein and Stein accept that result under the comment's broader definition, but distinguish it from a manager always following a predecessor. A sufficiently favorable prior can make even the first manager ignore a contrary signal. After an informative first action, the second manager can face similarly favorable odds and invest regardless of a new signal. In that case, the second manager could make the same choice even if evaluators could not observe the predecessor's action and could not tell that copying had occurred. The reply reserves the stronger sharing-the-blame interpretation for the mechanism in which visible agreement itself improves the evaluation. The exchange separates sometimes ignoring information, always copying another person, and benefiting from being seen to agree. [EF-W010, pp. 705–706]

Orléan: the resale price changes what it is rational to anticipate#

André Orléan's 1988 article, L'auto-référence dans la théorie keynésienne de la spéculation, reconstructs Keynes's distinction between estimating an asset's productive returns and anticipating its resale price. A person intending to hold an asset indefinitely has to consider its future income. A person expecting to sell also needs to consider the price another buyer will pay. The two valuations can be algebraically

compatible while depending on different activities of inquiry: examining productive conditions, or anticipating other market participants' judgments. [EF-W006, pp. 229–231]

Orléan emphasizes the institutional role of negotiability. The possibility of selling a financial claim does not make the corresponding physical investment withdrawable by the community as a whole. It does, however, expose the individual holder to changing market quotations. In the example he draws from Keynes, someone can judge an investment's long-term value to be thirty while expecting the market to value it at twenty in three months. Paying twenty-five now need not be attractive merely because the long-term assessment is higher. Expectations about a prospective transaction affect the sensible present action. [EF-W006, pp. 231–233]

Resources also affect whether a person can maintain a long-term position. Someone exposed to an unexpected need to liquidate cannot simply disregard an adverse current quotation, however confident that person is about eventual productive returns. Orléan uses this dependence to explain why attention to short-term market judgment can follow from the organization of a liquid market, rather than from a fixed personal disposition to speculate. In his interpretation, the same participant can be drawn into market-oriented anticipation by the conditions under which commitments must be financed and revised. [EF-W006, pp. 233–234]

Self-reference enters when the object being anticipated is produced by participants' anticipations. Each tries to anticipate the average judgment while recognizing that others are doing likewise. Orléan's discussion of Keynes's beauty contest gives this its recursive form: success depends on anticipating how other people anticipate the collective selection. A configuration of expectations can be validated by the price that those expectations help produce. Such validation answers a different question from whether investment is serving long-term productive requirements. [EF-W006, pp. 235–238]

Orléan presents this as a substantive reading of Keynes and a challenge to the organization of financial inquiry. He also finds formal resources in rational-expectations models that permit self-validating price paths in addition to a fundamental-value solution. His conclusion asks how conventions, institutions and forms of information beyond private competitive arbitrage can be understood. [EF-W006, pp. 238–241]

Morris and Shin: a public signal can coordinate a common error#

Stephen Morris and Hyun Song Shin's 2002 model gives public information two roles. It informs each participant about an underlying state, and it provides something each expects others to use. Participants want their actions to suit the state, but also want to be close to the actions of other participants. Each observes a shared noisy signal and a separate private signal. Their choices are made together rather than in the sequential chain used in an informational cascade. [EF-W008, pp. 1521–1526]

The equilibrium action places more weight on the public signal than a forecast concerned only with the underlying state would place on it. The reason is strategic: the common signal helps predict what others will do as well as what the state is. Higher-order expectations pull toward the information known to be publicly shared. The same property gives any error in that signal an unusually wide influence. Participants can all respond rationally within the model while reinforcing a common informational error. [EF-W008, pp. 1525–1530]

The welfare result depends on what is being evaluated. The authors construct the rewards for matching others as a zero-sum competition. Those rewards cancel when individual payoffs are added, leaving the average squared distance between actions and the underlying state as the social loss. Individually useful coordination therefore receives more weight in decisions than it receives in this welfare criterion. With a sufficiently strong coordination motive and sufficiently accurate private information, an improvement in a still-noisy public signal can worsen that criterion. [EF-W008, pp. 1524, 1528–1529]

Other cases in the paper preserve the value of information. When the shared signal is the only information available, greater accuracy improves welfare. Greater precision of private signals improves welfare in the central model. The authors also examine what changes when participants want to differentiate their actions rather than match them; the tendency to overweight public information then changes. Their disclosure problem concerns the interaction between signal precision, incentives and the stated objective, including the practical choice between early provisional information and later more accurate information. [EF-W008, pp. 1522–1523, 1529–1532]

Bacchetta and van Wincoop: finding a scapegoat in the price#

Philippe Bacchetta and Eric van Wincoop's 2004 article asks why market participants repeatedly change the importance they assign to particular macroeconomic indicators when explaining exchange rates. Its scapegoat is an observable economic variable given excessive weight through inference from the exchange rate. The model includes observed money supply and output, unobserved currency trades, and uncertainty about the persistence of economic conditions. Investors receive different private signals about the relevant persistence parameters. [EF-W001, pp. 114–116]

An exchange-rate movement is therefore ambiguous. It may reflect unobserved trading, or it may reflect information other investors possess about how persistent a change in money or output will be. Each investor rationally uses the price as a public signal alongside private information. The current price is partly an outcome of participants' expectations and partly evidence from which they estimate what those expectations contain. [EF-W001, pp. 115–116]

Consider the article's case in which money supply is unusually high and the exchange rate rises because of unobserved currency trades. An investor seeing a higher rate than expected can infer that other investors have information suggesting greater monetary persistence. Even investors whose own information gives little reason for that inference can revise their assessment because they do not know everyone else's signals. In the model, the private-signal errors average to zero, yet inference from the price produces a market-wide mistaken estimate. Everyone can think others possess supporting information that no one actually possesses. [EF-W001, pp. 116–117]

The resulting weight affects behavior and the exchange rate. Confusion amplifies the influence of the hidden trading shock and can increase both the actual and perceived importance of the visible monetary variable. Output can become the scapegoat under the corresponding conditions. The model also permits reduced weights, and its average weight can be correct across shocks while particular realizations produce substantial errors. Its result concerns the changing relation between observable conditions, private estimates and a price used as evidence. [EF-W001, pp. 117–118]

The short model limits information differences to one period and rules out bubbles in its later common-information setting. Its authors leave a fuller dynamic account to subsequent work. The operative explanation here is dispersed information and rational signal extraction; an observed indicator becomes influential because of what investors infer from the price, and that inference feeds into the price itself. [EF-W001, pp. 115, 118]

Soros: responses can change the conditions being interpreted#

George Soros distinguishes two functions of participants' thinking. The cognitive function attempts to understand conditions; the manipulative function attempts to influence events. In his account of reflexivity, these functions interfere because participants act within the reality they are trying to understand. Their interpretations influence conduct, while conduct changes the situation that will subsequently be interpreted. His 2013 symposium remarks distinguish corrective feedback from

reinforcing feedback and place interactions between authorities and markets alongside the more dramatic case of financial bubbles. [EF-W002, opening conceptual argument]

His 2009 lecture on financial markets gives a concrete credit mechanism. Easier credit encourages transactions and raises real-estate values. Defaults become less frequent, recorded loan performance improves, and lenders relax their standards. The increased apparent safety of lending is partly produced by the credit expansion whose safety is being judged. Treating collateral value as independent of credit availability misses this dependence. When the process reverses, forced liquidation can depress property values and weaken the collateral supporting remaining loans. [EF-W007, pp. 1–3]

In this account, a price is more than an inaccurate description of an independently fixed condition. Credit terms, transactions and collateral provide routes by which valuation affects what can actually be financed. Soros also discusses equity-financed expansion and the conglomerate boom: elevated expectations permit acquisitions that improve reported earnings, until performance can no longer sustain expectations. The credit example supplies the clearest operative sequence, while the conglomerate case illustrates his wider claim that different financing arrangements can transmit the interaction. [EF-W007, pp. 1–3]

Soros describes a boom as a real trend reinforcing a misconception about that trend. Tests can interrupt it; surviving a test can strengthen both confidence and the trend. He includes a period in which doubts grow while activity continues, followed by reversal and reinforcement in the other direction. He also says that corrective feedback can stop a reinforcing process before it completes this sequence. The account does not predict in advance whether a particular test will be survived. Soros acknowledges that he expected the 1997–1998 crisis to end a longer credit expansion and was wrong. [EF-W007, pp. 2, 4, 6]

Intervention is part of the feedback in his historical interpretation. Authorities rescue failing institutions and support activity; participants then interpret the resulting survival as evidence about the system. Soros argues that repeated successful rescues can reinforce confidence that markets are self-correcting even when official action was what prevented collapse. His proposed responses include varying margin and capital requirements and paying attention to credit availability, rather than treating control of the money supply as sufficient. He also distinguishes emergency support during a contraction from restrictions intended to limit a later expansion. [EF-W007, pp. 6–9]

The 2013 remarks extend his analysis to institutional rules. In his account of the euro, common treatment of government bonds in bank-capital and central-bank collateral arrangements encouraged purchases of higher-yielding debt. Converging borrowing costs contributed to divergent national economic trajectories; later recognition of default risk changed the pressures on indebted countries and on the authorities. The rules, interpretations and financing conditions developed together. This is Soros's application of reflexivity to a particular institutional history, where public authorities are participants rather than outside observers. [EF-W002, euro-crisis discussion]

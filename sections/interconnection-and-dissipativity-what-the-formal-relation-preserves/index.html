From an arrangement to an admissible behavior#

The selected control and network sources make the structure of an arrangement mathematically consequential. They explain different questions: how a governor responds dynamically; what an energy-accounting inequality establishes; how components share a behavior; and when decomposing a network calculation saves computation. The formal words “control,” “passive,” “interconnection” and “decomposition” retain their source-specific meanings here.

Maxwell: stronger correction can destabilize the motion#

James Clerk Maxwell's 1868 On Governors opens by distinguishing devices that increase resistance with speed from governors whose continuing adjustment can restore a normal speed within working limits. That difference concerns the dynamics of the correction, rather than simply the presence of a speed-responsive part. Maxwell then divides disturbances into growth or decay, with or without oscillation. A useful governor must reject growing departures, including oscillations whose amplitudes increase. The intended direction of an adjustment does not settle that question. [W069, opening, printed pp. 270–272]

He describes a limit at which making the regulation faster leads instead to dancing and jerking motion. The example associated with Fleeming Jenkin's governor gives the mathematical problem a mechanical setting: adjustment parameters that appear to increase governing power can change the character of the coupled motion. Its pertinent lesson remains specific: a regulator's nominal corrective action and the stability of the whole process are different claims. [W069, pp. 271–272]

Willems: stored quantity cannot increase beyond supplied quantity#

Jan Willems's 1972 dissipativity theory specifies a dynamical system, an input/output-dependent supply rate, and a nonnegative storage function of state. Dissipativity requires the increase of that storage over every admitted trajectory and time interval to be no greater than the accumulated supply over the interval. Written schematically, S(x1) − S(x0) ≤ ∫w dt. The storage function and supply rate must be identified together with the system and its allowed inputs. [W014, §3, definition 2, printed p. 327]

For a small arithmetic illustration, suppose the initial storage is 5 units and the integrated supply is 3 units over the chosen interval. The inequality permits terminal storage no greater than 8 units. It does not determine whether the actual value is 8,6 or another nonnegative value consistent with the trajectory. If the chosen supply has physical energy units, those units must be used consistently.

The universal quantification also matters. Checking one favorable trajectory is insufficient to establish the required inequality for all admitted inputs, states and intervals. Nor is nonnegative net supply on a closed state-space cycle alone the definition: Willems explicitly says that cyclic condition is inadequate by itself for dissipativity as defined. A proposed connection among components consequently needs more than an appealing energy-flow drawing or a single observed balance. [W014, definition 2 and following discussion]

Available storage is a limit on extraction#

Willems defines available storage by asking how much can be extracted from a given initial state over all allowed future motions and horizons. It is a supremum of the negative accumulated supply. Including the zero-duration choice makes this quantity nonnegative. Theorem 1 states, in the developed setting, that finite available storage for every state is equivalent to dissipativity, that available storage itself is a

possible storage function, and that it is no larger than any nonnegative storage function satisfying the inequality. [W014, definition 3 and theorem 1, pp. 327–328]

The proof's idea is useful to understand. From one state, first follow an allowed path to a second state, then extract what is available there. This combined procedure cannot exceed the best extraction possible from the first state. That comparison gives the dissipation inequality for available storage. Conversely, an existing nonnegative storage function bounds what can be extracted along any such path. The two directions connect the input/output extraction question to the storage bound. [W014, theorem 1 proof]

The article expressly warns that available storage need not equal actual stored energy. Not every internally stored quantity must be accessible through the external terminals under the admitted operations. Treating extraction capacity as the physical total would therefore overstate what the formal result supplies. The distinction is relevant whenever a selected interface is said to reveal all resources inside an arrangement. Likewise, an energy inequality alone does not establish that a chosen gait or a desired asymptotic equilibrium will occur.

Willems 1997: a controller can be another physical system#

In On Interconnections, Control, and Feedback, Willems questions the universal use of a sensor-to-computation-to-actuator signal-flow picture. His door and door-closing mechanism share a physical interaction: force and angle are related through both mechanical systems. Assigning force as cause on one side and effect on the other may be convenient for a conventional controller description, but it is not the only way to describe their interconnection. The paper retains intelligent feedback as useful while proposing a more general framework. [UX-W017, §III, pp. 328–329]

In the behavioral approach, a system's behavior is the set of trajectories compatible with its laws over a time axis and signal space. When two systems share signals, their interconnected behavior consists of trajectories satisfying both sets of laws: an intersection of behaviors. Interconnecting only some terminals can be represented by leaving the others free in the appropriate component. Internal connection variables can later be suppressed through the distinction between manifest and latent variables. The relationship supplied by the interconnection is part of the model, rather than something inferred from an unexplained arrow between boxes. [UX-W017, §IV, pp. 329–330]

This viewpoint lets a mechanical attachment count as a controller without requiring it first to sense, represent and compute in the manner of a digital controller. It does not make every physical attachment adequate. The admissible component behavior and the joint constraint must actually yield the required motions. An intersection can remove possible trajectories while also removing behavior needed for the intended task.

An unrestricted mathematical controller can make the problem trivial#

The 1997 paper itself demonstrates why controller admissibility matters. If arbitrary additional restrictions are permitted in a linear behavioral setting, an interconnection can trivially force a small stable behavior. That formal construction can look stronger than familiar control results because the permitted controller class is so broad. Willems calls attention to the objection and turns to regular interconnection and implementation. A mathematical subset selected by imposing arbitrary laws is not automatically a physical controller that can be connected through the allowed means. [UX-W017, §§VI– VIII, especially p. 332]

Controller admissibility determines which variables can be constrained and which interconnections can be physically realized. The system assumptions and permitted means of connection are therefore part of Willems’s control problem.

Van der Schaft: composition preserves an energetic structure#

Arjan van der Schaft's December 2024 preprint on nonlinear port-Hamiltonian systems describes physical modeling through energy storage, dissipation and power-conserving interconnection. In its selected composition account, components connected through an appropriate power-conserving relation yield another port-Hamiltonian system. The combined stored energy is the sum of component Hamiltonians, with the component dissipation relations retained. The relation at the interface is what permits this accounting; arbitrary coupling is not assumed to have the same property. [GL-W069, §1.1]

Combining the power-preservation relation, storage definition and dissipation condition gives its key inequality: the rate of increase of stored energy is no greater than supplied port power. If the Hamiltonian is bounded below, the system is passive with respect to that supply rate after a suitable storage normalization. “Passive” here is an energetic input/output property. It does not mean that no controller is present or that no external energy arrives. [GL-W069, §1.2, eq. 9]

The preprint also distinguishes zero-input analysis from steady operation with nonzero inflows and outflows, such as a power network. The relevant equilibrium and storage comparison must change accordingly; shifted passivity addresses that different question under further stated conditions. Its treatment of power ports and energy ports also retains different variable roles in later sections. [GL-W069, §§1.2–3]

Van der Schaft and Jeltsema trace port-based modeling to the bond graphs pioneered by Henry Paynter. Each bond carries two quantities whose product is power. Voltage and current serve this purpose in an electrical connection, and force and velocity in a mechanical connection. Using power at the connection gives the model a way to represent exchanges between components from different physical domains. [GL-W070, §1.1]

Willems gives a circuit example with two resistors, a capacitor and an inductor. He writes the laws of the components and connectors, then identifies the variables at the terminals that are joined. To describe what can be observed from outside, he retains the voltage difference across the external terminals and the current entering the circuit. The internal quantities still have to satisfy the component and connection laws. [UX-W019, fig. 8 and accompanying text]

Wu: separating subnetworks retains an interconnection solve#

Felix Wu's 1975 Solution of Large-Scale Networks by Tearing develops a network solution related to Kron's diakoptics. The network is separated into subnetworks, those are solved locally, and an interconnection-level calculation restores the constraints across the cut. The method retains the Kirchhoff relations rather than treating the cut parts as independent complete answers. The selected explanation distinguishes subnetwork computation from the work of joining their results. [SG-W023, selected opening and algorithm discussion]

Wu describes a possible parallel implementation in which satellite computers perform subnetwork work while a central computer performs an interconnection step. The algorithm can also run serially. Parallel execution and total arithmetic work are thus different comparisons. The selected computational discussion compares node-voltage solutions and counts multiplications, accounting for sparse matrices so operations on zero entries are omitted. Matrix ordering and fill-in during elimination materially affect the count. [SG-W023, algorithm remarks and §IV, printed pp. 14–16]

Its parameterized example reports one range in which tearing takes fewer multiplications and a smaller range, 3≤p≤9, in which it takes more. Another stated class of networks also gives no computational saving for the chosen tearing arrangement. The explanation for a possible advantage concerns fill-in relative to the comparator, not decomposition as an intrinsically better operation. The positive and adverse cases

nevertheless preserve the source's explicit conclusion that neither approach is absolutely superior on this counted task. [SG-W023, §IV, printed pp. 16–19]

The local solves can become easier while an interconnection solve and ordering effects remain. Wu supplies a concrete comparison in one technical problem, including an unfavorable result.

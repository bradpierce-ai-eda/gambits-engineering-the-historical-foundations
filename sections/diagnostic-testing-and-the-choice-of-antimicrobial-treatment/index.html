Making susceptibility a dependable measurement#

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing connects a laboratory observation to a choice among treatments. William Kirby's 1985 retrospective described why the convenience of placing antibiotic disks on an inoculated plate had initially exceeded the comparability of the results. Laboratories varied the medium, inoculum, disk content and interpretation of the boundary around a disk. A clear zone could therefore look precise while reflecting different procedures. Kirby recalled the work that led to the standardized method published in 1966 and its later collaborative evaluation and adoption. [TD-W010]

A disk creates a concentration gradient as the drug diffuses into the medium. The inhibition zone can be related to the concentration required to inhibit growth, with zone diameter inversely related to the logarithm of the minimum inhibitory concentration. The relationship becomes interpretable when the procedure is controlled. Standardization made the convenient measurement useful across laboratories: the question was no longer merely whether a visible zone appeared, but what a zone obtained under specified conditions indicated about susceptibility. [TD-W010]

From growth measurement to a clinical category#

The 2025 recommendations of the Comité de l'antibiogramme de la Société française de microbiologie show how much of that interpretive structure remains in a modern standard. Medium, inoculum and test conditions are specified so that a measurement can be compared with an applicable breakpoint. A heavier inoculum, for example, can produce a smaller zone. Without control of that feature, an apparent change in susceptibility may instead be a change in how the test was performed. The recommendations join the measurement procedure to the conditions under which its result has meaning. [TD-W011]

The raw diameter or minimum inhibitory concentration is then interpreted for an organism–drug combination. The categories distinguish susceptibility at standard dosage, susceptibility at increased dosage or exposure, and resistance. The French recommendations discuss increased dosage in relation to EUCAST's increased-exposure terminology, which includes how drug reaches the site of infection. A categorical result therefore already incorporates assumptions about achievable exposure. It is not a context-free property of a drug considered in isolation from the organism and treatment conditions. [TD-W011]

Deciding whether to prescribe#

Jochen Cals and colleagues' 2009 trial examined a different decision: whether adults with lower respiratory tract infection should receive an antibiotic in primary care. Twenty practices and forty general practitioners participated, with 431 patients. A factorial design tested point-of-care C-reactive protein measurement and enhanced communication skills as separate interventions. CRP is a marker of the host's inflammatory response. The approximately three-minute measurement supplied evidence for interpreting the presentation; it did not identify an organism or determine which antibiotic would inhibit it. [TD-W012]

The test was introduced with interpretation training and a run-in period. The communication intervention taught eleven tasks addressing such matters as the patient's concerns, expectations and understanding of the illness's likely course. In the consultation, a clinician could establish why the patient had come, discuss the symptoms and test result, explain what recovery might involve, and reach a prescribing decision. The trial consequently tested changes to the use of information as well as the availability of information. [TD-W012]

Antibiotic prescribing at the first consultation was 31% among patients in the CRP-testing comparison and 53% without CRP testing. The corresponding communication comparison was 27% versus 54%. At twenty-eight days, the proportions receiving antibiotics remained lower: 45% versus 58% for CRP and 38% versus 63% for communication. Recovery and satisfaction did not differ detectably between the groups; median recovery took twenty-two days. The combined-intervention cell had particularly low prescribing, but the interaction was not statistically significant, so the study's factorial main effects provide the clearer account of what was established. [TD-W012]

Smedemark and colleagues' 2022 Cochrane review placed biomarker-guided prescribing in a larger primary-care evidence base. Twelve CRP trials included 10,218 participants, of whom 2,335 were children; one procalcitonin trial included 317 participants. CRP-guided care reduced initial prescribing, with a risk ratio of 0.77 and a 95% confidence interval from 0.69 to 0.86. The review rated that evidence moderate certainty and found substantial variation across trials. Prescribing within twenty-eight days was also reduced, with a risk ratio of 0.81, supported by high-certainty evidence. [TD-W013]

Recovery changed little. Deaths were too few to provide a precise assessment of mortality, for which the evidence was low certainty. These outcomes describe different consequences of the intervention: fewer prescriptions, the course of recovery, and uncommon serious events. The review's primary-care procalcitonin evidence should also be distinguished from inpatient studies using repeated procalcitonin measurements to stop treatment already underway. [TD-W013]

Pathogen testing and ordinary prescribing practice#

A rapid streptococcal antigen test gives a more targeted answer than a general inflammatory marker. Anna Maria Bonet-Esteve and colleagues' 2021 report studied its introduction for children aged three to fifteen with suspected streptococcal infection. Twenty-seven pediatricians participated; 557 children were eligible and 519 were followed. Adding the rapid test to the ordinary clinical pathway reduced antibiotic prescribing from 88.5% in the control group to 45.5% in the testing group. All positive tests and relatively few negative tests led to prescriptions. The test altered the treatment decision by distinguishing children within a clinically similar presenting group. [TD-W015]

The investigators also followed adherence by telephone. Among those prescribed antibiotics, adherence was 68% in the control group and 62.9% in the test group, a nonsignificant difference. A test that made prescribing more selective did not thereby establish improved completion of the prescribed course. The two outcomes concern different parts of the treatment process: deciding who receives an antibiotic and what happens after that decision. [TD-W015]

Sydenham and colleagues' 2021 register study examined how testing and prescribing were associated in ordinary general practice. It covered 783,252 antibiotic courses in nine Danish municipalities from 2013 through 2017. Diagnostic tests were associated with 43.4% of courses, using a window extending seven days before and after a prescription. CRP use increased over the period, while overall point-of-care testing remained stable and the mixture of tests changed. The study records practice patterns at scale. Because the association window included tests after prescribing, it does not measure the proportion of prescriptions preceded by a diagnostic test, and its observational design does not by itself identify what testing caused. [TD-W014]

When a rapid panel leaves prescribing unchanged#

Alastair Hay and colleagues' 2026 primary-care trial supplies an informative contrasting result. A rapid molecular panel tested for twenty-three respiratory targets, nineteen viruses and four atypical bacteria, in about forty-five minutes. The study enrolled 552 patients across sixteen practices, with 276 in each group. Participants had to be willing to wait. Clinicians received information about the panel and

presentations, but the intervention did not include the decision and communication training used in the earlier CRP study. Typical respiratory bacteria were outside the panel's target set. [TD-W016]

Antibiotics were prescribed to 124 patients in each group, 45% of both. The odds ratio was 1.00, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.71 to 1.41. Prespecified subgroup analyses showed less prescribing when a virus was found, offset by more prescribing when no target was detected. A negative result on a panel dominated by viral targets did not establish a bacterial cause. The authors discussed how clinicians might interpret that absence of detection, while the randomized overall result remained no reduction in prescribing. Symptom severity met the study's noninferiority criterion. The trial demonstrates why a faster and more detailed result has to be assessed as part of the clinical decision in which it is used. [TD-W016]

Correcting treatment after a positive blood culture#

The RAPIDS-GN trial addressed patients whose blood cultures had already become positive for Gram-negative bacilli. Antibiotic treatment could then need correction: a resistant organism might require escalation, or a susceptible organism might permit a narrower drug. Banerjee and colleagues' report first appeared in 2020. Five hundred patients were randomized; the modified intention-to-treat analysis included 448, with 222 assigned to rapid testing and 226 to standard testing. This was a study of what happened after a positive culture, rather than of withholding initial treatment while waiting for a culture to grow. [TD-W017]

Both groups received antimicrobial stewardship review. A physician or pharmacist was paged when the Gram stain, identification or susceptibility result became available, reviewed the record, and telephoned the treating service when a treatment modification appeared appropriate. Scenario-based recommendations had been developed for the stewardship clinicians. The treating team retained the decision to accept them. In the rapid arm, organism identification and phenotypic susceptibility testing were performed on the rapid platform in addition to ordinary testing, and results entered the medical record. Thus the route from laboratory to medication included a named recipient, review and communication in both groups. [TD-W017]

Median time to identification was 2.7 hours with rapid testing and 11.7 hours with standard testing; susceptibility results took 13.5 and 44.9 hours respectively. The primary outcome, time from randomization to the first antibiotic modification, was 8.6 versus 14.9 hours. For modification of Gram-negative-active antibiotics specifically, the medians were 17.3 versus 42.1 hours. These clinical intervals include more than an instrument's running time. They describe when results became available and when treatment, as recorded in medication administration, actually changed. [TD-W017]

Among patients with resistant organisms, escalation occurred at a median 18.4 hours in the rapid arm and 61.7 hours in the standard arm. The trial did not detect significant differences in clinical outcomes. Its most direct demonstrated achievement was earlier adjustment within this culture-to-review-to-treatment pathway. That finding remains substantial even though faster adjustment and an improvement in mortality are different outcomes. [TD-W017]

The investigators' secondary analysis, first published in 2024, examined 386 patients with monomicrobial, on-panel organisms who had received empiric therapy. It classified treatment as undertreatment, optimal narrow-spectrum treatment or overtreatment at the initial zero-to-two-hour interval and again at twenty-four to twenty-six and forty-eight to fifty-two hours. The clearest impact occurred with resistant Escherichia coli, Klebsiella and Proteus, for which rapid results supported earlier escalation. Broad-spectrum treatment often persisted even when an isolate was susceptible to narrower options. [TD-W018]

For Acinetobacter, Citrobacter, Enterobacter, Serratia and Pseudomonas, the analysis found little effect on treatment, although the groups were smaller. The authors proposed that clinicians might treat escalation as more urgent than narrowing therapy, or hesitate to remove broader coverage under diagnostic uncertainty. Those were explanations they offered for the observed response. The analysis did not review patient-level clinical details to establish every reason for a choice. It identifies differences in the parent trial's treatment trajectories; it was not a separately tested rule for selecting patients prospectively. [TD-W018]

Laboratory speed and patient outcomes#

Anton-Vazquez and colleagues' 2021 Cochrane review examined rapid susceptibility testing in bloodstream infection. Its six randomized trials included 1,638 participants and covered phenotypic and genotypic approaches producing results within eight hours of a positive culture. Individual trials reported earlier treatment changes, but the mortality estimate was imprecise: risk ratio 1.10, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.82 to 1.46, supported by low-certainty evidence. The review did not establish either a mortality reduction or the absence of a clinically meaningful effect. [TD-W019]

The review distinguishes the test's turnaround, time to appropriate treatment, and outcomes such as mortality and discharge. Empiric treatment was already active against the pathogen for many participants, so an earlier result did not always rescue an inactive regimen. Studies also differed in how results reached clinicians and how stewardship was organized. Those differences form part of the intervention being evaluated, rather than merely background to a laboratory comparison. [TD-W019]

Designing the report that reaches the clinician#

西村翔's 2024 review develops susceptibility testing as a contribution to antimicrobial stewardship across specimen collection, analysis and reporting. A resistance marker can answer a useful question without supplying a complete susceptibility profile. This is especially consequential for Gram-negative bacteria with several possible resistance mechanisms. Phenotypic testing and molecular detection therefore provide different information, whose interpretation depends on what was actually measured. [TD-W020]

Selective or cascade reporting changes which tested options routinely appear on the clinical report. A laboratory can make a narrower set of appropriate drugs immediately visible while keeping additional results available on request or under specified circumstances. The policy requires communication with clinicians so that an omitted drug is not mistaken for an unperformed test or an unusable treatment. In the author's hospital account, restrictions on routine fluoroquinolone reporting were discussed, tried and then implemented in July 2018, with exceptions for specified organisms. Use fell by roughly half in the reported before-and-after experience. This was an implementation observation, rather than a randomized estimate of the effect of reporting alone. [TD-W020]

The 2016 expert consensus by 王辉, 宁永忠, 陈宏斌 and colleagues sets out the construction of a bacterial susceptibility report. It begins before the display of drug names: the laboratory must consider whether the isolate is clinically significant and recognize intrinsic resistance. Panels combine standard expectations with local needs and consultation. The consensus's required reporting group is a reporting category, not a declaration that every listed agent is the clinician's first choice. [TD-W021]

The report separates smear observations, culture findings and susceptibility results. Measurements such as minimum inhibitory concentrations or zone diameters are accompanied by interpretation under a stated standard; comments can explain a consequential finding. Urgent partial results should be communicated with their timing clear, and unexpected resistance may require confirmation. These details

let the recipient distinguish a preliminary observation from a completed interpretation and act on important information before every part of the laboratory process is finished. [TD-W021]

Deciding when enough treatment has been given#

The PROGRESS trial, first published in 2020, used repeated procalcitonin measurements to inform stopping antibiotics in sepsis. Of 266 randomized patients, ten withdrew permission to use their data, leaving 125 in the procalcitonin group and 131 receiving standard care. Patients had already begun treatment and were randomized within twenty-four hours. The intervention measured procalcitonin at baseline and reassessed from day five, with daily measurements when the stopping criterion had not been reached. [TD-W023]

The Methods specified stopping when procalcitonin had fallen by at least 80% from baseline or was below 0.5 micrograms per liter, subject to the patient's condition. Instability, including persisting fever or vasopressor requirements, could justify continuing treatment despite the marker. Thirteen patients who reached the biochemical criterion were considered unstable; ninety-six patients complied with the stopping advice. The protocol therefore coupled a repeated measurement with a clinical exception, rather than making a single laboratory value an unconditional instruction. [TD-W023]

Median antibiotic duration was five days with procalcitonin guidance and ten days with standard care. The primary 180-day composite counted new infections by Clostridioides difficile or multidrug-resistant organisms, or death associated with either kind of infection present at baseline. It occurred in nine of 125 and twenty of 131 patients respectively. Twenty-eight-day mortality was also lower, nineteen versus thirty-seven deaths, while the 180-day mortality comparison was not statistically significant. The study involved hospital wards and excluded circumstances requiring prolonged treatment and specified patient groups. Its result concerns a defined stopping strategy after treatment had begun, with its measured outcomes and follow-up periods. [TD-W023]

Organizing a result-to-action pathway#

Chen and colleagues' 2026 narrative review proposes an explicit framework for making rapid results actionable. Its four clocks distinguish analytical turnaround, the wider laboratory interval, the point at which a result becomes clinically actionable, and the delay from result to action. A short assay does not uniquely determine any of the other three. A result may be technically available while the clinician still lacks the interpretation, patient information or response arrangement needed to use it. [TD-W022]

The authors organize actionability around five domains: result type and certainty, patient risk, probability of resistance, capacity to respond, and the quality of stewardship. They propose notification, interpretive comments, patient-specific recommendations, documentation, audit and feedback as connected components. This is their organizing clinical framework, not a validated numerical score. Its practical emphasis is on a result reaching someone who can interpret it, choose an action and record the response. S33 develops earlier accounts of information and decisions; these clinical histories show the particular measurements, reporting forms and treatment pathways through which information was used. [TD-W022]

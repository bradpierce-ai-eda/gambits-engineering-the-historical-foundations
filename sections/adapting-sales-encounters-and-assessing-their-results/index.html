Prepared responses and room to adapt#

Frédéric Buchet begins with a technical engineer entering sales who asks for effective techniques, buyer types and convincing tricks. Buchet separates tricks, reported best practices and organized methods. A reusable reply can keep a novice from being disconcerted, but a recognizable stock answer can also invite distrust. Transferring a star seller’s practice requires attention to whether the example is representative, whether conduct was actually observed, and whether it can be organized into something teachable. [SL-W023]

CNZ organizes objection handling through investigating the objection, reformulating it constructively and then giving a targeted argument. The first two actions change what the answer should address. PLAISIR places contact, expression of needs, clarification, an argued offering, use of opportunities, purchase and reassurance within a longer encounter. A method provides a common vocabulary for instruction and coaching, while leaving a practical question about how much discretion its implementation permits. [SL-W023]

Jolson’s prepared/adaptive continuum, reconstructed in Chaker and colleagues’ review, makes room between a memorized presentation and unrestricted improvisation. A seller can prepare usable modules and combine them for the encounter. Structure supplies a repertoire; adaptation concerns selecting and connecting it in response to the situation. The proposal gives preparation a role in flexibility instead of treating the two as opposites. [SL-W001]

Buchet also distinguishes implementations that give sellers discretion from those that require near-verbatim use. A common method can help a manager and seller discuss the same part of an encounter, but the organization still decides how tightly the conduct must follow it. The method’s teachable structure and the latitude available to its user are therefore separate features of the arrangement he describes. [SL-W023]

Information, strategy, communication and adjustment#

Bigné, Küster and Torán describe adaptive selling through collecting information, formulating a strategy, transmitting a message, evaluating and adjusting it. A response can change within an encounter or across encounters. Their account distinguishes confidence in the ability to adapt from enacted changes in selling behaviour. A salesperson can endorse flexibility without reporting the same conduct as another who actually changes an approach. [SL-W012]

The authors connect this process with market orientation: generating information, sharing it and coordinating a response. Their scale-development work with 119 sellers is distinct from the focal study of thirty sellers in eleven paint and varnish firms. Reported associations with market orientation differ for the broader adaptation measure and a single adaptation item. The distinction makes the organization’s information practices and the particular measure part of interpreting what adaptation means in the study. [SL-W012]

Different meanings of adaptation and different measures of success#

Chaker and colleagues’ review separates adaptive selling understood as behaviour, ability, mindset and trait. Their scholarly corpus contains 188 articles; a separate practitioner review compares 71 resources. The review shows how the broad label can cover different objects and how diverse performance

measures complicate an unconditional claim that adaptation improves results. Its integrative framework is the authors’ proposed organization of that literature. [SL-W001]

McGowan’s review develops requirements that recur across selling approaches: qualification, listening, needs, product knowledge, adaptation, relationships and digital capabilities. Listening includes several forms of communication, and relevant networks extend within both buyer and seller firms. These connections make failure a concern of preparation and organizational access as well as individual conversation. The review of 104 articles identifies common requirements rather than establishing trial-based winners among branded systems. [SL-W011]

Verbeke, Dietz and Verwaal’s meta-analysis brings together 268 studies, 292 samples, 79,747 sellers and 4,317 firms over 1982–2008. Knowledge and adaptability are positively associated with performance, while role ambiguity is negatively associated. The interpretation depends partly on whether performance is self-rated, manager-rated or measured objectively. Their knowledge-broker interpretation concerns sellers’ use of knowledge across relationships. Pooled model coefficients describe associations in that analysis, rather than percentages of revenue obtained by adopting a technique. [SL-W018]

The Sales Agility Code, as reconstructed in Chaker and colleagues’ practitioner review, organizes action as assessing the situation, choosing a path and executing while monitoring impact and changing circumstances. Vazzana and Sottosanti Doyle name consultative, disruptive, competitive and financial strategies. The account concerns this repertoire and the choice among its paths; proprietary performance descriptions remain claims reported by the review. [SL-W001]

A verbal prompt at the point of ordering#

A field experiment at two Austrian fast-food branches examined a much smaller intervention: a suggestion when a customer had ordered a main dish but no side. The procedure first required attention to the order already given. Only an eligible transaction received the assigned prompt. Successive groups of ten transactions rotated among no prompt, a potato-salad suggestion, a fries suggestion and a salad-or-fries choice. The intervention was therefore a particular utterance at a particular point in ordering, rather than a general instruction to be more persuasive. [SL-W026]

Across 2,160 transactions, side-dish purchases were approximately 61 percent under the control condition and 78 percent across the pooled prompting conditions. The forced-choice version did not significantly outperform the simpler suggestions. An added verbal device could thus fail to improve on a useful prompt. The measured result was purchase of a side dish; the authors’ discussion of fit with the main dish and unfamiliarity concerns interpretation of those choices. [SL-W026]

Suggestive selling within a meal#

Lagueux and Rajaobelina place suggestive selling within a meal and service encounter. Menu information, the occasion and the diner’s involvement affect what may be pertinent. The encounter can involve several diners rather than a single buyer facing a single seller. Companions’ choices and experiences make the situation polyadic: one suggestion can enter a group conversation and influence more than its immediate recipient. [SL-W014] A business meal and a social occasion may permit different amounts of interaction with a server, while an informative menu can answer questions before the server intervenes. [SL-W014]

Their conceptual model also includes the server’s circumstances and workload. A relevant suggestion depends on an opportunity to notice, interpret and address what is happening during service. Bill size and customer satisfaction remain different proposed consequences. The model develops possible relations around the activity, complementing the narrower transaction outcome examined in the prompt experiment. [SL-W014]

Cross-selling and differences among retail settings#

Vieira, Pires and Galeano studied cross-selling in three settings: 49 clothing sellers, 57 pharmacy staff across thirteen stores and 27 wholesale respondents. Their measures include beliefs about training, selling conduct, organizational feedback and self-reported firm performance. Co-creation here concerns whether a firm listens and acts on seller feedback, giving frontline information a place in the organization’s response. [SL-W013]

An item about directing buyers toward what the seller thinks best has a negative association in clothing and a positive one in pharmacy. Those different results concern the sampled settings and measured constructs. The authors’ cold-remedy-and-tissues illustration shows what an additional related purchase might look like; the study’s performance measures concern selling and the firm. Beliefs about training likewise differ from an experiment that assigns training and measures its effects. [SL-W013] Understanding needs is positively associated with cross-selling in both the clothing and pharmacy accounts. The authors interpret the contrasting selling postures through their different settings, alongside the contribution of management’s response to frontline information. [SL-W013]

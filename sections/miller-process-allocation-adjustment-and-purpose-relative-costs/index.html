Processes, structures and adjustment#

James Grier Miller's living systems framework connects material and informational flows with structures, functions, regulation and costs. Miller asks how allocating processes to structures changes dependencies, communication, control, vulnerability and the effectiveness of adjustment.

Miller develops these concepts in the Basic Concepts excerpt from Living Systems and in his 1970 paper The Nature of Living Systems. Later author accounts and scholarly reconstructions develop and assess the framework. [RGC-W004; WME-W004; SG-W008]

Matter-energy, markers and meaning#

Miller treats living systems as concrete organizations existing in physical space and time. They process matter-energy and information, but information travels through physical markers. A message's pattern, its material carrier and its meaning to a recipient are related aspects, rather than one interchangeable quantity. Communication requires some physical realization and a system capable of receiving and interpreting it. [SG-W008, printed 1–9; WME-W004, §§1–4]

The distinction prevents a particular omission. A brief signal can influence a large activity without supplying all its energy, but the receiver still requires energy and the relevant capability. Information processing also involves channels, transformations, memory and other activities. Describing a message in informational terms does not remove those processes from the system's resource requirements. [SG-W008, 6–9; WME-W004, §§3–4]

Miller expressly separates Shannon information from meaning. Measures defined over alternatives and probabilities do not automatically measure significance, usefulness or agreement. Miller also connects information with negative entropy and develops speculative optimizing extensions. [WME-W004, §§3, 13]

Formal identities also require compatible dimensions, units or specified transformations. A curve drawn in one coordinate system can look different after a change of scale, while superficially similar curves can concern different relations. Miller's cross-level ambition therefore needs more than a visual resemblance. An asserted identity requires the measurement relation as well as a shared name for the process. [SG-W008, 23–25; WME-W004, §§1–4, 8]

Structure, process and history differ#

Structure describes spatial organization at a selected instant; process describes changes in matter-energy or information through time. History includes changes in the structure and functioning of the system. Its explanatory question includes how the organization changes, not only how flows pass through an unchanged diagram. [WME-W004, §§5–8]

The distinction between a component and a subsystem is equally important. A component is an identifiable structural unit. A subsystem is identified by the process it carries out and can span several components. One component can perform several processes, and several components can jointly perform one. The mapping between structures and functions is many-to-many. Counting departments, persons or physical parts is therefore different from identifying the activities they perform. [WME-W004, §11]

The discussion of organizational roles makes this concrete. A role specifies demands and activities, but the characteristics and needs of the person filling it also affect what happens. Adjustments can involve compromises between the member and the larger system. A formal position does not by itself establish

who actually leads or decides. Miller's distinction asks the investigator to examine the process rather than infer it directly from a title or shape. [WME-W004, §11]

He also distinguishes a system level from a decision echelon. Levels concern the organization of cells, organs, organisms, groups and further forms. Echelons are steps within a decider's organization, at which different decisions may be made. They need not coincide with levels, and some deciders are not arranged in echelons. [WME-W004, §§8–9]

Cross-level comparison requires both identities and “disidentities.” Similar functions can coexist with differences in mobility, duration, transferability of processes, boundaries and other characteristics. Miller's programme seeks quantitative similarities and relevant differences together. [WME-W004, §8.2]

Allocating a function changes dependencies#

Miller's allocation patterns include localization in one component, combination with other processes, lateral distribution across components, upward or downward provision within a hierarchy, and outward provision by other systems. The allocation determines which parts depend on which other parts. Two systems can carry out similar functions while distributing the work differently. That difference belongs in an explanation of their performance. [SG-W008, 28–34; WME-W004, §11]

The framework explicitly proposes studying effectiveness and costs under alternative allocations. Distance over which material or messages must move, the number of participating components, difficulties of integration and duplicated capabilities can all matter. These are presented as candidate general principles and research questions. [WME-W004, §11]

Redundancy supplies an important countervailing consideration. A duplicate component capable of the same process can maintain function when another is disabled. Whether that possibility improves survival or just adds an unnecessary cost depends on the failures, capacities and other conditions. Miller offers a proposed relationship between allocation and vulnerability. [WME-W004, §11]

Outward provision is especially relevant. A function absent inside a system may still be carried out by a provider with which it exchanges resources or services. The absence of internal production does not mean the life function is unnecessary. It creates a relation to another system, potentially with exchange costs, asymmetric dependence or parasitism. [WME-W004, §11 and dispersion discussion]

The framework also describes differentiation or “shred-out,” in which increasingly specialized components take on processes previously combined. It supplies a way to ask how specialization changes structure without assuming that the function itself has newly come into existence. More differentiated parts can still depend on communication and integration. [WME-W004, §§8, 11]

Critical processes and the decider condition#

The earlier architecture names nineteen critical subsystems. They include the boundary and reproducer, matter-energy ingestion, conversion, production, distribution, storage and output, and information transduction, channels, decoding, association, memory, deciding, encoding and output. The list identifies required kinds of activity in the framework. [SG-W008, subsystem table; WME-W004, §11]

A system can lack an internal instance of a critical process if another system effectively supplies it. Miller makes an exception for deciding: an independently existing living system must retain its own decider. If that function is wholly transferred upward, downward or outward, he treats the focal unit's independent system identity differently. This is a substantive condition of his theory. [WME-W004, §11.8]

An artifact can participate in a critical function without itself becoming a living system. Living and nonliving components can be included in a larger organization. Thus the capability to store, filter or

transmit something is not sufficient on its own to establish living-system status. The decider requirement and living-system scope limit transfer to passive devices or decentralized formal arrangements that are described under different definitions. [WME-W004, §11]

This distinction also shows where an organizational decision remains. Miller permits variations in the internal distribution of deciding but requires an account of that process. Describing an organization as decentralized does not answer who settles conflicts, establishes comparison values or changes the allocation. Those activities must be located rather than assumed absent. [WME-W004, §§9, 11, 13]

Adjustment can change more than the immediate response#

Adjustment maintains relevant variables within a range despite stress. Negative feedback uses information about actual performance to compare an outcome with a desired condition and alter activity. Timing matters: feedback arriving after the effective opportunity for correction can fail its purpose. A label saying “feedback” does not supply adequate speed, accuracy or action capacity. [SG-W008, 41–44; WME-W004, §13]

Miller's adjustment repertoire also includes learning or reorganization, changing the environment, withdrawal and changing targets. Not every difficulty is answered by exerting more force within an unchanged structure. A system can alter its relations or the condition it seeks. The range of alternatives makes its selected purpose and boundaries essential to evaluating the result. [WME-W004, §§12–13]

Equifinality is correspondingly bounded. Different starting conditions or routes may lead to a similar condition, but only within relevant ranges and with essential inputs available. The text explicitly notes histories and conditions that alter or destroy an organization. [WME-W004, §13.2.4]

Costs are tied to purposes and time#

Miller's cost discussion is unusually direct. Adjustment uses material resources, energy, information and time; social systems also use money, which he discusses as information conveyed through markers. Any of these can be scarce. Costs differ between processes and can be immediate or delayed, short-term or long-term. The framework therefore explicitly reaches beyond the visible effort of the current act. [SG-W008, 45–46, §13.2.4; WME-W004, §13.2.5]

Efficiency is defined in relation to success at a known purpose and the costs involved. An operation can be active without being useful in the larger system. One must know what it is expected to achieve before judging whether its resource use is effective. Miller uses programme budgeting to illustrate separate accounts for the components or programmes carrying out distinct activities. [WME-W004, §§13.2.4– 13.2.5]

Different performances and resource dimensions can require different comparisons. Miller's claim that economic decisions improve performance and decrease costs expresses the account's optimizing orientation. [SG-W008, 45–46; WME-W004, §13.2.5]

Information itself has a place on both sides of the decision. Relevant information can improve selection among adjustments, but obtaining it costs resources. Reliability, distortion, accuracy, whether the inquiry concerns an act or a policy, and how it is acquired affect its value. The account thus includes information acquisition as work. More messages or more measurement are not automatically an improvement if they do not help the selected performance enough to justify their cost. [WME-W004, §13.2.5]

These passages connect allocation, dependence, regulation, information and performance within Miller's own account.

Hammond and Wilby: a substantive reconstruction with objections#

Hammond and Wilby's 2006 article reconstructs Miller's effort to connect behavioral science, cybernetics and an organismic hierarchy through material and informational processes. It traces his research institutions and quantitative ambitions, while examining the implications of his categories. The article is adapted from a chapter of Hammond's 2003 book. [RGC-W005, 429–435]

The decider is a central issue in their account. They describe its role in forming intentions, coordinating activity and allocating power, and ask how that model works as the levels become socially more complex. The existence of a category called deciding does not by itself settle the relation between individual purposes and collective control. They preserve the tension between integrating members and supporting their needs. [RGC-W005, living-systems section]

They also report Boulding's criticism: an essentially physiological template can be too elaborate for lower levels and insufficiently complex for higher ones. This criticism is mediated through Hammond and Wilby. It challenges the adequacy of the cross-level template while leaving open whether particular relations or questions are useful. Their discussion of measurement limits is likewise a critical interpretation. [RGC-W005, concluding living-systems discussion and reflections]

Ruscoe: a schema does not supply its own instruments#

Gordon Ruscoe's response to Miller's editorial shares the aspiration to inquiry beyond artificial disciplinary boundaries but questions the proposed strategy. He distinguishes a conceptual system, an empirical theory and a research paradigm. A classificatory arrangement can contain useful concepts without itself supplying empirically testable statements and the instruments needed to examine them. [WME-W005, printed 11–12]

His operative objection is the chain from process to variable to indicator. The investigator needs reasons for selecting a variable, connecting it to the process and deciding that an indicator measures it. Those inferential steps require methodological justification beyond placing the study within a living-systems box. A formally similar label is insufficient to establish a valid cross-level measurement. [WME-W005, 12]

Ruscoe also distinguishes generating a fruitful empirical question from retrospectively fitting completed research into a schema. Both can be useful, but the second does not prove the first. His letter is a methodological challenge to the warrants for the research programme. [WME-W005, 11–12 and editorial note]

Later applications and the status of their evidence#

Miller and Miller's Applications of Living Systems Theory (1992) reports that the 1978 book contained 173 cross-level hypotheses and discusses studies of overload, communication networks and conflicting commands. Some model applications are explicitly described as compatible with the framework rather than derived from it. Completed and proposed studies remain distinguishable. [RGC-W006]

The overload account reports measurements at several levels, from neural preparations to individuals, small groups and laboratory organizations. It proposes a response in which increasing input initially increases output, then exceeds capacity and can produce deterioration. The later account also stresses compatible units and dimensions for a formal cross-level comparison. [RGC-W006, basic-research section]

The conflicting-command game offers a more specific organizational example. Individuals or groups receive instructions from several nominal superiors, and the account reports slower deciding as commands differ. That result belongs to the reported experimental setting. The biased-network study

similarly concerns specified communication probabilities and reported predictions. [RGC-W006, cross-level tests]

The organizational application describes a three-year study of forty-one Army battalions with more than five thousand participants. It reports relationships between information-processing characteristics and effectiveness, including meaning, lag, volume, cost and distortion. The same account initially lists objective process data but later says only subjective data were collected in that study. [RGC-W006, Army and industrial-project sections]

The later proposed industrial study includes objective measures, computerized responses, voluntary location badges and expert-system analysis. These are plans for a study, not results. [RGC-W006]

The Swanson–Miller EOLSS chapter introduces a later eight-level, twenty-subsystem form. It distinguishes conceptual and concrete systems and retains limitations on measuring meaning. This differs from the earlier seven-level, nineteen-subsystem architecture. [RGC-W007]

Swanson's 2007 proceedings abstract for Living Systems Theory and Entity-Systems Theory raises questions about dispersed subsystems, conceptual systems and emergence. [RGC-W008, abstract]

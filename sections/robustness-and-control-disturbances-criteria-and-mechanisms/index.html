The scope of a favorable response#

The accounts in this section ask different questions about maintaining or recovering performance. Some characterize exact adaptation in a biochemical model, some optimize protection against declared uncertainty, and others reduce controller updates or describe organized physiological responses.

Yi and colleagues: integral feedback in a chemotaxis model#

Tau-Mu Yi, Yun Huang, Melvin Simon and John Doyle reconstruct the robust perfect adaptation of the Barkai–Leibler bacterial chemotaxis model through integral feedback. The pertinent variable is approximately total receptor methylation. Its rate of change can be expressed as a methylation contribution minus a term proportional to receptor-complex activity. At a stable steady state that rate is zero, so the activity returns to a value set by the ratio of those rates. If the relevant rates remain independent of ligand level, a changed ligand input does not change that steady-state activity. This is the selected structural reason for adaptation in their model. [W012, pp. 4650–4651]

Integral feedback is more specific than feedback in general. The accumulated error supplies a state that continues to influence the regulated process. A constant present error and a controller whose state retains accumulated history are different situations. The authors' description explicitly requires stability of the closed loop; an algebraic steady-state relation alone does not establish that the dynamics actually approach it. [W012, integral-control derivation and fig. 2]

They also identify four biochemical assumptions needed for the reconstruction: the demethylating action concerns active receptors; specified kinetic parameters and rate ratios are independent of the relevant methylation/occupancy distinctions; activity of the unmethylated form is negligible; and bound CheR does not depend on ligand level. Relaxing these assumptions can spoil exact adaptation. The authors inspect biological evidence for their relevance rather than hiding them behind the word robust. [W012, pp. 4650–4651, assumptions and discussion]

Yi and colleagues reconstruct the model introduced by Barkai and Leibler in Robustness in simple biochemical networks. [W033, through W012]

Cannon: constancy can require ongoing cooperation#

Walter Cannon's 1929 Organization for Physiological Homeostasis defines homeostasis for an open living system whose automatic adjustments keep disturbances of internal conditions within relatively narrow limits. He deliberately distinguishes this from using equilibrium in the simple closed physicochemical sense. His choice of “homeo” also permits variation rather than rigid identity. The selected explanation concerns coordinated physiological reactions, including organs and material supplies, rather than an immobile substance that stays correct without activity. [W062, pp. 399–404]

That distinction makes the apparent absence of change informative only when its means are understood. The selected postulates describe agencies acting or ready to act, stronger resistance to a disturbance, and simultaneous or successive cooperation among factors. Cannon calls these propositions tentative and conditional, acknowledges pathological failure and substantial gaps in knowledge, and distinguishes their status from a strict equilibrium principle. His expectation that further governing agencies will be found is a research position, not proof that every instance has already been mechanistically explained. [W062, pp. 424–426]

Sterling and Ramsay/Woods: anticipation does not settle the terminology#

Peter Sterling's allostasis account challenges a version of homeostasis centered on preserving a constant internal milieu through feedback error correction. He argues that regulation instead adjusts conditions to support survival and reproduction, using prediction and coordinated effectors. In the selected description, prior knowledge and current sensing help anticipate required resources; experience updates what can serve as prior knowledge later. Anticipation therefore retains acquired information, sensory input and action. [GL-W028, selected abstract and predictive-coordination discussion]

Sterling's broader article connects that argument to efficiency, behavior and proposed medical implications. These are his model and proposals. A response need not wait for a large harmful departure if relevant demands can be anticipated and effectors prepared. The adequacy of those predictions remains consequential.

Douglas Ramsay and Stephen Woods dispute the idea that anticipation alone requires abandoning homeostasis. Their selected 2014 discussion argues that contemporary homeostatic models can already accommodate anticipatory responses and errors of prediction. They also criticize using homeostasis for virtually any return of any variable to a former value, because that stretching can obscure its physiological scope. The disagreement is about concepts and mechanisms, rather than a simple contest between a modern predictive model and a uniformly static predecessor. [GL-W029, introduction and Scope of Homeostasis]

Their suggested distinction turns attention to underlying effector loops. They discuss persistent overcorrection, continuing effector activity after an initiating disturbance is gone, and competition among effectors as candidates for an allostatic explanation. They explicitly call their suggestions speculative and open to revision. Observing a changed temperature or other regulated value alone is therefore insufficient to decide which account applies. [GL-W029, Scope of Homeostasis and conclusions]

Carlson and Doyle: optimization can expose an unanticipated fragility#

Carlson and Doyle's highly optimized tolerance account uses a percolation template to distinguish structured design from random configurations near criticality. Occupied clusters are separated by vacant barriers; a spark damages the cluster it reaches. Optimizing expected yield requires a distribution of spark locations and a set of constraints. In the selected example, more protective cuts are placed where hits are more probable, producing smaller clusters there and larger clusters in less frequently hit regions. A high-performing arrangement is specialized to the risks it was designed to face. [W026, preprint pp. 1– 3]

This specialization explains a vulnerability as well as a benefit. Change the hit distribution or place a defect in a barrier and damage can spread differently. The source explicitly considers a tree occupying a location that should be vacant in a separating barrier: a small structural defect lets fire pass into neighboring regions. Adding protection against such flaws or multiple sparks can be possible at a cost in yield. The favorable performance and the sensitivity to what was omitted are consequences of the same specified design comparison. [W026, p. 3]

The authors emphatically say the simple model is not a realistic representation of a particular system and is only remotely related to forest management. Their broader engineering/biological comparisons are their interpretive extension. The model therefore offers a concrete mechanism and a caution about inference from a power-law distribution. Self-organized criticality and optimized tolerance remain different explanations in the source's comparison. [W026, pp. 3–4]

Bertsimas and Sim: robustness is chosen against a stated uncertainty class#

Dimitris Bertsimas and Melvyn Sim's robust discrete-optimization framework introduces a tunable degree of conservatism. Instead of requiring an exact distribution for every uncertain coefficient, it uses nominal values, permitted deviations and a declared uncertainty structure. Their selected mixed-integer formulation treats uncertain constraint coefficients as bounded, symmetric and independent, while uncertain costs have a specified range. Unknown distributional detail consequently does not mean all assumptions about uncertainty have been removed. [W060, §§1–2.1]

Protection parameters control how conservative a solution is relative to the uncertainty being represented. A stronger protection requirement can change the feasible choices or objective value; choosing the budget is part of the modeled decision. The article gives tractability results for specified problem classes and transformations. Its relevance is the explicit separation of information that can be dispensed with from bounds, structure and computation that remain. [W060, selected robust-formulation discussion]

Tabuada: an event trigger still has to be detected#

Paulo Tabuada's 2007 control-task paper studies execution of a stabilizing control task when plant state makes another update necessary, instead of at a fixed periodic rate. Between updates, an input is held constant; the difference between the last sampled state and current state grows into a measurement error. A trigger limits that error relative to a state-dependent bound so that an appropriate Lyapunov function continues to decrease. The draft separately accounts for time to sense, compute and update actuators: with nonzero delay, execution must start early enough to keep the required bound. [TA-W013, draft PDF pp. 3–4]

Its positive lower bound on inter-execution times is conditional on input-to-state stability with respect to measurement errors and specified Lipschitz/Lyapunov assumptions, for initial states in the stated compact set and sufficiently small implementation delay. Those conditions make the schedule feasible within the model rather than allowing update times to accumulate arbitrarily closely. The discussion of co-schedulability then retains other processor tasks and scheduling assumptions; fewer control executions are useful only in an arrangement that still meets those obligations. [TA-W013, theorem 3.1 and co-scheduling discussion]

The practical discussion is especially revealing. A trigger based on a difference between current and previous control-law values could require repeatedly computing the very control law whose executions one wants to avoid. The measurement-error version instead allows a simpler comparison that can be implemented in separate hardware. The activity is reduced or relocated on a specified resource account; monitoring has not vanished. [TA-W013, §7.2]

Anta and Tabuada: compute the next occasion from current information#

Adolfo Anta and Tabuada's 2008 self-triggered account addresses the continuous-monitoring demand of event triggering. When the current state is measured or estimated for a control update, that information and the model can also be used to determine when the controller should execute again. The next occasion is thus computed at the current one. The selected introduction frames this as a trade between resources allotted to control and achieved performance, including processor and communication demands. [TA-W014, introduction]

Their developed conditions concern state-dependent homogeneous and polynomial systems, with the relevant dynamics, desired performance and current measurement explicitly involved. Continuous trigger

tests may be avoided while predictor/model computation and a correct representation of the operating conditions remain. [TA-W014, introduction and §§2–3]

Self-triggering predictive control and feasibility#

Patent CN116627039B presents a self-triggering predictive-control proposal by Wang Mengzhi, Zhao Chengcheng, Yang Zeyu and Che Xin. Its abstract constructs a continuous-time networked model, solves a finite-horizon optimization, constructs a candidate feasible sequence at a later time and uses a cost-function deviation to select an update occasion. This gives the claimed reduction in updates a substantial computational setting: optimization and feasibility reasoning are part of the proposal, rather than work removed by the label self-triggering. [TA-W039, abstract and claims]

The patent record dates the application to 2023 and publication to 2026. Reduced computation is a proposed effect of the control scheme, rather than a measured deployment result.

Shraim: reacting to each fluctuation can add variation#

Mustafa Shraim's author report of an educational catapult exercise distinguishes reacting to ordinary variation as though every observation signals a new special cause from failing to investigate a genuine special cause. Its three scenarios run the process without adjustment, ask volunteers to adjust toward a target, and then repeat after collaborative improvements to the operating method. The report says informal repeated adjustment produced the most variation, while standardizing how the catapult was held and launched reduced it. A slip-of-hand observation in the first stage was retained and identified, rather than erased as ordinary noise. [W095, 2019 author report]

The example concerns the response to the type of variation and a changed work method. Its relevance is the bounded observation that more corrective activity can worsen a process when its basis is wrong, while deliberate work on the process can improve it.

Doyle and Csete: constraints that permit flexibility#

John Doyle and Marie Csete explicitly connect engineering, biology and neuroscience through architecture. In the selected clothing example, component constraints, outfit requirements and protocols jointly determine which combinations are useful. A wardrobe can permit many interchangeable outfits while excluding an even larger set of arbitrary heaps. The common restrictions enable a kind of flexibility by making components compatible. [UX-W042, Architecture as Constraints That Deconstrain]

They also distinguish minimal function under ideal conditions from robustness to broader conditions. Paper can be made into a garment, but that does not give it the endurance and protection of a layered textile architecture. Their discussion retains hidden technologies, layered relationships and control over different time scales. Even familiar outfit protocols do not automatically guarantee fit or suitability: people still try things on and choose for weather and activity. [UX-W042, Layered Architectures Simplified and following discussion]

A word, a sentence, an image or a way of attending can become material for deliberate transformation. The practices examined here include changing perception, suspending familiar control during writing, substituting words through a rule, following associations and assembling images taken from different settings. Their artistic purposes give the operations a particular direction. What is being generated may be a text, an image, a new relation to familiar material or a starting point for further work.

Rimbaud and the cultivation of perception#

Arthur Rimbaud's letter to Paul Demeny, written on 15 May 1871, places the transformation of the poet within the work of poetry. He calls for knowing and cultivating the self and for a sustained, reasoned disordering of the senses. The ambition is to reach what has not yet been apprehended and find a language capable of expressing it. The letter addresses a way of becoming a poet as well as the making of individual poems. [CRE-W014]

The programme joins experience to form. If perception reaches something unfamiliar, inherited ways of writing may fail to express it. Rimbaud's demand for new forms follows from that ambition. The poet is not simply asked to vary the surface of conventional verse while leaving the conditions of perception untouched. The creator's habits of sensing, interpreting and speaking become part of what is to be changed. [CRE-W014]

The letter also imagines a future of poetic work beyond its present participants and conventions, including women's future place as poets. Its rhetoric is expansive and extreme, and its practical object is an artistic life and language. The text's historical force lies partly in making those capacities a matter of deliberate cultivation. Written in 1871, the letter first appeared complete in print in 1912. [CRE-W014]

Breton and the procedure of automatic writing#

André Breton's 1924 Manifesto of Surrealism includes explicit instructions for automatic writing. The writer first obtains materials and chooses a setting favorable to concentration. Breton asks for a receptive state, a departure from judgments about one's own or other people's talent, and rapid writing without a preconceived subject. The pace is intended to keep the writer from holding back and rereading what has just been written. [CRE-W015, “Composition surréaliste écrite”]

The sequence changes when ordinary control begins to return. If the writing falls silent or its continuation becomes too clear, Breton recommends breaking the line of development. After a word whose origin seems suspect, the writer can impose a fixed letter as the beginning of the next word. The arbitrary letter interferes with the expected continuation. A procedure intended to allow a flow therefore includes an intervention for restarting it. [CRE-W015, “Composition surréaliste écrite”]

Breton's account is concerned with the relationship between conscious supervision and expression. Preparing the place and materials, writing quickly, suspending rereading and disrupting an overly controlled continuation belong to one exercise. Removing immediate judgment does not mean that no conditions have been established. The conditions are directed toward changing what can emerge before the familiar act of approval or correction takes over. [CRE-W015]

The manifesto gives this practice its surrealist purpose and explanation. Breton treats the activity as a means of encountering thought and expression that ordinary literary monitoring can inhibit. The instructions make that claim available through an actual way of working. They also show how a writer can detect a change in the exercise and alter the procedure in response. [CRE-W015]

Lescure and a rule that produces an unexpected text#

Jean Lescure presented the S+7 procedure at an Oulipo meeting on 13 February 1961. It begins with an existing text and a chosen dictionary. Each noun is replaced by the seventh noun following it in that dictionary. The source supplies much of the grammatical frame, while the dictionary supplies the replacement vocabulary. The writer does not choose each new noun because it seems the best expression of an intended message. [CRE-W016]

The Oulipo account demonstrates the operation through a transformation of Baudelaire's L'Étranger into L'Étreinte. The question-and-answer pattern remains recognizable, but the successive nouns disturb what a reader expects the exchange to concern. The result invites another reading of an inherited form. Both the starting text and the dictionary matter: changing either changes what the counting rule encounters. [CRE-W016, example]

Once those inputs and the rule are fixed, the substitutions are determined. Their effect can be surprising without arising from a random draw. Creative decisions have partly moved to the choice of material and operation. The produced wording then becomes something to read, interpret and potentially develop. Cage's chance-selection procedures in S66 place the source of variation differently. [CRE-W016]

The extensions show the procedure itself becoming material for invention. Raymond Queneau applies related substitutions to adjectives, verbs and other grammatical categories. Another rule admits a replacement only if metrical or rhyme requirements are met. A simple instruction can thus be combined with further constraints that govern which candidate is accepted. [CRE-W016, Extensions]

Other variants alter the displacement number, change the kind of dictionary or construct reciprocal texts. In Jacques Jouet's example, one part is the S−7 of the other, while the other is its S+7. The two parts share a syntactic organization but exchange related vocabulary through the rule. The account develops a family of operations with specified inputs and relations, rather than an instruction to be novel in general. [CRE-W016, Extensions]

Rodari and the word that sets other things moving#

Gianni Rodari opens Grammar of Fantasy, published in 1973, with the image of a stone entering a pond. It produces movements at several depths, disturbing things that had seemed separate. He uses the image to describe what can happen when a word enters the mind. Sounds, meanings, memories, images and earlier experiences become active together. The mind also intervenes, connecting, accepting and rejecting what arises. [CRE-W017, chapters 1–2]

His example begins with sasso, a stone. One path follows initial sounds; another follows rhyme; another follows meanings associated with stone and other materials. These are different ways to continue from the same word. Personal memory supplies another path. Rodari recalls a place, a friend, conversations and musical instruments. A word's usefulness as a starting point can therefore depend on the person who encounters it and the experiences available to that person. [CRE-W017, chapter 2]

A later association joins a brick to a song. Rodari develops the conjunction into a musical house whose parts can be sounded, then into a musical village. The image is not complete when the first unexpected pair appears. It can be expanded by asking what follows from the relation and letting remembered music, social experience and further images change the development. Rodari's account follows that movement closely enough to show a story becoming possible. [CRE-W017, chapter 2]

He then changes the form of the starting material. The letters S A S S O are written vertically, each supplying the initial of another word. One result suggests seven geese on a swing. A further attempt produces seven hundred lawyers playing seven hundred ocarinas. The second attempt develops material

already active in the exercise: seven becomes seven hundred, and geese helps prompt ocarinas. The formal positions remain the same while the material continues to accumulate associations. [CRE-W017, chapter 2]

The chapter supplies a repeatable operation without expecting the first output to be valuable. An initial word can be followed through sound and memory, split into letters, rebuilt as a sentence and developed into an image or story. Rodari distinguishes the educational purpose of such activity from using an imaginative task solely to rank children. His exercise is intended to make the capacities available to the participants themselves. [CRE-W017, chapter 2]

Álvarez Rodríguez and the writing workshop#

Maritza Isabel Álvarez Rodríguez describes adapting Rodari's techniques for a university course in children's literature. Students are asked to produce texts through the techniques, rather than encounter them only as a theoretical topic. The workshop supplies prompts, a coordinator and other participants. The resulting activity joins the beginning of a text to reading, discussion and further work on what has been written. [CRE-W018]

Her account stresses an experimental relationship to language. Students attend to sound, images, oral expression and alternative ways of reading everyday experience. The prompt initiates activity, but revision and rewriting also belong to the workshop. The coordinator helps participants sustain writing and makes it possible to discuss the material they produce. [CRE-W018, characteristics of creative writing and workshop adaptation]

The article reports the course experience and argues that the arrangement helped students approach writing with less fear and develop expression. Its practical contribution includes this passage from a named technique to a teaching situation. Reading, speaking, generating and revising are organized around the production of texts for children. S70 develops other accounts of how creative methods are taught and assessed. [CRE-W018, conclusions and recommendations]

Pérez Jiménez and the operations of image making#

Mauricio Pérez Jiménez distinguishes the material equipment of image production from the rules, decisions and intentions through which it is used. A camera or digital tool supplies possibilities, but a procedure determines what is done with them. Acquiring a technique also changes ways of seeing: isolating a detail, framing a subject, attending to a relation or imagining a transformation. [CRE-W019, pp. 72–75]

His discussion develops operations such as changing apparent size, combining events separated in time or space, reversing movement and altering its speed. A small detail can be emphasized until it stands for a larger event. Something motionless can be given movement, or an action can be slowed enough to invite a different perception of it. The artistic choice concerns both what is represented and how the technical operation transforms it. [CRE-W019, pp. 72–75]

The distinction between equipment and operation also makes room for purposes and attitudes. Identical equipment can support different work because the maker selects, combines and interprets differently. Pérez Jiménez explains the image as the result of a sequence involving an idea, material means and a developing representation. His account gives technically mediated creation an operative vocabulary rather than treating a new device as a creative method by itself. [CRE-W019, pp. 72–75]

Adamowicz and the selection and assembly of images#

Elza Adamowicz's Surrealist Collage in Text and Image begins with the 1921 Paris exhibition of Max Ernst's work. Her historical account identifies materials taken from encyclopaedic plates, anatomical treatises, engineering diagrams, commercial catalogues and advertisements. Printed elements and photographs are cut and joined in new combinations. Words and inscriptions can participate in the work as well. [CRE-W046, pp. 1–3]

These operations move material out of the contexts in which it ordinarily has a recognizable use. A technical illustration can acquire a poetic role through its placement beside another image. Selection, combination and accompanying language alter what the familiar element can suggest. The material remains recognizable enough for its new relationship to have force. [CRE-W046, pp. 2–4]

Adamowicz examines the interpretations offered by Breton and Louis Aragon. Breton emphasizes the encounter of disparate realities and the disorientation it can produce. Aragon gives a different emphasis to the diversion of an image into a new role and to the relationship between literal and figurative meanings. Their interpretations are part of the history of what artists understood collage to make possible. [CRE-W046, pp. 3–5]

She also places collage beside automatism. Deliberate cutting and assembly and the flowing production of automatic writing become two significant modes in the development of surrealist practice. Their relationship does not erase the difference between their operations. Adamowicz gives both material process and intellectual interpretation enough detail to explain why joining fragments can concern a transformation of meaning, not merely an altered surface. [CRE-W046, pp. 4–5]

The sources in this section work on perception, words, images and the conditions of expression. Their procedures can be followed through particular changes to material and activity. The related performing practices in S66 extend the account to bodies, partners, scores and rehearsals.

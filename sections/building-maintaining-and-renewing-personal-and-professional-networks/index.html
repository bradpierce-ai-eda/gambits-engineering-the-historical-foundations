S97 Building, maintaining and renewing personal and professional networks #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

A relationship can provide companionship, belonging, advice or practical assistance. Its development involves encounters and communication over time, while changes of workplace, residence or activity can alter the opportunities for contact. The accounts here examine how people form relationships, sustain them and reconnect after an interval. Friendship and mutual support are purposes in their own right alongside professional access and advancement.

Encounters and the development of a friendship#

Claire Bidart describes sociability through repeated encounters that acquire a shared history. As a relationship develops, its participants cease to be interchangeable: knowing this person involves a history that another person does not simply replace. Her discussion distinguishes a network centered on an individual from the network of a collective. Specifying which relations are included is part of describing the network. This account gives particular relationships and their development a place within a picture that might otherwise appear to consist of nodes and connections alone. [NN-W018]

Jeffrey Hall examines friendship formation among 355 recently relocated adults and begins a nine-week longitudinal study with 112 first-year students. Participants identify acquaintances, report time and activities together and describe their closeness. Shared work or study does not carry the same association with closeness as chosen social time: leisure and everyday interaction help distinguish developing friendship from merely being present in the same workplace or classroom. The studies examine both the amount of time together and what people do during it. [NN-W013]

In the relocated-adult study, the logistic models put the probability of the casual-friend to friend transition above one half at approximately 164 hours together, and the friend to good/best-friend transition above one half at approximately 219 hours. These retrospective modeled estimates concern different relationship categories among the sampled adults, distinct from the students' longitudinal observations. They describe observed relationships between time and closeness rather than durations that guarantee friendship. [NN-W013]

岡田涼's review brings friendship research together with achievement goals, social goals, social cognition and self-determination accounts. His proposed model distinguishes motivation, conduct toward a friend and the relationship that develops through their interaction. Environmental conditions, including parent– child relations and classroom climate, and individual characteristics influence motivation for friendship. Motivation in turn encourages actions such as initiating conversation, helping and disclosing personal thoughts. The model places these actions between motivation and the closeness, satisfaction, trust and affection characterizing a friendship. [NN-W016, pp. 578–580, Figure 1]

The resulting relationship feeds back into later motivation while also contributing to adjustment and

mental health. 岡田 distinguishes possessing social skills from being motivated to use them: someone may know how to help or disclose appropriately without taking the initiative to engage. His discussion therefore follows the emergence of interaction, not just the qualities of an already established friendship. The review proposes this explanatory framework using findings primarily about schoolchildren and university students; it identifies developmental differences and tests of motivational intervention as questions for further research. [NN-W016, pp. 580–582]

Maintenance, motives and reciprocity#

Debra Oswald, Eddie Clark and Cheryl Kelly identify positivity, supportiveness, openness and interaction as dimensions of friendship maintenance. Across three studies they develop and confirm a measure and examine the interdependence of friends' behavior. Their findings connect both partners' reported conduct with dyad-level satisfaction and commitment. Maintenance in this account belongs within a reciprocal relationship: what one person does and what their friend does contribute to the relationship-level picture. The dimensions describe a family of maintenance behaviors rather than a count of how many contacts a person possesses. [NN-W014]

本田周二 studies relatively less intimate friendships, asking how motivation relates to communication, responses to conflict, satisfaction and mental health. Participants nominate a less-close friend of the same sex and answer questions about that relationship. Of 247 surveyed students, 211 remain in the analysis after exclusions. The study distinguishes motives arising from enjoyment or personal importance from motives involving obligation, anxiety or internal pressure. It also records communication through different media and responses to actual interpersonal conflict. [NN-W015]

In 本田's analyses, introjected motivation is associated with avoidance and self-concession in conflict and with lower relationship satisfaction. More autonomous motivation has few associations with the measured communication behaviors. The finding gives a particular difficulty in maintenance an explanatory home: continuing a relationship can involve pressure to retain it, and that pressure relates to how disagreements are handled. These are associations in the students' reported relationships. The reasons for continuing contact and the frequency or form of communication remain distinct parts of the investigation. [NN-W015]

李松, 刘峻君, 鲍秀琴 and 陈旭 review interpersonal gratitude in relationship maintenance. They distinguish buffering a relationship against threats from strengthening it after the threat has subsided. Their account also includes maladaptive maintenance, in which preserving a relationship conflicts with a person's wishes or social norms. They propose self- and other-evaluation as explanatory links and call for investigation at the dyadic level. Gratitude thus enters their account in both repair and further development of a connection, with the value of preserving the connection itself remaining part of the question. [NN-W017]

Professional contacts inside and outside an organization#

Hans-Georg Wolff and Klaus Moser's 2006 measurement work crosses two distinctions: contacts inside versus outside the organization, and building, maintaining versus using contacts. The resulting six subscales concern conduct, rather than network size alone. Three studies examine the proposed structure and validity. This formulation separates meeting a person from continuing the relationship and from seeking help through it, while keeping the organizational boundary explicit. Their later longitudinal investigation uses this measurement tradition to examine career outcomes. [NN-W019]

In the 2009 study, examples of the measured conduct include using company events to make contacts, catching up with colleagues in other departments about their work and seeking confidential business advice through those contacts. Building an internal relationship, maintaining it and making use of it consequently appear as different activities. External contacts receive the corresponding distinction. The three-wave analysis includes 235 participants using available repeated observations, a different number from the respondents present at the final wave. [NN-W020]

Networking is related to salary level and salary growth in this study. Career satisfaction is related to networking concurrently, with no corresponding satisfaction-growth effect. The different results matter

to the interpretation of professional success: income trajectories and satisfaction trajectories are separate outcomes. The study follows behavior and careers over time; its account does not reduce professional relationships to attendance at events or treat every favorable career measure as the same result. [NN-W020]

Tiziana Casciaro, Francesca Gino and Maryam Kouchaki distinguish deliberately instrumental professional networking from personal support and spontaneously formed relationships. Their experiments examine feelings of moral contamination, while a law-firm survey relates networking experiences to networking frequency and billable work. In that setting, relationships with clients and colleagues are consequential for work allocation. The study makes the experience of forming an instrumental tie part of the account of whether people engage in professional networking. [NN-W021]

The 2024 correction records an independent reanalysis of the available data for Studies 2–4; Study 1's original data were unavailable. In Study 3, eight participants began the survey twice but completed it only once, and the analyses used their complete entries. The correction also clarifies the office-location controls in models predicting billable hours; supplementary controls did not change the key findings. In Study 4, correcting a coding error involving two participants changes the interaction between networking content and power on cleansing-product preference to p = .072. [NN-W022]

Reconnecting after losing touch#

Daniel Levin, Jorge Walter and Keith Murnighan investigate the usefulness of dormant ties for work-project advice. They ask 224 Executive MBA participants to reconnect with two contacts after at least three years without communication, one formerly strong and one formerly weak. Participants select people who might help with an important current project, locate them, reopen communication and seek their advice. Ultimately, 129 executives complete both surveys. The comparison concerns the usefulness and features of these exchanges relative to current ties. [NN-W012]

Reconnected strong ties combine novel knowledge with retained trust and shared perspective in the authors' findings; dormant weak ties are also useful. A past relationship can retain something relevant to a new exchange while the participants have acquired different knowledge during their separation. The authors also examine contact selection in a further sample by asking participants to rank possible contacts and try lower-ranked choices. Their account describes purposeful selection and project-related reconnection. Its demonstrated outcome is useful renewed exchange, rather than sustained restoration of a close friendship. [NN-W012]

Mentorship and support communities#

Lillian Eby and colleagues' meta-analysis combines 116 independent samples comparing mentored and non-mentored individuals across youth, academic and workplace settings. Their definition involves a more experienced person taking an interest in and encouraging someone less experienced or disadvantaged. The review considers behavioral, attitudinal, relational, motivational, health and career outcomes. Associations are generally favorable and small, with differences across settings and outcome categories. [NN-W023]

Mentorship includes attention and encouragement extended to another person rather than only access sought for oneself. Its developmental and supportive outcomes differ from the salary and networking-frequency measures in the professional-contact studies. Sponsorship through advocacy and allocation of opportunities has its related account in S91, while S92 examines enrolling support for strategic work. [NN-W023]

Giovania Maesima, Lívia Gomes and Daniela Levandowski review 25 studies of internet use and interpersonal relationships. Their account describes online communication as part of existing relationships and as an entrance to new ones. Shared activity and support communities give the practices a concrete setting. Among the populations considered are parents of autistic children and people affected by cancer; exchanges can provide practical knowledge and a sense of belonging. These examples are the review's accounts of the studies it examines. [NN-W024]

The review challenges a simple division between online and offline relationships by examining particular media practices and communities. Communication can continue a connection across separation, support participation in a group or help a new relationship develop. The relevant conduct concerns what participants do together and what they exchange, as well as the medium available to them. Alongside Hall's relocation and university transitions, these accounts show relationships developing and continuing as the conditions for encounter change. Encounters and the pursuit of unexpected connections receive their complementary account in S83. [NN-W024]

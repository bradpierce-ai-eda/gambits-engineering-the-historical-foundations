Practice tasks and performance tasks#

Seventeen advanced pianists were given the same three measures of Shostakovich to learn. They chose how to practise and returned about a day later to perform the passage. Robert Duke, Amy Simmons and Carla Davis Cash examined the recorded practice sessions alongside independent rankings of those later performances. Their 2009 study made the organization of practice visible: people could spend their time on the same notes while doing substantially different work. [LP-W006]

The three highest-ranked pianists dealt with errors in a distinctive way. They identified the precise place and source of a mistake, worked on its repair and changed tempo systematically. Slowing down meant slowing enough to play the passage correctly; speeding up followed what the player could already control. A successful correction was then repeated until it remained secure in subsequent attempts. Diagnosis, a changed attempt and stabilization formed a connected sequence. [LP-W006]

These sessions also included musical inflection early in learning. The players were preparing an expressive performance, not postponing interpretation until after mechanical accuracy. Silent examination of the score, singing, annotations and changes in playing could participate in that preparation. The later rankings included musical quality as well as correct pitches and rhythms. That matters when interpreting what the best practice sessions accomplished. [LP-W006]

Total practice time and the number of attempts did not significantly predict the retention rankings in this sample. The top performers had not simply learned the excerpt more quickly. Their handling of errors distinguished them, particularly the combination of precise diagnosis, controlled tempo changes and repetition that secured a repair. The researchers observed these strategies in the pianists’ chosen practice; they did not assign a strategy to each pianist experimentally. [LP-W006]

K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer’s 1993 account of deliberate practice distinguishes improvement-directed training from work and play. Occupational performance must deliver a result with the capabilities already available. Play may be organized around enjoyment. Deliberate practice instead constructs an activity to improve a particular aspect of performance. A practice task can therefore differ markedly from the activity for which someone is preparing. [LP-W001]

Their baseball example concerns opportunities that ordinary performance supplies only sparsely. A game gives a batter particular pitches at particular moments, amid other demands. Training can concentrate attempts on the kind of pitch or response that needs improvement. Selecting, repeating and adjusting those attempts changes what the player gets to learn from. The quantity of relevant opportunities is organized deliberately rather than left to the frequency with which the situation occurs in competition. [LP-W001]

In the authors’ account, a teacher or coach identifies a suitable task and helps the learner understand what to attempt. The learner then has opportunities to repeat it, obtain informative feedback and revise the attempt. As capability changes, the task can change too. Solitary practice by an advanced music student can belong to this relationship: what looks like independent repetition may carry forward tasks designed through individualized teaching. The existing tutoring account in S41 explains other ways support can organize what a learner is able to attempt. [LP-W001]

The 1993 paper connects this training account with expert development, including studies of musicians and their accumulated practice. It emphasizes sustained concentration and efforts directed at improvement, rather than treating enjoyable absorption as the defining condition of practice. Its definition of the activity, its studies of attained performance and its larger explanation of expertise

became objects of later debate. Those questions return below, after several accounts of how practice itself can be arranged. [LP-W001]

Building an action in stages#

Pyotr Galperin’s 1959 account asks how a mental action can be formed through instruction. He distinguishes the form in which an action is carried out from its degree of generalization, abbreviation and mastery. An action performed with objects can become more economical while still depending on those objects. Removing a visible step is therefore a different change from developing an action that can be performed mentally. [LP-W009]

The orienting basis specifies what the learner attends to in deciding how to act and whether the action is correct. Galperin distinguishes orientation built around a model, orientation supplied by complete instructions for particular tasks, and orientation that teaches a method of analysing a class of tasks. The third approach changes what instruction supplies: the learner is helped to establish relevant conditions in a new case, rather than receiving only another ready-made procedure. [LP-W009]

His account follows an action through material or materialized execution, overt speech, speech addressed to oneself and increasingly internal execution. Speech can make operations explicit after the learner ceases to rely directly on the objects. Subsequent abbreviation changes how the action proceeds without requiring every earlier operation to be expressed each time. The instructional sequence concerns the form and organization of activity as well as its speed. [LP-W009]

The counting example shows why withdrawing a support and teaching an unsupported action are different instructional events. A child counts two groups of objects and is then asked to determine their combined number when the objects are covered or no longer visible. Initially, the child may try to reproduce the objects in imagination and count those images. Galperin’s discussion treats acting without the objects as something that may need to be taught, rather than a result guaranteed by successful visible counting. His larger project concerns how an action is formed and transformed through these changing supports. [LP-W009]

Practising parts and returning to the whole#

Judith Jiménez-Díaz and María Morera-Castro’s 2018 review distinguishes several choices involved in designing motor practice: physical or mental execution, the composition of the skill, variability, distribution of attempts and their ordering. These choices concern different properties of a session. Imagining a movement changes the form of execution; separating its components changes what is practised together; changing the order of tasks changes what must be selected or reconstructed between attempts. [LP-W015]

Their account of part practice separates fragmentation, progressive segmentation and simplification. In a swimming example, the learner works on kicking, arm movements and breathing separately, then performs them together. Progressive segmentation joins successively learned units: learn one group of dance steps, learn a second, combine them, then incorporate a third. Fragmentation separates coordinated components; segmentation rebuilds a sequence through successive combinations. Both accounts include returning to the activity whose performance is intended. [LP-W015]

Simplification can preserve the overall action while easing one of its demands. Hitting a stationary ball on a tee changes the difficulty of batting without requiring the learner to practise only an isolated body movement. Mental rehearsal provides another choice: imagining the execution of a skill without carrying out its full movement. The review discusses physiological and cognitive explanations for such rehearsal and organizes decisions about practice around the learner and task. Its distinctions describe alternatives

that can be combined within an instructional design, rather than one standard way to break every skill into pieces. [LP-W015]

Building a route through a performance#

Roger Chaffin and Gabriela Imreh’s 1997 study follows Imreh’s learning and relearning of the Presto from Bach’s Italian Concerto. Recordings, verbal reports and analysis of practice starts and stops make the development of one concert pianist’s memory unusually visible. The account asks how a performer can use conceptual knowledge of a piece while also relying on highly developed motor memory. [LP-W007]

The piece’s formal structure organized practice. Boundaries between musical segments supplied places to begin and end attempts, and local difficulties received concentrated attention within that larger structure. Learning the notes was joined to identifying where the performer was in the piece and how one passage related to another. A repeated theme could require a different continuation at a different point in the performance; remembering the motor sequence alone did not describe the whole problem. [LP-W007]

The pianist identified conceptual, technical and expressive features that could function as performance cues. A fingering decision, a dynamic change or a musical interpretation could become a point of deliberate attention. Later work increased the speed with which the conceptual organization could bring the relevant feature to mind during playing. The resulting route through the music supported expression and control as well as the recall of a sequence. [LP-W007]

The study also examined memory after a long interruption, including written reconstruction and recall at the piano. Formal organization remained relevant to what could be recovered. Chaffin and Imreh describe a representation that helps elicit motor performance, keep track of location and retrieve important features when they are needed. Their case complements the rehearsal and transmission accounts in S66 by showing in detail how one performer develops access to a demanding performance. [LP-W007]

Who chooses when feedback arrives#

Suzete Chiviacowsky and Gabriele Wulf’s 2002 experiment gives control over feedback a precise form. Thirty adults learned a four-key timing task. Some could ask for information about a completed attempt. Each participant in a comparison group received feedback on the same numbered attempts as a corresponding participant who chose it. The paired schedule held the occurrence of feedback and no-feedback trials together while changing who controlled those occurrences. [LP-W034]

The self-controlled group performed better on a delayed transfer test involving a changed version of the timing task. The participants tended to request feedback after attempts they thought had gone well. Questionnaire responses and comparisons of errors on feedback and no-feedback trials supported that pattern. Requests were serving partly to confirm a successful attempt, rather than simply to obtain correction after an obvious failure. [LP-W034]

This made the relation between an attempt and the subsequent information important. A schedule could be identical in numbered trials yet fit one learner’s experience differently from another’s. Chiviacowsky and Wulf proposed that self-controlled requests were more closely aligned with the learner’s needs. Their reported advantage concerned delayed transfer; the experiment’s value also lies in showing how the selection of feedback can be studied separately from simply supplying more of it. [LP-W034]

Chiviacowsky, Camila Neves, Liliane Locatelli and Cibelly Oliveira’s 2005 study examined forty children, approximately ten years old, using a timed four-key sequence and self-controlled or paired externally scheduled feedback. Its results separated relative timing, the proportions within the sequence, from absolute timing, its overall duration. On the relative-timing retention measure, the externally scheduled

group performed better. The absolute-timing comparison did not show the same significant difference. [LP-W013]

The authors considered children’s ability to recognize good and bad attempts when discussing why feedback control might work differently in this setting. Selecting when to ask for information itself requires a judgment about the attempt. Their developmental interpretation concerns that judgment and the demands of the task. It is a separate account from the adult study, with its own population, assessment and finding. [LP-W013]

Grouping attempts and feedback#

Satoshi Shibuya and Masanobu Ito’s 1999 experiment combined two scheduling choices. Participants practised timing tasks in blocked or random order and received feedback summarizing one, three or five attempts. Blocking repeated the same task together; random practice switched among tasks. Changing the summary size altered how many attempts preceded a presentation of their results. The experiment could therefore examine whether the effect of one choice depended on the other. [LP-W014]

For relative timing in the retention test, the random-practice group with three-attempt summaries performed best. Among the blocked groups, five-attempt summaries produced the best relative-timing accuracy. Relative timing and overall duration were assessed separately. The pattern concerned the combination of practice order and feedback summary, rather than establishing one summary size as preferable independently of the practice arrangement. [LP-W014]

The authors interpreted these findings through the information-processing demands created by switching tasks and retaining information about recent attempts. A longer summary changed the demands under which the learner interpreted feedback; switching among tasks changed them again. Their discussion asks how these arrangements interact in forming the timing structure of an action. [LP-W014]

Ivan Wallan Tertuliano, Orlando Pereira de Souza Júnior, Antônio Sabino da Silva Filho and Umberto Cesar Corrêa’s 2008 volleyball study used a larger motor activity. In 144 children, it combined four practice arrangements with feedback on every attempt or on approximately a third of attempts. The feedback concerned movement quality, and the study distinguished the score achieved by the serve from the pattern of movement used to achieve it. A later adaptation phase changed the task after the stabilization phase. [LP-W012]

The authors did not establish learning in the experiment and consequently did not infer a successful combination of schedules from the group comparisons. Their design nevertheless states the practical question concretely: organize where serves are directed, choose when to provide information about movement, then examine both the result and the developing movement pattern when conditions change. The intended skill, the information supplied and the measures used to assess adaptation were separate parts of that question. [LP-W012]

Relating practice to expertise#

Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick and Frederick Oswald’s 2014 meta-analysis related accumulated practice to attained performance across music, games, sport, education and professional activity. It combined studies of structured activity intended to improve performance. The reported overall correlation was .35, with substantial differences across domains and ways of measuring practice and performance. The review treated practice as important while arguing that it left considerable variation among people unexplained. [LP-W002]

Those comparisons concern differences between people: whether participants reporting more practice tend to attain higher performance. They do not assign a percentage of one person’s skill to a cause. The review’s domain and measurement analyses also show why a single correlation conceals consequential choices about the activity counted and the result being explained. Retrospective reports, recorded practice and different performance assessments contribute different kinds of information to the pooled comparison. [LP-W002]

The authors’ broad structured-practice category became central to disagreement about what their estimates could test. A measure of accumulated activity can include practice that meets some descriptions of deliberate practice while failing stricter requirements advanced by its proponents. The dispute therefore concerns the identity of the activity as well as the size of its association with performance. [LP-W002]

Friedrich Platz, Reinhard Kopiez, Andreas Lehmann and Anna Wolf’s 2014 music meta-analysis organized its pool around accumulated task-relevant practice and objectively assessed musical achievement. Its final pool contained thirteen studies and 788 participants. It reported a correlation of .61, with a confidence interval from .54 to .67. The authors argued for comparisons in which the predictors and performance outcomes were sufficiently alike to support aggregation. [LP-W008]

Their category of task-relevant practice included deliberate practice but was not identical to the strict teacher-guided category subsequently defended by Ericsson. The music analysis supplies a developed example of how defining the domain, predictor and outcome changes a research question. Its larger association and the broader review’s smaller one describe differently assembled evidence, with the authors’ own reasons for their choices. [LP-W008]

Ericsson and Kyle Harwell’s 2019 criticism returns to what counts as expertise and what counts as deliberate practice. They distinguish reproducibly superior performance on representative tasks from some educational or professional proxies. They also require evidence that the measured activity fits the original account of improvement-directed, individualized training. Their argument challenges the inclusion of studies whose performance measures or practice categories do not meet those criteria. [LP-W003]

Their more restrictive reanalysis reports different practice-performance associations, including an adjustment for measurement error. They regard these changes as evidence that broader pools can underestimate the relationship they intend to study. They also recommend examining concentration, analysis and problem solving during practice. In their account, duration alone cannot represent the quality of a training activity whose very definition includes how it is organized. [LP-W003]

This position joins an empirical question to a methodological one. Which activity has been measured, and is the performance assessment appropriate to the domain’s expertise? Ericsson and Harwell argue that answers are required before a correlation can bear on their theory. Their proposed criteria specify the body of evidence to which they believe the theory applies. [LP-W003]

Macnamara and Hambrick’s 2020 commentary disputes how the terms have been used across publications. They compare earlier descriptions with later distinctions between purposeful and deliberate practice, and object to relabelling activities previously discussed under the latter heading. Their examples include the treatment of solitary practice and research on word-game performance. The criticism concerns whether changes in terminology alter what evidence is accepted as a test of a theory. [LP-W004]

For these authors, cumulative research depends on definitions that permit a finding to count against an explanation as well as for it. If an activity can be accepted under one description and excluded under another, the relation between prediction and evidence becomes unstable. Their commentary develops that objection rather than introducing another training intervention. It argues for practice as an

important contributor to expertise whose explanatory reach can be investigated alongside other factors. [LP-W004]

Ericsson’s 2020 reply defends continuity in the original definition. He specifies a task that the learner understands, opportunities for independent attempts, actionable feedback, repetition and individualized guidance from a teacher or coach. He calls activity satisfying the first four requirements purposeful practice; deliberate practice also includes the fifth. On this interpretation, broadly structured self-study is not automatically evidence about the narrower training category. [LP-W005]

The reply also explains why solitary activity and teacher-guided training need not be opposites. An advanced student can work alone on exercises established through individual instruction. What matters to Ericsson is the relation of those exercises to the student’s present performance and subsequent guidance. His answer defends a narrower training category, while Macnamara and Hambrick question whether its usage has remained stable across the research being debated. [LP-W005]

What is carried into the next task#

Geneviève Oléron’s 1955 review begins with an operational definition of transfer. Compare learning task B after learning task A with learning B without that preceding experience. An improvement, an impairment or no measured difference supplies the corresponding positive, negative or null transfer result. The comparison asks about the effect of prior activity on a later one, rather than inferring transfer simply because the activities look alike. [LP-W011]

Oléron also emphasizes that an organized activity can combine movement, perception, memory and intellectual operations. Describing two tasks by one conspicuous surface feature may therefore miss what the learner is carrying between them. The analogy experiments developed in S64 give another concrete setting in which recognizing and using a relevant relation matters. The retention, transfer and scheduling accounts in S72 follow how different practice conditions affect what becomes available after the immediate attempt has ended. [LP-W011]

Viability and organizational functions#

Stafford Beer's viable system model concerns an organization's continuing existence in a changing environment. It locates activities among local units, coordination, resource allocation, audit, attention to future conditions, and the articulation of identity and policy.

In The Heart of Enterprise, Beer develops the viable system model. John Brocklesby, Stephen Cummings and John Davies's 1991 reconstruction draws on Heart, Brain of the Firm and Diagnosing the System. J. P. van Gigch's book review and Michael Jackson's critique offer distinct judgments. Beer's later methodological account and Raul Espejo's 2022 development extend the discussion. [W101; RGC-W009; RGC-W010; RGC-W011; RGC-W012; UX-W007]

The Italian Viable Systems Approach, whose authors acknowledge Beer, appears below. [SG-W014]

Variety is relative to the distinctions and purpose being considered#

The reconstruction begins with the problem of maintaining a separate existence while circumstances change, including circumstances not foreseen when an organization was designed. It distinguishes responding within established operating norms from reconsidering those norms. A procedure can correct deviations toward a target while leaving untouched the question of whether that target or procedure remains appropriate. Brocklesby and colleagues use this contrast to explain why viability is more demanding than repeating a previously successful routine. [RGC-W009, printed pp. 3–5]

Their account of variety concerns distinguishable states relevant to a system's purposes. A producer's relevant circumstances include customers, suppliers, regulations, and conditions bearing on what it is trying to do. Different operational activities encounter different local environments, which may overlap. The choice of system and purpose therefore enters the identification of what differences matter. [RGC-W009, pp. 5–7]

Ashby’s variety counts distinguishable possibilities within a specified set, as explained in S03. Brocklesby and colleagues use “absorbing local variety” to describe how local circumstances relate to an organization’s capacities for action. [W001; RGC-W009, pp. 5–7]

Brocklesby and colleagues describe two ways of changing the relation between demands and response capacity. Amplification increases what a system can bring to bear; attenuation reduces the variety with which it must contend. Their examples include improving a response capability and avoiding an exposure, rather than merely collecting more data. The usefulness of either depends on what the system seeks to accomplish. Avoiding a demand by abandoning the relevant purpose would describe a different accomplishment. [RGC-W009, pp. 6–7]

The original Heart passage: ignorance is also a filter#

The selected original passage of The Heart of Enterprise gives an unusually sharp qualification to the language of attenuation. Beer argues that the variety reaching an operation or management can be reduced through ignorance: the operation recognizes only some environmental features, while a manager recognizes only what the manager's existing model makes perceptible. Such reduction can produce an apparently manageable world without competent management. He explicitly distinguishes this from designing effective amplifiers and attenuators. The existence of filtering is therefore not evidence that the right distinctions have been preserved. [W101, pp. 96–97]

His first principle of organization calls for designing the relationships among managerial, operational, and environmental varieties with attention to damage to people and cost. The passage also criticizes the automation of an inherited information arrangement whose relevant design questions have not been addressed. A computerized filter can preserve an inadequate selection while losing the flexibility of the earlier manual system. A capacity to process more data does not decide what distinctions the organization must be able to act upon. [W101, pp. 97–98]

Beer then considers amplification of managerial influence through meetings, communications, staff, and the development of policies. Their appropriateness depends on the variety of the situation; a managerial style is not assigned a constant effectiveness independent of the circumstances. This selected passage describes design judgments and their possible costs. It also directs attention away from an immediate argument about what a manager wants to know and toward the design of regulation. [W101, pp. 98–99]

System 1: put local response where local knowledge exists#

In the 1991 reconstruction, System 1 consists of the operational activities through which the system in focus does what it exists to do. The system in focus is the entity and activity boundary selected for the analysis. For an automobile producer, different model-producing units can be System 1 activities; for a university considered through teaching and research, academic units can occupy that role. These are examples of identifying primary activities from a stated purpose, rather than deriving them from the appearance of an organization chart. [RGC-W009, pp. 10–11]

The operational units are expected to have local management and enough autonomy to deal with their own circumstances. Senior university officers, in the authors' example, cannot keep abreast of every academic field in the way specialists can through journals, conferences, and professional communities. Moving all the relevant decisions upward would move work away from the people who encounter and understand those distinctions. Their account predicts overloaded higher management, weak sensing of local conditions, and inadequate responses. It connects autonomy to the location of knowledge and action. [RGC-W009, pp. 11, 14–16]

“Local” need not mean geographically small. An academic field's environment can be international. Nor does the model's use of an autonomous unit imply a unit without dependence on other people or resources. The reconstruction's criterion concerns potential independent existence and management of its activities, within the chosen account. [RGC-W009, pp. 10–11]

The boundary can also change the classification. A university computer-support service may be a supporting activity when the whole university is the focus. If the focus is a support system whose purpose is to provide that service, computer support can become a primary operational activity, with the rest of the university now part of its environment. The authors use this example to warn against treating corporate boundaries and departmental names as the only possible analytical divisions. A change of focus must remain visible, because it changes what a label such as System 1 refers to. [RGC-W009, pp. 10–11]

Systems 2, 3, and 3*: autonomy keeps coordination and accountability#

Autonomous operational units can interfere with one another. They may seek the same scarce resource, create incompatible standards, or withhold something whose shared use matters to the whole. The reconstruction gives System 2 the coordination function that reduces harmful interference or oscillation. This is not a second layer that performs every local task. It concerns relations among tasks whose individual pursuit can create trouble for one another. Scheduling, shared guidance, and the communication necessary for decentralized action are among the relevant activities. [RGC-W009, pp. 9, 12–13]

System 3 concerns internal cohesion and the present conduct of the organization. Resource bargaining connects what operational units undertake with what they receive and with the expectations under which they act. Allocation is one outcome of that bargain, rather than the whole relation. The reconstruction also gives this function a role in implementing policy and establishing criteria of accountability. Coordinating potentially conflicting activities and deciding how the organization uses its resources are consequently related but distinguishable responsibilities. [RGC-W009, p. 13]

The audit function, System 3*, examines operational performance rather than relying entirely on routine representations reaching management. The authors use “audit” more broadly than an occasional accounting review: it includes ways of questioning whether activities are being performed within the agreed guidelines. Their discussion includes both checking what has occurred and a continuing capacity for examination. [RGC-W009, pp. 13, 15]

In the reconstruction's critical case, System 3 tries to occupy the local management role, generating transmissions and interventions until it becomes overloaded and loses capacity for its own tasks. The proposed alternative retains coordination, resource relationships, and audit while allowing operational managers to address the circumstances for which they hold better knowledge. [RGC-W009, pp. 14–16]

Training, induction, and shared guidance are important parts of the alternative. People can respond without seeking a senior decision at every step when they understand the broad expectations. Yet those expectations should leave room for judgment in circumstances that cannot be exhaustively anticipated. A highly elaborate rule network providing a supposed answer to every possible local situation would defeat the use of local knowledge that the account seeks to preserve. These passages locate an enabling arrangement and the continuing competence needed to use it together. [RGC-W009, pp. 15–16]

The guidelines must also be open to challenge. An operational unit may learn that an established way of acting no longer suits its environment, and the communication with coordinating functions must allow proposed changes. The authors further retain an accessible direct connection between operational and higher management for exceptional situations. Being free from constant interference is not the same as being unable to obtain guidance. Their account includes the social possibility of making contact, not just the existence of a line on a diagram. [RGC-W009, p. 16]

Systems 4 and 5: operating now while considering what should change#

The reconstruction distinguishes System 3's present, internal concern from System 4's attention to the wider environment and future. Intelligence here can involve research, development, planning, and interpretations of emerging conditions. Its purpose is not simply to produce a larger information store. It must bring the implications of those conditions into a relation with the organization's actual capacity and conduct. [RGC-W009, p. 18]

The authors' automobile example gives this relationship an ordinary managerial form. Market research may favor producing six models, while current operations favor four because of the resources and reorganization required. Both positions bear on the undertaking, and neither alone determines the appropriate decision. A claim about future demand has to meet a claim about present productive capability. [RGC-W009, p. 18]

System 5 concerns identity and policy: what purposes hold the undertaking together and how competing interpretations are resolved. In the example, a policy about a broad range of family cars at a reasonable price admits disagreement over both range and price. Policy is therefore not a substitute for every operational decision, but neither is it a decorative statement unrelated to them. The reconstruction gives it a role in balancing the present and future concerns represented by Systems 3 and 4, and in providing the context for guidelines used elsewhere. [RGC-W009, pp. 18–19, 21]

The performance discussion makes a related distinction among actuality, capability, and potentiality. Actuality concerns what is achieved under existing resources and constraints; capability concerns what could be achieved with those resources and constraints; potentiality concerns possibilities through developing resources and removing constraints. These are different comparisons, rather than three names for a current output measure. The source proposes ratios among them. Their explanatory relevance is the distinction between doing better with what is already available and changing the conditions of what becomes possible. [RGC-W009, p. 17]

A communication link must carry usable distinctions#

In Brocklesby and colleagues' account, having each of the five functions present is insufficient if the relationships among them do not work. Channels must be established and maintained. Messages must arrive in a form that recipients can understand. They use transducer for the means by which information is carried across a boundary in usable form. The term does not make interpretation disappear; it identifies a function whose adequacy must be examined. [RGC-W009, p. 19]

Their computer-system example concerns departments whose specialists express different requirements in different technical languages. A message's transport does not show that the receiving group can interpret its significance. The authors make this issue explicit through a passage in which Beer distinguishes a translator's linguistic competence from grasp of the intended possible states. They acknowledge that perfect conveyance is an ideal rarely achieved in practice. Thus the reconstructed model retains both communication capacity and the understanding required at the boundary. [RGC-W009, p. 19]

Recursion is a claim about organization at selected boundaries#

The model is recursive in the reconstruction's account: an operational unit can itself exhibit the functions needed for viability, and the system in focus can be an operational unit within a wider system. The five labels are therefore not five fixed departments at the top of a corporation. The local operational unit has its own management and relevant internal relationships. Repeating the logical organization is how the model describes relations among selected levels. [RGC-W009, pp. 10–11, 21–23]

The selection of levels remains an analytical act. The authors' automobile example permits different wider systems to be drawn, and they acknowledge interpretive choices in higher-level boundaries. The activity, environment, capacities, and relationships claimed for it still have to be identified. [RGC-W009, pp. 21– 24]

The reconstruction itself ends with a warning against the attraction of a tidy diagram. A model does not provide a complete picture of people and their interactions, and its appearance does not make participants committed to changing an organization. The authors offer it as a language and framework for examination, sometimes alongside other models. It also preserves the work of application: empirical understanding, judgment, and participation are not supplied by the diagram alone. [RGC-W009, p. 24]

Van Gigch's review: an explanation still needs practical mastery#

J. P. van Gigch's 1981 review of The Heart of Enterprise reconstructs the five functions and their variety relationships while raising objections about how the account can be put to work. He finds important insights in the book but considers the presentation difficult to master. His criticism is more precise than saying that the model has no relevance: an uninitiated reader may not acquire the knowledge and practice needed for application simply by reading the book. He also questions the presentation of rules as maxims, principles, theorems, axioms, and laws without the mathematical derivations he expected in that volume. [RGC-W011, pp. 128–129]

The review further distinguishes identifying obvious mismatches from calculating calibrated entropy exchanges in a real organization. Van Gigch describes practical measurement of the relevant entropy as extremely difficult, including attempts to compare mental effort across degrees of mechanization. The review leaves the reader with both an account of what the book illuminates and a concrete reason why conceptual clarity is insufficient for competent implementation. [RGC-W011, p. 129]

Jackson's dispute: structural constraints or an interpretive instrument?#

Michael Jackson's 1992 research note rejects a simple reading in which an analyst finds an objective organization and rearranges it to fit an idealized chart. He then distinguishes two more serious interpretations. His preferred structuralist reading allows observers to select identity and purpose, while maintaining that the organization pursuing that purpose faces real structural constraints. On this view, the model seeks underlying relationships that explain operational difficulties; the analyst's task is more than facilitating whichever account participants already favor. [RGC-W010, pp. 561–562]

Jackson contrasts this with the interpretive use he attributes to Harnden and Espejo, where a model supports conversations among different perspectives about emergent organizational forms. The dispute becomes sharp when a cybernetic diagnosis conflicts with participants' beliefs. Jackson asks which should guide action and argues that the existence of cybernetic constraints cannot be settled merely by choosing a preferred conversation. [RGC-W010, pp. 563–564]

The note is relevant to gambits engineering because it makes the status of an organizational explanation consequential. A claim about a constraint, a proposal for collective interpretation, and a participant's agreement are different grounds for action. Jackson argues for one reading. [RGC-W010, pp. 561–564]

Beer on modeling, identity and informational economy#

The reproduction of Beer's account of the VSM's provenance and methodology distinguishes literary analogy from more precisely specified mappings. Beer argues that a model selects relationships and invariances, and that the context and boundaries of its application must be nominated. His account of internal regulation, attenuation, and amplification remains connected to that modeling program. The reproduction also lists applications, while acknowledging the absence of a systematic archive and reliance on records and recollection at hand. [RGC-W012, pp. 4–8, 16–17]

Within that account, Beer uses “intrinsic control” for a relation in which a common influence delimits environmental circumstances and evokes a response converging on an adaptive outcome. The described ordering need not be understood only as a rule imposed by a separately identified manager. This is his explanation through Sommerhoff and Ashby, whose positions are mediated through this particular passage. [RGC-W012, p. 5]

Identity is also explicitly difficult in the later text. Beer asks who “we” are when a manager asks what business an organization is in: shareholders, employees, managers, customers, and other interested people can offer different answers. His account recognizes an observer's imputation of purpose and reports disagreement about the purpose of a health system. The source therefore makes the choice of an organizational identity visible rather than treating it as an automatically shared fact. [RGC-W012, p. 10]

Appendix 1 gives a concrete connection between informational economy and lost explanatory power. Beer describes combining the outputs of three shifts into one total. The resulting representation handles fewer distinctions, but calculations within it cannot recover which shift produced what. He treats this as a choice to give up some modeling power for economy in recording and handling data, acceptable only when deliberate and remembered as a limitation. The appendix also distinguishes this known

simplification from aspects of a large system that the modeler has failed to recognize at all. This extract is reproduced from The World, the Flesh and the Metal. [RGC-W012, appendix 1, pp. 13–14]

Espejo's later account: residual variety depends on local organization#

Raul Espejo's 2022 article develops an explicitly retrospective account of Cybersyn and his subsequent work. One relevant distinction concerns information systems and organizational transformation. He reports that some later clients possessed substantial computing resources and programming capability but sought help understanding their organization and developing meaningful performance indicators. The difficulty in his account was not merely a lack of software. It concerned how activities, purposes, and communications were understood and arranged. [UX-W007, “Real-time management and variety engineering”]

The article's central illustration concerns residual variety. Local self-organization and self-regulation handle much of what occurs before every matter reaches a separate managerial decision. The organization then faces a remaining range of circumstances, and management a further residual. Espejo explicitly calls the lines used to show these reductions impressionistic. They are not measured ratios demonstrating that a particular number of units of effort or information has been eliminated. His argument concerns the response demands that reach particular relationships after other activities have already occurred. [UX-W007, fig. 3 and accompanying discussion]

His account retains the means through which such an arrangement operates. Information filters, communication devices, skills, learning, collaboration, and wider cultural or regulatory conditions remain relevant. Reducing the variety reaching a manager can depend on activities taking place among people and within the surrounding institutions. His political and ethical preferences about how these relations should be formed remain part of his argument. [UX-W007, “Real-time management and variety engineering”]

Espejo also distinguishes two descriptions. A black-box description examines relations among an organization, its environment, and regulation under an assumed agreement about purpose. An operational description examines the interactions through which people construct the organization, its purposes, and coordinated action. He argues that the descriptions are complementary. The second description matters because the agreement presupposed in the first is itself something people have to produce and sustain. [UX-W007, figs. 4–5 and accompanying discussion]

The operational relationships include performance, cohesion, policy, inclusion, and organizational citizenship. Policy involves conversations between present operations and those attending to future circumstances. Inclusion concerns to whom policy makers are accountable; wider normative contexts also enter the account. Espejo contrasts authoritarian and more mutually respectful forms of cohesion and raises questions of power, ownership, and stakeholder participation. These are explicit concerns in this later formulation. [UX-W007, fig. 5 and accompanying numbered relationships]

His discussion of VIPLAN adds a methodological example. Naming the transformation, suppliers, actors, customers, owners, and interveners is a way of making the selected organizational description explicit. Later methodological development attends to communications and interactions through which problematic situations are addressed. The article traces this development through his own projects and collaborations. They supply the source's own account of how governance and meaning enter its organizational method. [UX-W007, “From the Viplan method to the Viplan methodology”]

A distinct Italian programme: structure, interaction, and compatibility#

Sergio Barile, Jaqueline Pels, Francesco Polese, and Marialuisa Saviano introduce the Italian Viable Systems Approach as a methodological framework for business and social phenomena, including marketing. They acknowledge Beer as part of its background while drawing on a broader set of systems traditions. Their text explicitly describes the approach as a meta-level orientation, rather than a ready set of operative models for each problem. [SG-W014, printed pp. 62–63]

Their structure–system distinction separates a description of components and organized relations from the dynamics of their actual interaction. The account also identifies decision-making and operations, selective openness to resources, and dependence on other actors. The relevant context includes actors whose resources and influence bear on the undertaking's viability. Their broader propositions about all viable systems are premises of their approach. [SG-W014, pp. 63–65]

Consonance and resonance make one selected distinction particularly concrete. Consonance refers to potential compatibility among entities that might interact. Resonance concerns the harmonious interaction that actually develops. A favorable structural possibility is consequently distinguished from its realization in activity. The source relates this distinction to selecting relationships and engaging actors who control relevant resources. [SG-W014, pp. 65–66]

Viability, identity and organizational purpose#

The central VSM account is connected: local units respond using local knowledge; coordination addresses interference; cohesion and resource bargaining relate activities to the undertaking; audit supplies examination; intelligence brings changing wider conditions into view; policy addresses identity and direction. Channels and interpretation connect these functions.

These sources ask whose identity an organization preserves, how purposes are agreed, and what its measurements support. They locate decisions and responsibilities among participants with different information, capacities and authority.

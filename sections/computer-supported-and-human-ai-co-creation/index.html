S69 Computer-supported and human–AI co-creation #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

Computer support for creative work includes preserving alternatives, sharing developing representations, exchanging ideas and generating candidates. These activities have different histories. Work on creativity-support interfaces and electronic brainstorming provides a setting for later studies of human–AI collaboration, whose interventions range from an initial suggestion to continuing participation in a shared performance or design.

Shneiderman's creativity-support tools#

Ben Shneiderman's 2007 account places exploration at the center of creativity-support design. A person looking for an answer may need to inspect distributions, relationships, gaps and unusual cases before knowing exactly what to ask. Faceted search and dynamic queries allow incremental changes to a view. Rapid, reversible responses help users learn from successive attempts rather than commit to one query and receive a final answer. [CRE-W035, pp. 25–27]

Rich history-keeping supports this extended activity. A record of alternatives lets users compare what they have tried, return to an earlier state and modify it. A promising case can be sent to a colleague, and an investigation can resume without reconstructing its previous path. Shneiderman connects such facilities to both systematic and relatively free exploration. His design proposals concern the working relationship between successive attempts, their representations and the people who may develop them. [CRE-W035, pp. 26–27]

The familiar combination of a low threshold, a high ceiling and wide walls expresses another part of his argument. Beginners should be able to start, experienced users should be able to attempt ambitious work, and the environment should permit different kinds of activity. A tool that supports only a narrow demonstration may be easy to learn while offering little room to develop. Movement among exploration, representation, analysis and communication is therefore part of the design problem. [CRE-W035, design principles]

Collaboration also changes over a project's life. Early exchanges concern problem definition and goals; later ones may involve information, refinement, validation and dissemination. Shneiderman discusses trust, records of contributions and opportunities to expose uncertainty safely. He argues for evaluating creativity-support tools through observation of serious work over extended periods, alongside shorter studies. The article supplies an account of computer support in which a useful intervention can preserve, connect or expose work without generating the central idea itself. [CRE-W035, pp. 26–31]

Electronic brainstorming and time to use others' ideas#

Hamit Coşkun's 2011 study varied group size, memory instructions and session length in three electronic-brainstorming experiments. Participants worked at separate computers and completed a typing-speed test before the exercise. Tasks included imagining the consequences of children being born with an extra thumb and proposing improvements to a university. Electronic exchange made other participants' contributions available while people generated their own. [CRE-W036, pp. 91–94]

Across the experiments, larger groups generated more ideas in total and more unique and original ideas. The corresponding individual-output analyses did not show that each participant produced more merely by belonging to a larger group. The results describe an enlarged collective pool alongside a different finding about its individual contributors. Both are useful for understanding what the electronic arrangement accomplished. [CRE-W036, results and general discussion]

The memory instruction asked participants to remember ideas because they would later be tested. It reduced production in a fifteen-minute session but improved production in a twenty-five-minute session. In the longer exercise, participants had more time in which attention to the shared material could contribute to further generation. Coşkun presents this interaction as a way of understanding inconsistent findings about such instructions. The same request can have a different effect when the period available for using it changes. [CRE-W036, experiment 3]

Typing speed affected total and unique production, while the study did not find corresponding effects on originality and feasibility. The experiments thus bring the interface, attention to others' ideas and session duration into the account of the exercise. The earlier discussion of group idea generation in this volume provides the broader background; Coşkun's contribution concerns these particular arrangements for electronic participation. [CRE-W036, general discussion]

COFI and the design of a creative partnership#

Jeba Rezwana and Mary Lou Maher developed COFI to describe interaction in human–AI co-creative systems. Their framework considers collaboration style, communication, creative process and creative product. Partners can work on a shared task or divide the work, take turns or act in parallel, and follow a plan or respond spontaneously. These choices describe how participation unfolds, beyond identifying what kind of output the system produces. [CRE-W037, framework]

Communication receives particular attention. A person can intentionally instruct a system through text, voice, gesture or direct manipulation. A system may also collect consequential information that the person gives off without making it an explicit instruction, such as gaze, bodily movement or facial expression. Communication from the AI to the person can provide feedback, reveal a contribution's status or support a response. The framework separates these channels so that the presence of an input facility is not mistaken for a complete two-way exchange. [CRE-W037, communication components]

The creative process and product provide further distinctions. A partner may contribute to generating a new element, evaluating an existing one or changing material already present. A shared dance, drawing or narrative presents different opportunities for timing and response. COFI asks how the contributions relate to the evolving product and to one another, making interaction itself an object of analysis. [CRE-W037, creative-process and product components]

The authors applied the framework to 92 systems across thirteen creative domains. Two coders independently examined a quarter of the systems, discussed differences and established a coding basis for the remainder. Their analysis identifies recurring interaction designs in the described systems. Its contribution is a structured account of creative participation, grounded in those designs, rather than an effectiveness ranking of every possible form of partnership. [CRE-W037, §§4–5]

Suggested beginnings and the stories people write#

Anil R. Doshi and Oliver P. Hauser studied access to AI-generated ideas in a short-story task. The 293 writers were asked to produce an eight-sentence story suitable for a teenage and young-adult audience. They were assigned to write without AI assistance, with access to one generated idea, or with access to as many as five. A generated idea consisted of three sentences offered as starting material; the writer composed the story. [CRE-W038, experimental design]

The second phase obtained evaluations of the stories from a separate group of participants. Evaluators first judged the work without being told about its AI assistance. In the condition allowing five ideas, average novelty ratings were 8.1 percent higher and usefulness ratings 9 percent higher than in the

unassisted condition. The study therefore found an improvement in judged individual work under this specific form of assistance. [CRE-W038, results]

The authors also compared stories with one another and with their generated starting ideas. AI-assisted stories were more similar across writers. Access to a pool of suggestions could improve a writer's evaluated result while drawing the collection toward more similar content. These analyses concern different objects: the quality attributed to one story and the relationships among many stories. [CRE-W038, collective-diversity analysis]

The intervention's sequence is important to understanding the result. The model supplied a possible beginning, and a person interpreted and developed it within a tightly bounded writing task. Doshi and Hauser's study makes this early contribution visible as part of the subsequent human work. It also provides a concrete case in which an individual's benefit and the variety of the resulting collection can be assessed separately. [CRE-W038]

Comparing a language model with oblique prompts#

Barrett R. Anderson, Jash Hemant Shah and Max Kreminski compared ChatGPT with the Oblique Strategies prompt deck. Thirty-three participants were included in the analysis. Each worked with both forms of assistance on timed tasks involving product improvements and improbable consequences. Examples included making a jigsaw puzzle more engaging and imagining a world in which only people's feet could be seen. [CRE-W039, §3]

The deck offers brief provocations that a participant must interpret in relation to the task. A language model can provide developed candidates and respond to further requests. The experiment gave these two kinds of assistance a common set of ideation tasks and an eight-minute period for each session. It thereby compared an established prompt device with conversational model support, rather than comparing assistance with no intervention at all. [CRE-W039, materials and procedure]

With ChatGPT, participants produced approximately 15 percent more ideas and covered 27 percent more categories in the reported analysis. Their ideas were also more homogeneous across participants. The analyses did not detect the same change in diversity within an individual's collection, and the unique-idea measure did not show a significant improvement. More output and more represented categories can therefore coexist with greater similarity among the collections produced by different people. [CRE-W039, results]

The authors discuss the interpretive distance between an oblique prompt and the current task, compared with the more developed material a model can supply. This is their interpretation of how the forms of assistance may shape ideation. Together with the task and outcome distinctions, it gives the comparison more explanatory value than a single judgment that one tool makes people more creative. [CRE-W039, discussion]

Generating a pool of product concepts#

Karan Girotra, Lennart Meincke, Christian Terwiesch and Karl T. Ulrich's 2023 working paper compared student product concepts with concepts generated using GPT-4. The task was to propose a physical product for college students that could retail for less than fifty dollars. The comparison used 200 student ideas from a 2021 course, 100 model-generated ideas and another 100 generated with examples of highly rated student concepts in the prompt. [CRE-W040, approach]

The researchers requested descriptions of forty to eighty words in batches of ten, using GPT-4-0314 with a temperature setting of 0.7. To continue generating a large pool within the available context, they had the model compress its previous ideas into shorter summaries. Those summaries accompanied subsequent

requests for ideas different from the preceding ones. The candidate pool was thus constructed through repeated generation with a representation of what had already been proposed. [CRE-W040, approach]

The example-seeded condition supplied seven highly rated student ideas from a separate set. It made examples part of the intervention while retaining the same product challenge. The paper compares distributions of evaluated concepts as well as their averages, reflecting the innovation-tournament setting in which a large pool is generated and then selected. [CRE-W040, approach and evaluation]

Survey respondents rated purchase intent for the descriptions. Average ratings were higher for both generated pools, and AI-generated ideas occupied 35 of the best-rated forty places in the combined collection. The human ideas received higher novelty ratings. These results describe judgments of proposed concepts, including the promising end of their distribution; the study did not observe the products being launched and purchased. [CRE-W040, results]

This account connects prompt construction, repeated generation, retention of prior candidates and evaluation of a pool. Its particular contribution differs from the story-writing studies: the generated descriptions are themselves the concepts being screened, rather than starting ideas that participants subsequently turn into finished stories. The procedures and assessed outcomes make those differences intelligible. [CRE-W040]

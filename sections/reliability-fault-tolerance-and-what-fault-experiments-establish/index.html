Reliability under disruption#

A system can contain unreliable components and still provide a useful service. Explaining how requires more than counting spare components or naming a recovery mechanism. The relevant literature specifies relationships among components, assumptions about failures, the service to be preserved, and ways of checking what happens under disruption.

Fault injection is especially informative because it intervenes in a system rather than waiting for a naturally occurring failure. It can reveal a weakness, exercise a recovery path, or test a bounded proposition about a model. The meaning of its result depends on what was changed, when the change occurred, the workload that exercised it, the observations collected, and the criterion of success. These dependencies are explicit in the sources considered below.

Von Neumann: reliability through an arrangement of fallible elements#

The reconstruction of John von Neumann's lectures develops the possibility of reliable computation using unreliable elements. A majority arrangement is a concrete part of the discussion. Its inputs are intended to represent the same state, while their errors are assumed independent in the relevant calculation. Agreement among several representations can then help preserve a correct result when an individual representation is wrong. The majority element can itself fail, so its error must also enter the account; counting additional representations alone does not settle the output's reliability. The source later explicitly calls a constant error probability independent of time and previous inputs unrealistic. Actual failure probabilities can vary with input, history, environment, and element. [W043, §§7–8, especially reconstructed pp. 23–24; §11.3]

Stott and colleagues: a modeled improvement can come from counting assumptions#

James Stott and colleagues examine a problem in transferring common-cause failure models and generic data from nuclear applications to aerospace. Redundant components can be affected together; their failures therefore cannot always be treated as independent events. The paper's selected methodological discussion goes further than this familiar warning. It asks what the recorded failures and testing arrangements actually allow an analyst to estimate. A test interval, an individual test episode, and a demand on the system are not automatically the same observational unit. The number of opportunities to detect failures changes how observed counts relate to the quantities in a model. [W065, §§3–5]

Their comparison distinguishes testing all members of a group in one episode from testing them in separate, staggered episodes. In the latter scheme, finding a failed component can lead to additional tests of the others. Those procedures affect the challenges and observations represented by the data. Using factors estimated under one interpretation with a model that assumes another can therefore alter the calculated risk without establishing a corresponding alteration in the physical system. The authors specifically criticize using mixed interpretations of generic data within one risk assessment or trade study. An alternative can receive a lower calculated risk because of the assumption assigned to it, then appear to earn that advantage as an analytical result. [W065, §§3–5]

The paper does not conclude that the actual testing schedule can never affect common-cause risk. It gives a possible real mechanism: separating test episodes may separate operators and thereby reduce some shared influences. Its point is that this causal difference is not captured merely by switching the parametric counting convention. Quantifying that benefit requires analysis of the relevant causes and coupling factors. The authors' recommendations concern the NASA modeling problem they address in

2010. The substantive lesson supplied by this source is how an apparent advantage of an arrangement can arise from the interpretation of evidence rather than an established change in the arrangement's failure behavior. [W065, §§5–6]

Hsueh, Tsai, and Iyer: the experiment includes a workload and an observer#

Mei-Chen Hsueh, Timothy Tsai, and Ravishankar Iyer's 1997 survey explains fault injection as an experimental approach to dependability evaluation. Its relevance extends beyond the act of introducing a fault. Their account distinguishes simulation during conception and design, injection into a prototype or operational system, and measurement of naturally occurring behavior in operation. Each supplies different evidence. A simulation can explore a design before implementation but embodies simplified assumptions. A physical or software intervention exercises an implemented system but represents the faults that the experimenter can emulate. Operational measurement observes real events and workloads but can require long collection periods and face changing field conditions. [AFC-W003, pp. 75–76]

The survey's experimental architecture makes these differences concrete. A target system runs a workload drawn from a workload generator and library. A fault injector applies changes selected from a fault library, including choices of type, location, and time. A controller organizes the experiment; monitoring and data collection record behavior; analysis interprets the observations. These are distinct functions even when some share a machine or program. The result is thus produced by a target-plus-experiment arrangement. The injector alone does not determine whether the relevant behavior was exercised or whether its consequences were observed. [AFC-W003, p. 76, fig. 1]

An introduced fault can produce an internal error without causing failure of the service being judged. The workload may not exercise a changed location. An internal error may be detected, contained, or masked before it affects the chosen result. Conversely, an incorrect result may escape a detector. The survey describes a Messaline device that senses whether an injected fault is activated and produces an error, and Ferrari experiments in which detection depended on fault type and on how frequently the affected routines were exercised. These details explain why the mere number of injections is an inadequate account of the experiment's outcome. [AFC-W003, pp. 77–80]

Selecting an injection technique also selects what can be reached and how the experiment may interfere with the system. Pin-level hardware methods can control particular physical intervention points and offer high time resolution, but do not literally reproduce every fault inside a chip. Contactless physical methods can reach internal behavior while providing less precise control over the exact time and location. Software methods offer access to applications and operating-system state without requiring the same specialized hardware, yet cannot reach every hardware location. Their added execution and instrumentation can disturb the workload or change the software structure. These are substantive differences in the relationship between intervention and object. [AFC-W003, pp. 76–79]

Timing matters to the observation as well as the intervention. A method with inadequate time resolution may miss rapid propagation even if it is adequate for a longer-lived memory error. The survey discusses combining software injection with hardware monitoring to address such cases, while retaining the extra cost and constraints of the monitoring equipment. Compile-time modification and runtime triggering likewise provide different control: placing a change in a program image differs from invoking an injector when a timer expires, a specified instruction is reached, or an exception occurs. The useful technique depends on the fault behavior and question being investigated. [AFC-W003, pp. 79–80]

The reported tool comparisons show why reach is consequential. In the cited MARS experiments, pin-level injection, heavy-ion radiation, and electromagnetic interference exercised different error-detection mechanisms. The methods were complementary in the surveyed results. Evidence about one chosen family of interventions cannot automatically establish coverage of error behavior that the family does not

produce. The survey also reports undetected incorrect outcomes in selected Xception tests; that observation remains attached to the tested processor units and setup rather than becoming a general percentage for all computers. [AFC-W003, pp. 78, 81]

Finally, the survey expressly distinguishes fault-injection findings from operational measures such as mean time between failures and availability. Choosing and injecting faults does not determine how often those faults occur in service. A collection of tests can investigate behavior conditional on specified interventions without providing the occurrence rates and representative exposure needed for an operational frequency estimate. A high pass rate across chosen injections therefore cannot simply be relabeled high field availability. The distinction is relevant even when the injections and observations are perfectly executed. [AFC-W003, pp. 75–76]

Alvaro, Rosen, and Hellerstein: choosing faults from an outcome's dependencies#

Peter Alvaro, Joshua Rosen, and Joseph Hellerstein address a difficulty that arises when a service can fail through combinations of disruptions. Selecting individual faults at random may quickly reveal shallow bugs while rarely reaching a particular sequence of message losses and crashes. Their lineage-driven fault injection, or LDFI, reasons backward from a successful outcome. It asks which events and messages supported that outcome and what combinations of allowed faults could remove all of the known support. This is a method for choosing informative candidate experiments. [AFC-W010, §§1–2]

The paper's historical Kafka replication example shows why a combination matters. In the described beta-version protocol, a leader becomes separated from other replicas. Those replicas are removed from the in-sync set; the remaining leader acknowledges a write without disseminating it, then crashes. The acknowledged write is lost. A nominal count of replicas does not describe the sequence that defeats the stated stable-write requirement. [AFC-W010, §1.1]

LDFI starts from the computational lineage of a good outcome: the sequence and dependencies through which it was obtained. Its MOLLY prototype turns information about uncertain communications into a logical problem for a satisfiability solver. A solution identifies a candidate combination of failures that could defeat the currently known derivations of the outcome. The system then performs a concrete execution with that candidate. This forward step is essential. A program may have a recovery path or alternative derivation that was absent from the earlier successful execution, so removing its known support need not actually make it fail. [AFC-W010, §1.2; appendix B]

The procedure alternates between those steps. A further successful execution can reveal additional support that the next candidate must defeat. An execution that violates the chosen assertion supplies a counterexample. When the specified search completes without such a counterexample, the authors give an assurance for the declared configuration and bounded model. Their soundness account depends on confirming candidate violations through execution rather than publishing every candidate generated by the solver as an actual bug. The completeness argument concerns coverage within its formal setting. [AFC-W010, §1.2; appendix B]

The bounds define the proposition being checked. The program, topology, inputs, and assertions are supplied. A logical end-of-time bound limits the execution length; an earlier end-of-finite-failures bound specifies when message loss ceases, leaving time for the program to respond; a separate bound limits permanent node crashes. The paper's example of six global transitions, message loss permitted during the first four, and at most one crash is an example of a particular failure specification. Byzantine behavior and crash recovery are outside the stated failure model. [AFC-W010, §2.1.1]

The abstraction of time has an additional effect. MOLLY evaluates the programs in a synchronous simulation with a deterministic ordering for successfully delivered messages. This focuses the analysis on faults and reduces the execution space, while potentially missing bugs that depend on asynchronous

ordering and timing. The paper explicitly says that this sacrifices completeness in the more general model. Similarly, the assurance relies on internal determinism: repeating the same faults must reproduce the relevant outcome lineage. Merely using the tool on an internally nondeterministic protocol does not confer the same completeness guarantee. [AFC-W010, §§2.1, 7]

Language and evidence requirements also remain. The method needs identifiable outputs, sufficiently fine-grained lineage, and control over the relevant communications. The prototype uses Dedalus programs to provide these facilities. Where a derivation depends on a fact's absence, the lineage analysis must account for why that fact did not occur. Appendix B describes different treatments of this issue, including an explicitly incomplete option that ignores such provenance. The assurance depends on these analysis conditions. [AFC-W010, §2.2; appendix B]

The evaluation is informative both for favorable cases and for a case that resists a blanket efficiency claim. In the reported Kafka model comparison, MOLLY found a counterexample in 38 concrete executions and 3.74 seconds; the random strategy averaged 1,183.12 executions and 133.30 seconds over 25 runs. For the three-phase-commit model, however, MOLLY took 55 executions and 9.60 seconds, while the random strategy averaged 40.60 executions and 6.24 seconds. These are source-reported measurements for the studied configurations. They show why fewer irrelevant candidate executions can be valuable, while also showing that the analysis is not uniformly faster for every example. [AFC-W010, §5, fig. 12]

The paper also distinguishes finding a counterexample from ruling out counterexamples within a bounded space. A random strategy may discover one quickly, yet an absence claim would require coverage it has not thereby provided. The reported examples of bug-free models compare MOLLY's executed tests with the much larger set of possible failure combinations under the chosen parameters. Their relevance is to the work required for that specified verification task. [AFC-W010, §5, figs. 11–13]

To see the boundary concretely, change the proposed application from the given inputs and message-loss/crash model to a new topology with recovery after a crash. The previously completed analysis no longer establishes the new proposition. The difference follows from the declared scope of the paper's model.

La Porte: efficiency and vulnerability in interdependent infrastructures#

Todd La Porte's selected chapter passage addresses large technical systems whose components and organizations are connected across locations. The examples include mutual dependence among electricity, telecommunications, transport, and water services. A disruption is not confined by the organizational ownership of the first affected component. He describes tight coupling as a source of efficiency in ordinary operation that can also increase vulnerability. Defensive management, stockpiling, and relationships with other organizations can then consume some of the efficiency the coupling was intended to provide. These are propositions in his organizational account of infrastructure protection. [UX-W005, pp. 135–137]

The same passage reports Charles Perrow's argument about conflicting demands for authority: tightly coupled operation can call for centralized coordination, while unexpected problems can require discretion among those closest to them. La Porte's surrounding discussion also makes the response depend on the character of disruption. Relatively predictable hazards support routinized preparation; purposive, changing threats can require other capabilities. Maintaining several modes of readiness creates organizational and budgetary demands that may be difficult to sustain after a conspicuous event has passed. [UX-W005, pp. 136–138]

An official practice account: resilience retains resources and responsibility#

The 2020 US interagency Sound Practices to Strengthen Operational Resilience supplies a further kind of source: an official practice formulation. The cyber-risk table describes governance, identification, protection, detection, response, recovery, and third-party risk management. It identifies data, personnel, devices, facilities, systems, and external providers as part of the dependencies of critical operations. Its account preserves a declared tolerance for disruption rather than promising uninterrupted performance in every respect. [SG-W029, selected body and table 1]

The table's detail is relevant because protection does not remove ongoing activity. It calls for training, maintenance, configuration management, monitoring, tested backups, exercised response and recovery processes, and independent oversight. Lessons from incidents are to inform later activities. Outsourcing likewise does not remove the firm's responsibility for risks associated with a critical operation: the formulation retains due diligence, contracts that allocate responsibilities, monitoring, and checks of a provider's ability to continue or restore service. These prescriptions describe organizational work that remains around the technical arrangements. [SG-W029, table 1]

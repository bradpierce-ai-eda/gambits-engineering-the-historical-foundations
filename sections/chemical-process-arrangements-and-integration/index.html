Targeting heat recovery before choosing equipment#

Truls Gundersen’s primer describes a history from process development through unit operations to attention to interactions between operations. Heat recovery gives a concrete reason to study those interactions. The network with minimum utility use, fewest pieces of equipment or lowest total annual cost can differ because these criteria value different features of the design. The primer describes how a more detailed distillation model can change the estimated opportunities for heat integration. The model supplies temperatures and duties on which the estimate depends. [T09-W001]

In the first of their 1978 papers, Bodo Linnhoff and John Flower use a problem table that divides the heat exchange problem into temperature intervals. Its utility requirements can be calculated before specifying the network. The authors’ first four-stream example shows how subnetworks respecting those requirements can be joined by moving duties from paired heaters and coolers to new process exchangers. Neighboring units can be merged where the resulting connections allow it. Their five-unit alternative in Fig. 6 gives up some heat recovery and has a higher overall cost in that example. The number of units, heat recovery and annual cost give different descriptions of the same alternatives. [T09-W005]

Evolving a feasible network#

The second paper begins with a feasible design, preferably one with maximum heat recovery, and examines changes that promote properties such as low cost or easier start-up. In case 4SP2, the problem table specifies permissible subnetwork utility loads for the recovery objective. The authors form new matches from paired utility duties and merge units. Their later alternatives show roles for both a cyclic topology and parallel stream splitting. The cycle can serve the recovery objective, while splitting can allow a reduction in unit count. The designs and comparisons are worked calculations for stated streams. [T09-W002]

Ryu and Maravelias use a discrete temperature grid to incorporate streams with variable temperatures and flow rates in an optimization model. The utility-targeting objective concerns the cost of hot and cold utilities. A network objective adds the annualized costs of exchanger area and units. In one solution, stream s6 splits at 290 K to exchange heat with s5 and s4. A later split at 344 K supplies matches with s1 and s3. The split is part of a computed configuration whose heat balances and admissible matches are expressed on the temperature grid. [T09-W003]

Changing a crude-oil preheat train#

Li and Chang’s retrofit design starts with an existing crude-oil preheat train and restrictions on reusing equipment. Their fired-heater target moves from 200 to 166.2 million Btu per hour, a reduction of 33.8. They distinguish the process pinch from a utility pinch associated with steam generation. The specified minimum temperature difference is 70 °F between process streams, 35 °F against steam and 30 °F against cooling water. A proposed match must be judged against the appropriate difference. [T09-W004]

The authors prioritize large cross-pinch duties and propose new connections that retain parts of the old network. For the proposed retrofit, Table III projects a fired-duty reduction of 26.08 million Btu per hour, about 77 percent of the target, and recovery of an additional 18.6 million Btu per hour for generating 120-psig steam. [T09-W004]

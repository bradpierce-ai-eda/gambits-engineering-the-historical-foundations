Firepower and the scale of operations#

В. К. Триандафиллов’s 1929 work examines changes in weapons and in the composition and movement of forces. In his account of cavalry, the capacity to move and the ability to fight mounted are different problems. The spread of automatic weapons changes what can be done in a mounted engagement and shifts the importance of the fire weapons a formation can bring. His argument is framed by the forces and operations of his period. [T15-W001]

The Marine Corps account of maneuver warfare#

The 1997 Marine Corps publication Warfighting describes maneuver and attrition as styles along a spectrum. Its account of maneuver emphasizes identifying and exploiting gaps and vulnerabilities, moving rapidly enough to exploit a changing situation and giving subordinates room to act under uncertainty. The discussion still gives combat power a place. Disruption is an intended effect on the opponent’s capacity to respond coherently. S16 explains the separate command question of how shared intent can support subordinate decisions. [T15-W002]

Cole Petersen examines the difficulty of carrying the maneuver and attrition distinction into strategy. His account traces part of the debate through Liddell Hart’s discussion of an indirect approach. A method of fighting can describe how a force acts in a particular engagement, while a strategic account has to explain the relationship of those actions to a wider object. Petersen disputes treating the two labels as complete competing strategies. [T15-W004]

Competing accounts of a campaign#

A Command and General Staff College study asks how to interpret the coalition campaign in the Gulf War. It contrasts an explanation centered on AirLand Battle maneuver precepts with one centered on firepower and attrition. The study concludes that the coalition used maneuver-warfare precepts in defeating Iraqi forces. The author’s judgment concerns the campaign as interpreted through AirLand Battle doctrine. [T15-W003]

The Defense Department’s 1992 report to Congress documents how different aircraft could supply parts of a single engagement. In the northern Persian Gulf, U.S. SH-60B helicopters provided electronic surveillance and radar capability, while British Lynx helicopters brought radar-guided missiles. The report describes this combination in operations against Iraqi vessels and distinguishes it from helicopter patrols for mines. [T15-W005]

Florian Waitl’s Army University Press study describes a different operation at the opening of the air campaign. Early on 17 January 1991, Task Force Normandy combined eight Army AH-64 Apache attack helicopters with Air Force MH-53J Pave Low helicopters. It destroyed two Iraqi air-defense command and control sites. In Waitl’s account, the strikes opened a corridor through the air-defense network for coalition aircraft. The chapter then traces the shift in targeting from the air campaign to preparations for the ground campaign, including strikes on communications, logistics and armored units. Waitl describes what destroying the sites enabled. The staff-college study’s judgment about maneuver and attrition concerns the campaign as a whole. [T15-W006]

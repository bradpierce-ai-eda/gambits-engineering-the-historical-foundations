Framing problems and organizing inquiry#

The sources here propose ways to frame problems, make models, organize discussion, design arrangements and examine consequences. Some sources contain their own extensive methodological syntheses.

Checkland: use declared activity models to question a situation#

Checkland's plenary summary describes the development of Soft Systems Methodology through action research in management situations. Its contrast is with a systems-engineering approach that takes objectives as given and systematically compares means. In the situations he describes, people act purposefully but do not necessarily share one objective or interpretation. SSM becomes a learning system rather than a procedure for optimizing an already agreed task. [SG-W001, sections I–VII]

Its purposeful-activity models are built according to declared worldviews. Several models can structure debate about a messy situation, helping people find accommodations that permit action. A model is therefore not simply asserted to be the uniquely true system hidden in the world. Connecting an activity model to information support asks what people would need to know to carry out that activity.

Checkland's 1999 retrospective summarizes his methodological development. [SG-W001]

Ulrich: the boundary determines which facts and values count#

Ulrich's mini-primer defines boundary critique as examining the judgments that make some observations and values relevant while excluding or subordinating others. Its twelve-question framework, also developed in the Ulrich–Reynolds chapter, distinguishes sources of motivation, control, knowledge and legitimacy. It asks about an actual arrangement and what ought to be the case, including the difference between people involved in a decision and people affected by it. [SG-W002]

The contribution is not a command to include everything. It is a way to make the selection discussable and expose a claim that disguises its boundary as necessary or neutral. Relevant expertise does not remove normative choices, while a person affected by a choice can question those choices without possessing all of an expert's knowledge. The primer presents this as useful in cooperative inquiry and in critical challenges where agreement is absent.

The source explicitly does not offer a self-contained method that settles every issue. [SG-W002]

Vickers: appreciation includes standards of fact and value#

Hudson's notes reconstruct Checkland's account of Vickers's appreciative systems. In this account, people respond to events and ideas through standards of fact and value, selecting what seems relevant and judging what relationships to maintain or change. Action can modify the situation, while experience can modify the standards through which later situations are interpreted. A situation is not first perceived without any selection and only afterward assigned a value. [SG-W010, text]

Hudson draws on Checkland's notes on Vickers, adding his own explanation.

The separate 1978 interview transcript supplies Vickers's own short discussion of people as agents and observers. It situates systems inquiry in the relationships people participate in, rather than only a detached description. [SG-W012]

Shchedrovitsky: distinguish thinking, communication and practical action#

The chapter by Georgy Petrovich Shchedrovitsky presents a thought-activity schema with three relatively autonomous belts. Practical collective thought-action concerns what people do together; thought-communication is the polyphonic exchange through which positions and meanings are articulated; schematic thinking works with organized representations. Understanding and reflection connect the belts. They are not three interchangeable names for the same process. [WME-W019, chapter and schemes]

The account of the 1980 game involving Meerovich makes this distinction concrete. A participant could share communication and collective action with other architects while insisting that his individual thinking differed. Separate boards made that difference visible. A common conversation is not evidence that all participants possess the same conceptual content. The scheme preserves individual and group representations rather than imposing identity by drawing one group box.

A schematic sign can retain a long chain of intellectual operations, but it still needs interpretation. The chapter describes reconstructing meaning, making it explicit through material boards or paper and learning the conventions through which a scheme can be used. Concision at the level of a diagram does not eliminate the history or competence needed to understand it.

The middle communication belt can accommodate conflict, questioning and multiple positions in a way different from formally checking a symbolic derivation. The author's concern is the work of problematization and coordination among heterogeneous practices. He also warns about isolating a belt: formal thought can become detached symbols, communication empty talk, and action mere repetition. These are his methodological evaluations. [WME-W019]

The organizational-activity game supplies a proposed operating process. Facilitated encounters expose incompatible practices, create opportunities for reflection, develop new schemes and lead toward programmes that participants enact and revise. It retains organizers, participant effort, conflict over days, shared material representations, learned norms, goal setting, testing and later institutionalization.

The chapter's conclusion also limits what its schema represents. It is an ideal theoretical basis for deriving models, rather than a model of one concrete real system. Its synthesis of thinking, communication and action is the author's philosophical and methodological proposal. The article was first published in the 1986 yearbook issued in 1987 and reprinted in Избранные трудыin 1995. [WME-W019]

Pyotr Shchedrovitsky's later thought-activity-ontology account develops a distinct formulation from Georgy Shchedrovitsky's original essay. [SG-W024]

Midgley and Lindhult: systemic innovation includes inquiry into purpose#

Midgley and Lindhult's article distinguishes several uses of systemic innovation: complementary technologies, enabling governance, sustainability transitions, collaborative value networks and systemic thinking-and-action practice. The authors favor the last as a way to engage with the others. This is their conceptual synthesis. [WME-W020, article, sections 1–8]

Their account relates boundaries, purposes and values in both directions. Values affect whom and what a study considers; established boundaries can also constrain which values count as legitimate. A system can be used as a tool for thinking without denying that the world has material consequences beyond the model. The authors' assumptions about interconnectedness, partial knowledge and action's consequences are philosophical starting points, not proved equations that determine every ethical judgment.

The critique applies to expertise and participation alike. Expert modeling can impose a client's purposes without examining affected interests. Participation can preserve bias, coercion, deception or groupthink. Dialogue is therefore not a sufficient condition of good outcomes. The article examines conflict, stereotypes, marginalization and the possibility that a ritual of participation conceals exclusion. [WME-W020]

The figures help articulate these relations: differing concerns, overlapping boundaries, conflict shaped by stereotypes and nested boundaries that marginalize people or issues. They are conceptual diagrams, not calibrated causal models. Broadening a boundary can also exceed available resources or collide with wider institutions.

Their examples include reframing in a water company, an imposed supplier-margin reduction followed by failure, and the marginalization of an innovation unit. These are author-reported vignettes. They illustrate why an apparently local improvement can fail within wider relations.

The article changes methodological questions across values, resource and risk distribution, delivery, scaling and governance, with a return to purpose when needed. It does not provide a universal algorithm for selecting the correct method. Internal expertise can sometimes replace an outside facilitator, while facilitation and knowledge work remain. The authors explicitly acknowledge no guarantee of success, call for evaluation and retain adaptation to context. [WME-W020]

Ackoff: idealized design, realization and decision rules#

Ackoff's guide distinguishes removing disliked deficiencies from designing a desired future. Its idealized design is not a claim to create a perfect organization unconstrained by reality. The guide separates idealization from means, resources, implementation and controls, and asks how a design can learn and improve. Eliminating an unwanted condition need not produce the desired one. [SG-W040, guide]

The organizational questions make retained responsibilities visible: which services to provide internally or acquire, how to assure quality, how to change facilities and staffing, and how to respond when the situation changes. A stated ideal is therefore an input to further work rather than a complete plan of construction.

Its final decision procedure is especially important. Practical agreement can permit action without agreement on every principle. If participants cannot agree on alternatives, the guide first proposes a test they agree to accept. If a test cannot be agreed or completed in time, the responsible manager declares a position. Agreement among the other participants can override that position; if they fail to agree, the manager's position enters the design. Authority remains in the procedure. [SG-W040, final decision pages]

The guide also seeks comments from stakeholders not involved in preparation and asks for explanations when suggestions are not incorporated. These are prescriptive procedures.

Inquiring organizations: different ways of warranting a claim#

Courtney, Croasdell and Paradice reconstruct Churchman's five inquirers and apply them to learning organizations. The Leibnizian account relies on derivation from axioms; the Lockean emphasizes observation and agreement; the Kantian combines models with data; the Hegelian develops knowledge through opposed interpretations; the Singerian treats measures and the inquiry arrangement as revisable. The different guarantors are the point of the comparison, rather than five names for a universally reliable search. [SG-W039, selected main review and table]

In the Singerian discussion, persistent disagreement can motivate sweeping in new variables or revising measures. That expands the inquiry rather than forcing agreement under a fixed representation. Yet the authors explicitly warn that a community's knowledge generation can drift toward its own illusion without monitoring. Their IT-support discussion retains debate, justification and checks on inquiry.

Courtney and colleagues reconstruct and apply Churchman's inquiring-systems account. [SG-W039]

Gigamapping, SODA and Interactive Management#

Sevaldson's Gigamapping account proposes rich, large mappings to explore complex relations across disciplinary boundaries. Creating the map is a way to investigate and communicate. [SG-W016, selected account]

Ackermann and Eden's selected SODA opening uses cognitive mapping to represent participants' construals and means–ends relationships. The central example helps organize an issue through connected concepts. A link records an understood relation in the inquiry; it is not thereby a demonstrated causal law. Map construction, merging, facilitation and political feasibility remain part of the method. [SG-W051]

The Warfield archive's Interactive Management exhibit is more concrete about the support arrangement. Sessions can require several days from selected participants, trained facilitators, a room with extensive display space and computational support. Structured questions and consensus methods organize contributions. The sponsoring organization helps identify the participants, so a room designed to hear every voice does not itself determine whose voice was invited. [SG-W060, main exhibit]

The Warfield archive reconstructs a structured group-inquiry process. Facilitators help participants develop and organize their judgments into a problem representation.

Meadows: leverage depends on the structure and purpose#

Meadows's essay distinguishes parameters from stocks, flows, delays, feedback, information, rules, self-organization, goals and paradigms. The stock–flow explanation makes a useful initial point: a stock changes through rates of inflow and outflow, and an intervention's timing can matter as much as an isolated numerical setting. The essay's ranking is a heuristic account of leverage, not a universal theorem. [W107, selected printed pp. 1–6 and 12–18]

Information-flow changes can expose consequences that actors previously did not see, while rules govern incentives and permitted action. The examples are reported anecdotes or interpretations. Changing a parameter is not always weak: the essay itself contains qualifications to that kind of ranking.

The selected goal and paradigm passages concern changing what a system is organized to do and the beliefs through which it is interpreted. Such a change may be consequential, but it changes the comparison. A new purpose cannot be counted as supplying the same outcome with fewer resources unless that equivalence is separately established. [W107]

The selected introduction and table of Crielaard and colleagues' 2026 review of twenty-nine studies discuss systems archetypes in public-health applications. Recurring feedback patterns can help formulate a hypothesis about a problem, but a causal-loop diagram does not itself validate an intervention. [W106]

Schwarz: a three-plane metamodel and six cycles#

Schwarz's 2005 formulation proposes a metamodel with physical, information and existential planes, associated with objects, relations and wholes. It distinguishes cycles supplying persistence—material circulation, homeostasis and self-reference—from morphogenesis, autopoiesis and autogenesis as change

processes. The metamodel organizes his proposed interpretation of viable systems and informs his intervention discussion. [SG-W003, central diagram and dynamics, PDF pp. 5–9]

Schwarz offers an authorial synthesis with ontological commitments. Its necessity across physical, biological, social and cognitive systems is asserted rather than established through comparative tests. [SG-W003]

Suh: functional independence and probability of success#

Suh's chapter introduces functional requirements, design parameters and process variables in distinct domains. Its independence axiom concerns satisfying the selected functional requirements without undesired coupling. Some designs can preserve independence directly, while others require a sequence that avoids interactions. The account concerns the relation among stated requirements and the parameters used to realize them. [SG-W052, chapter discussion of domains and design axioms]

The information axiom evaluates success using a probability-based information content. That quantity is not automatically knowledge possessed by an actor, sensing demand or total lifecycle cost. Functional independence is also different from statistical independence: multiplying probabilities requires assumptions about their joint relation.

Suh’s chapter introduces the axiomatic-design approach associated with his 1990 The Principles of Design. [W037, through SG-W052]

Altshuller and Shapiro, and a later QFD–function-analysis–TRIZ proposal#

The selected 1956 article organizes inventive work through analytic and operative stages, examining a technical difficulty and seeking changes or transfers that can overcome it. Its examples and stated limits provide an account of organizing search rather than treating invention as an unexplained inspiration. Later formulas of TRIZ ideality are not retroactively attributed to this early article. [W022, analytic and operative stages]

León-Rovira and Aguayo's later proposed integration assigns different tasks to QFD, function analysis and TRIZ. QFD helps identify what customers want and how existing functions relate to those wants; the authors explicitly say it is not a problem-solving tool. Mapping the relationships is nontrivial and requires experienced designers. The proposal therefore retains expertise inside a structured method. [SG-W057, integration of QFD, function analysis and TRIZ]

Their account reports problems when a team tries to define new functional parameters before establishing the existing relationships. The ideality expression combining benefits, costs and harms is a heuristic, not a universal commensurate measure. A ratio cannot establish comparable quantities or remove a conflicting requirement simply by being written down. The source's tool boundaries and reported inappropriate use matter as much as its promised integration.

Theory of Constraints: a bottleneck can move#

The practitioner account presents five focusing steps: identify a constraint, exploit it, subordinate other activity, elevate it and repeat when the constraint changes. The method asks why improving a nonbinding part may fail to improve overall throughput, and why relieving one constraint can make another important. Buffers and the distinction between investment and operating expense are part of the account. [SG-W019, five focusing steps and accounting distinctions]

Single-constraint rules have difficulties in product-mix problems with multiple constraints; ratio heuristics can then give misleading priorities. [SG-W019, practitioner account and abstract-level counterpoint]

Robust Decision Making: inspect vulnerabilities across possible futures#

RAND's account describes an iterative process of decision framing, evaluating strategies across futures, vulnerability analysis, tradeoff analysis and developing new futures or strategies. It seeks strategies that perform acceptably across a range rather than relying on one predicted future. A vulnerability summary can help stakeholders understand where a strategy fails. [SG-W031, main body and FAQ]

The futures are simulations, not automatically probabilities. Model construction, computation, storage, training and stakeholder judgments remain.

The EPTA method orientation addresses technology assessment through examination of consequences and options in relation to public decision making. The Collingridge dilemma concerns limited early knowledge versus costly later change. Constructive assessment is a response to that difficulty, not a guarantee that anticipatory knowledge can be made complete. [SG-W030]

Dori, DSRP, grey prediction and Extenics: representations with stated limits#

Dori's selected Object-Process Methodology opening relates structure and behavior in a common representation. Its bread-baking example connects objects, states and transforming processes rather than leaving a static parts list separate from action. Representational coherence can help an inquiry, but does not ensure that the model corresponds to the world. [SG-W058]

Cabrera and Cabrera's selected DSRP primer organizes questions through distinctions, systems, relationships and perspectives. These operations supply a vocabulary for making a model explicit and examining its implications. The primer advances universal claims and strong comparisons with other approaches. Its proposed ratio between model and reality does not specify operational resource quantities. [SG-W041]

Yang and colleagues' selected grey-prediction account uses satellite observations of vegetation recovery after a landslide. Classification and observation feed a particular modeling method with its own assumptions. [SG-W048]

Cai, Yang and He's publisher abstract describes Extenics as formalizing and transforming descriptions of contradictions or incompatible requirements. The application discussion develops this orientation. Changing a goal or constraint is different from satisfying the original task. [SG-W050, abstract and application discussion]

Brand: different rates of change can retain different functions#

Brand's selected pace-layering essay proposes that faster-changing layers explore or respond while slower layers preserve continuity and impose constraints. Their interaction, rather than uniform speed, is the explanatory idea. The forest and social-layer examples are conceptual illustrations, not proof that every durable system has one fixed hierarchy. [SG-W059, pace-layering discussion]

Slow structures can also obstruct needed change, and fast or slow does not constitute a moral ranking. The proposal supplies a way to ask about timing and retained memory.

Purposes, expertise and the organization of inquiry#

The sources explain substantial operations: making a boundary explicit, separating kinds of activity, modeling a requirement, inviting criticism, guiding a search or comparing a strategy across futures. They also retain the people and institutions that choose purposes, supply expertise, facilitate disagreement, build representations and test consequences.

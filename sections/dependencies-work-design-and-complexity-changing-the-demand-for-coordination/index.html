The question each arrangement answers#

The sources in this section examine how dependencies, uncertainty and the allocation of work affect coordination. Some redesign tasks so that fewer exceptions arise across boundaries. Others increase the capacity to handle information, distribute expertise or compare mechanisms for managing a dependency. Their criteria differ. Fewer messages, less delay, lower learning cost and broader control capability are not interchangeable results.

Galbraith: reduce information demand or increase processing capacity#

Jay Galbraith's 1974 article starts with a specified level of performance. When a task is well understood, much activity can be planned before execution. When uncertainty is greater, decisions about resources, schedules and priorities have to be made while work proceeds. More information then has to be processed among the relevant decision makers. Organizational design concerns the arrangements that permit the interdependent work to continue under those conditions. [W002, pp. 28–29]

Rules and programs precoordinate recurring situations. A worker can recognize the situation and apply an agreed response without communicating with everyone affected each time. Exceptions can be referred upward until they reach a level with a sufficiently broad perspective. Goals allow local discretion about behavior, but Galbraith's qualification is explicit: the tasks, budgets and completion dates still have to remain within the assumptions of the plan. New products, technologies or markets can invalidate the subgoals and generate renewed exceptions. [W002, p. 29]

The hierarchy has finite capacity. Galbraith distinguishes reducing the need to process information from increasing the ability to process it. Slack can absorb variation: longer lead times, inventories, budget allowances or other margins make fewer events demand immediate joint decisions. But slack has costs. Customers can wait longer, capital can be tied up and performance can be relaxed. Lower information demand obtained by accepting a less demanding output is not automatically an improvement at unchanged performance. [W002, pp. 30–31]

Self-contained tasks provide another demand-reduction strategy. Instead of grouping all specialists by resource or occupation, an organization can give a product, region, project or customer group much of what it needs to produce its output. Fewer conflicts over shared priorities then require cross-group scheduling. The tradeoff is reduced specialization, duplicated equipment or lost scale economies. Galbraith's example has groups do their own programming when each group needs too little work to sustain a separate full-time specialist. Some shared resources can remain centralized, so self-containment has degrees. [W002, pp. 31–32]

Vertical information systems take another route. They support more frequent replanning through clerks, computers, devices and decision capacity. Formalized language can allow more information to be carried in a given number of symbols under the model's assumptions. Yet more frequent reports can overload the recipient. Where relevant knowledge is qualitative and ambiguous, moving a decision toward the people who hold it can be more useful than repeatedly transmitting it upward. [W002, p. 32]

Lateral relationships include direct contact, liaison roles, task forces, teams and integrating roles. They can place joint decisions below the top of the hierarchy while preserving information about effects across departments. Nor does an informal relationship necessarily arise spontaneously across language, cultural or physical barriers. Galbraith treats its design as part of the task. [W002, pp. 32–35]

The article reports associations between uncertainty and integrative roles, including an adapted comparison of successful firms. These are reported cases and associations. Its concluding possibility of contracts, coalitions and influence over the environment also qualifies any claim that the four internal strategies exhaust all ways to reduce uncertainty. [W002, pp. 35–36]

De Sitter and colleagues: redesign production before allocating control#

L. Ulbo de Sitter, J. Friso den Hertog and Ben Dankbaar's 1997 article proposes integral organizational renewal. Its starting point is the architecture of functionally differentiated relationships. The authors object to designing separate social and technical systems and only afterward attempting to optimize their combination. Their proposed relations are simultaneously technical, social and normative. [W003, pp. 503–506]

They distinguish the likelihood that disturbances arise through an arrangement from its sensitivity to those disturbances. Production design can reduce the variation that has to be dealt with; control design supplies opportunities to respond effectively to what remains. Their controllability concerns the ability to formulate and achieve a range of objectives. It is not identical to the technical reachability definition in every other control theory or a guarantee of one specified outcome under every disturbance. [W003, pp. 505–510]

Parallelization divides incoming work into relatively separate flows, preferably around product–market combinations. Segmentation groups highly interdependent operations within a flow so that fewer interfaces require cross-boundary management. The aim is to avoid sending each order through many specialized departments with repeated scheduling and priority conflicts. The proposed economy comes from changing the pattern of dependencies, before designing a larger apparatus to handle the old pattern. [W003, pp. 510–512]

The examples preserve residual sharing. At St. Antonius, the three product flows retain common preparatory and final work. The insurance example groups work around regions and customers while distinguishing risk acceptance and claims. These arrangements are not wholly independent systems. Decisions about what to combine, what to separate and what to leave shared remain part of the design. [W003, p. 512, table I]

Complete-task groups retain flexible equipment, multiskilled work, internal coordination, process control, boundary control and responsibility for improvement. Their greater local scope requires capabilities. Training, reliable and timely information, memory, experience and learning remain necessary. A group can ask for external help. [W003, pp. 512–514]

The authors propose designing production structure before control structure, moving from overall flows through segments to groups, and selecting process technology in relation to those requirements. Control allocation then works upward from decisions that can be handled locally. The article also says that actual redesign is iterative and can take years. Leadership, design expertise, a shared language, participation and resources are implementation conditions rather than incidental additions to a finished chart. [W003, pp. 515–524]

The article uses schematic functions to relate control opportunities to required variation and reports that architecture can resolve up to eighty percent of control questions. It does not supply calibrated general prediction formulas for these claims. Its account explains how changing production relations can alter control demands. [W003, pp. 506, 509–512]

A commercially successful change toward a more customized product mix overloaded the former workflow. The redesign introduced four self-managing teams while retaining inspection, scheduling,

calculation and information systems. The authors report improved delivery reliability and productivity. [W003, appendix B, pp. 530–531]

The authors themselves discuss conflict, failed consensus, mistrust, participation used badly, mergers and exhausted funds. They warn that adoption and satisfaction do not establish validity or superiority and note the lack of adequate comparative evaluations. Their design scheme is a tool for developing a local arrangement. It is not a guarantee that interests align or that the implementation succeeds. [W003, pp. 525–529]

Simon: stable intermediate forms and conditional simplification#

Herbert Simon's The Architecture of Complexity connects assembly, dynamic interaction, description and problem solving. Hierarchy means nested subsystems and need not mean an authority relationship. Interaction strength matters more than simple physical distance. [W015, pp. 467–469]

The watchmaker parable concerns interruption. If partial work falls apart whenever an interruption occurs, completing a complex product can require repeatedly starting over. Stable intermediate subassemblies allow progress to be retained. The illustrative comparison shows why the availability of intermediate stable forms can affect the path to complexity. Its numerical ratio is a toy result under its specified probabilities and assembly assumptions, not an estimate of biological evolution or manufacturing productivity. [W015, pp. 470–473]

The associated search argument also requires recognizable partial success. A solver must be able to identify useful components or intermediate states and preserve them. Arbitrarily partitioning a difficult problem does not ensure that local correctness can be recognized independently or that components will combine. Simon's account of experience and reproduction retains information about solutions already found. [W015, pp. 471–473]

He explicitly preserves energetic conditions in biological discussion. Organisms are not energetically closed, and intermediate forms may be stable only while an external source of free energy continues. Classical thermodynamic considerations alone do not provide the direction and rate of evolution described by the assembly argument. [W015, p. 471 and governing notes]

Near-decomposability is another conditional property. Interactions within subsystems are strong relative to those among subsystems. Short-run behavior can then be approximately independent, while long-run behavior depends on more aggregate relationships. Simon's heat example uses a building insulated from its surroundings, rooms with relatively resistant walls and cubicles with weaker partitions. Temperatures equilibrate within rooms before the building reaches its overall equilibrium. [W015, pp. 474–475]

After the within-room transients have subsided, a smaller set of room-level measurements can suffice for the relevant dynamic description. The simplification depends on the timescale and approximation tolerated. Simon calls near-decomposability a strong property and qualifies his economic illustration with a consumption subsystem broadly connected to others. A hierarchy on paper therefore does not establish the required separation of coupling strengths or timescales. [W015, pp. 474–477]

The description argument uses actual redundancy. Repeated subpatterns can be named and reused, and a generating law can sometimes replace an explicit account of every successive state. Simon's array illustration replaces sixty-four displayed symbols with a thirty-five-symbol description under its stated notation. Constructing the notation, interpreting it and retaining adequate information remain conditions of use. [W015, pp. 477–479]

An entirely unredundant object may be its own shortest description. Simon also raises the observation problem: perhaps hierarchical things are more understandable partly because less hierarchical aspects escape attention. His distinction between a desired state and a process that produces it further preserves

invention and search. Recognizing an acceptable result is different from possessing a recipe for obtaining it. [W015, pp. 478–482]

Simon develops three mechanisms: stable intermediate assemblies retain progress after interruption; weak intersystem coupling permits approximate local treatment; and redundancy permits a reusable description. Each addresses a different part of the complexity problem. [W015]

Malone and Crowston: the dependency and the mechanism#

Thomas Malone and Kevin Crowston define coordination as managing dependencies between activities. Their 1994 article develops a vocabulary for identifying those dependencies across human, computational and other settings. The definition includes competition, conflicting interests and political processes insofar as they manage interdependent activities. It does not assume that all participants cooperate toward one agreed good. [WME-W011, pp. 90–92]

Different dependencies require different work. Activities may compete for a shared resource. A consumer may need a producer's result, which introduces prerequisite, transfer and usability issues. Activities may need simultaneous occurrence, or a task may depend on subtasks. The authors describe the list as nonexhaustive. What looks like one object dependency becomes more precise when the activities that require or change that object are identified. [WME-W011, pp. 92–97]

Transfer is not just communication. Moving a material output can require transportation, storage and handling. Timing production closely can reduce storage, while an inventory can buffer timing differences. A standard can make an output usable without renewed joint design, while participative design can address requirements that were not settled in advance. [WME-W011, pp. 94–95]

The activity description is selective. A whole division can be treated as one activity for one question and individual stations as activities for another. The authors deny that there is a single correct granularity. Evaluation criteria also have to be identified: speed, accuracy, cost and the treatment of conflicting goals can lead to different assessments. More coordination is not always worth its cost. [WME-W011, pp. 100– 101]

The survey reports related models comparing production, coordination and vulnerability costs. A central allocator can reduce some communication while creating exposure to a central failure. Decentralized bidding requires announcements, bids, awards and estimates. Dedicated product hierarchies can retain unused capacity. These comparisons are reported through Malone and Crowston’s review under the stated model assumptions. [WME-W011, pp. 98–100]

Cross-domain transfer needs additional care. Human motivation, incentives, emotion, reputation and the capacities of the communication system can change a proposed mechanism's effects. The article distinguishes varying a model's parameters from adding behavioral qualifications to a simple baseline. Shared terminology is an invitation to investigate the comparison, not evidence that people and processors are identical. [WME-W011, pp. 91, 100–101]

The discussion of cheaper coordination is especially consequential. Lower unit cost can substitute technology for human activity, increase the quantity of coordination used, and enable more coordination-intensive structures. The authors describe a case where removing a layer of managers was followed by the creation of roughly as many specialist staff positions, some devoted to developing the new systems. Their interpretation concerns reallocation toward more complex analysis. [WME-W011, pp. 102–103]

Tools also retain contribution and interpretation problems. A shared database can be technically accessible while experts have incentives to withhold useful knowledge. Rules for filtering messages, making commitments, choosing tasks and maintaining standards still require organization. Distributed

computer mechanisms likewise incur resource bookkeeping and message overhead. [WME-W011, pp. 105–109]

The article ends with a research programme concerning dependency types, the composition of processes and comparison across design spaces. The source offers a connected vocabulary and partial results, with questions of generality still open. [WME-W011, pp. 110–114]

Garicano: specialize learning and pay for communication#

Luis Garicano's production model examines how people can specialize in knowledge. A worker who encounters problems need not learn to solve every possible problem personally if unresolved cases can reach someone with the relevant knowledge. Specialized learning can broaden the knowledge usable by a productive organization while reducing what each person needs to acquire. Escalation, matching and communication then have costs. [W030, selected introduction and model, pp. 875–880]

The setup specifies a problem distribution and explicit learning and communication costs. Those assumptions determine which problems are routine enough for production workers and which justify specialized assistance. The relevant claim is conditional organization of knowledge. [W030, §II]

Radner: processor count and delay are joint criteria#

Roy Radner's The Organization of Decentralized Information Processing models programmed networks of limited processors. Associative operations allow portions of an information-processing task to be carried out and combined. A network can then be assessed through both the number of processors and the delay before a result is available. A reduction in one measure need not settle the other. [TA-W040, pp. 1109– 1115]

The model uses prescribed programs and timing, identical limited processors and a fixed amount of environmental data. Communication has no additional cost in that formulation. The selected account consequently cannot be used as a whole organizational cost analysis that includes every communication and learning burden. Information-processing decentralization is also distinct from decentralized authority. [TA-W040, introduction and §2]

Distributed control has a setup phase#

Debbie Harrison, Kristin Munksgaard and Frans Prenkert propose distributed control for coordinating dissimilar but complementary activities across actors. Their selected discussion distinguishes the intensive interaction needed to establish the arrangement from thinner, potentially digitally mediated later interaction. Actors can achieve partial local goals while participating in a wider interdependent activity structure. [UX-W002, abstract and “A new coordination principle”]

The distinction makes setup substantive. Establishing relationships, specifying interfaces and finding workable accommodations precedes the apparent ease of later coordination. The circular-economy and blockchain discussions illustrate a conceptual proposal. [UX-W002]

Alter: work systems include customers and continuing adaptations#

Steven Alter's 2019 account defines a work system through participants or machines performing processes and activities with information, technology and other resources to produce products or services for customers. The framework treats these elements in relation rather than forcing every situation into separately designed social and technical systems. Its nine-element representation also includes the surrounding environment, infrastructure and strategy. [SG-W015, selected pp. 1–3, 5–13]

The lifecycle includes planned projects and unplanned adaptations or workarounds. A specification can fail to describe useful work, and compliance with it can obstruct the actual purpose. Partial and total automation change participants and activities without making the rest of the work system disappear. The article's emphasis on products, services and customers also makes it possible to distinguish a provider's local efficiency from the customer's total costs or ability to obtain value. [SG-W015, selected lifecycle, workaround and service discussions, including p. 19]

Alter presents a descriptive and design framework with methods and local hypotheses. [SG-W015]

Sociotechnical groups in citizenship services#

Almir Cordeiro Júnior and Mônica Bianco apply a sociotechnical work-group perspective to conflict-resolution services in integrated citizenship centers in Vitória. The selected abstract and final discussion extend the subject beyond factory flows. Semi-autonomous service work remains dependent on technical knowledge, relationships among people and learning. The service's social interaction is part of the work rather than interference that can simply be removed from a schedule. [W028, abstract and final discussion]

This organizational analysis asks what a work group must be capable of doing in a service whose cases and interpersonal requirements cannot be treated as identical manufactured units. [W028]

Joseph and Sengul: design problems are distinguishable#

John Joseph and Metin Sengul's specialist review distinguishes configuration, control, channelization and coordination. The selected sections treat organization design as more than drawing formal reporting lines. Choices about the arrangement of units, the guidance of behavior, the channels through which information and attention move, and the management of interdependence answer different questions. Their integrative argument retains contingent findings and differences in assumptions. [W046, “Contemporary Research, ” table 1, “State of Research, ” “Organization Design as a Theory, ” conclusion]

Sternieri: autonomy and shared dependencies#

Armando Sternieri's 2026 Dependency Restructuring distinguishes changes in activities from changes in dependency and coordination relationships. The abstract proposes that local autonomy can rise while reliance on common capabilities also rises. More freedom at a visible operating point does not by itself establish less dependence throughout the arrangement. [UX-W011, abstract and illustrative cases]

Sternieri presents the cases as illustrations rather than tests. [UX-W011, abstract and illustrative cases]

Production relations, local control and shared capability#

The selected explanations identify different places where work can change: recurring exceptions, production interfaces, retained partial assemblies, descriptive redundancy, shared resources, specialist learning and the processing of a fixed computation. They also identify different costs and failure conditions.

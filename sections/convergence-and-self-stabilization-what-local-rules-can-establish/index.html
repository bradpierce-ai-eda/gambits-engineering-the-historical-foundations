Two questions about distributed behavior#

Edsger Dijkstra's self-stabilization paper and the replicated-data-type report by Marc Shapiro, Nuno Preguiça, Carlos Baquero and Marek Zawirski examine different global properties obtained through constrained local operations. Dijkstra asks whether a distributed state can enter a legitimate set from an arbitrary initial state and remain legitimate. The CRDT report asks when replicas that receive the same updates give equivalent answers despite asynchronous exchange. [W006; W061]

The permitted local actions replace some continuing global direction. Dijkstra specifies a scheduler and local interfaces. The CRDT report specifies update/merge properties and eventual delivery.

Dijkstra: recovery differs from preserving a good initial state#

In his 1974 two-page paper, Dijkstra begins with synchronization as preservation of legitimate global states. A system can be designed so that correctly admitted steps preserve legitimacy once established. That leaves another problem: an illegitimate state reached through an error may persist indefinitely. Self-stabilization adds recovery from any initial state admitted by the finite-machine model. Thus a local rule that keeps an already correct execution correct has not yet met the stronger requirement. [W006, p. 643]

His model places finite-state machines at nodes of a connected graph, with each able to inspect its own state and those of its neighbors. A privilege is a Boolean condition on that available state. When the privilege holds, its associated move is enabled. A central daemon chooses one present privilege, that machine changes state, and the process repeats. The central daemon models arbitrary relative speeds through nondeterministic choice of successive moves. Replacing it with a distributed daemon is expressly outside this short paper. “Distributed control” therefore does not mean the model has no global scheduling assumption. [W006, p. 643]

Legitimacy is a criterion on the joint state, although a node makes its move from local information. Dijkstra requires legitimate states to have at least one enabled privilege and to remain legitimate under every permitted next move. He also imposes conditions connecting the privileges and legitimate states. Self-stabilization then requires that some privilege is always present and that every allowed succession of daemon choices reaches a legitimate state after finitely many moves, regardless of the initial state. The quantification over allowed choices matters: one fortunate simulation is not the result being asserted. [W006, p. 643]

This is a recovery guarantee under fixed rules and scheduling semantics. Arbitrary initial state does not mean a continuing external agent can corrupt state at every step while the system is guaranteed to remain legitimate. Nor does an arbitrary corruption of the machine's transition rules fit the same model merely because its register contents were allowed to start arbitrarily.

The ring constructions retain an asymmetry#

Dijkstra supplies three ring constructions and says their full proofs and much of the discovery process are omitted from the article. In the K-state construction, the distinguished bottom machine advances its state cyclically when it matches its predecessor. Other machines copy their predecessor when they differ. The legitimate states selected for the ring have exactly one privilege present. The source's sufficient state-space relation is K greater than N for N+1 machines under the central daemon. The distinction of the bottom machine is consequential: this is not a construction of wholly identical nodes each executing an identical rule. [W006, pp. 643–644 and note 1]

A small legitimate trace helps explain the mechanism without purporting to prove recovery. Take three machines, four state values, and write the states in bottom-to-top order. Starting at (0,0,0), only the bottom machine's equality condition is enabled. It advances to 1, giving (1,0,0). The next machine now differs from its predecessor and copies it, giving (1,1,0). The third then copies, giving (1,1,1), where the bottom is again enabled. The single privilege has moved through the ring. This displayed trace illustrates behavior within the legitimate set; it does not establish the universal finite recovery assertion from all states or all daemon choices. [W006, K-state construction; explanatory instance]

The three-state and four-state constructions use different neighbor conditions, with special endpoint roles. Their presence shows that the short paper is offering concrete ways of meeting a global criterion with locally determined moves, rather than only naming self-stabilization. The constructions distinguish between closure of the legitimate set and convergence into it, together with the precise local-information and scheduling conditions used to demonstrate the possibility. [W006, p. 644]

For gambits engineering, the relevant material is consequently more specific than a slogan about self-repair. State and permitted relations can be arranged so that recovery need not require a separate controller to inspect the entire state and issue each corrective instruction. The construction nevertheless retains a selected notion of legitimacy, special rules, sufficient state space and an admitted order of moves.

Shapiro and colleagues: equal histories must give equivalent answers#

The 2011 INRIA report defines eventual convergence through both safety and liveness. Replicas with the same causal history must have equivalent abstract states, meaning their query operations give the same values. Updates must also eventually reach the causal histories of the other replicas. Identical answers obtained only by permanently ignoring some updates would not satisfy the combined condition. Nor does temporary disagreement before propagation automatically violate it. The property concerns histories and eventual communication in an asynchronous system. [W061, §§2.1–2.3]

The surrounding model allows network partitions and later recovery. A process can crash and recover while its memory survives. Byzantine behavior is excluded. These are substantial assumptions about what the arrangement must tolerate. [W061, §2]

The report gives two sufficient routes to convergence. State-based replication sends state, while operation-based replication sends operations. They place different requirements on the payload and communication. [W061, §§2.2–2.3, 3.1]

State-based merging retains order through the payload#

For a state-based convergent replicated data type, payload states form a join semilattice: any two states have a least state above both in the specified partial order. Merge returns that least upper bound. Local updates advance monotonically in the order. This supplies a way to retain all relevant progress while combining states. The order concerns the representation, rather than necessarily an increase in every visible application quantity. A type that supports a decreasing visible value may need a representation whose accumulated information still advances. [W061, §2.3.1]

Least-upper-bound merge is associative, commutative and idempotent. Changing the order of combining states, grouping several combinations, or receiving the same state again does not change the abstract combined result. These properties relieve the communication channel of some demands: individual messages can be duplicated, reordered or lost if sufficiently new state eventually reaches every replica, directly or through other merges. They do not remove the eventual-delivery requirement. A permanently missing update cannot be inferred from these algebraic properties. [W061, §2.3.1, proposition 2.1]

The proof's core comparison is between replicas with the same accumulated histories. Their respective merges contain the same updates, and commutativity of the least upper bound gives equivalent abstract states. Liveness supplies the eventual arrival needed to obtain those histories. The proof is therefore informative about how the obligations fit together. [W061, proposition 2.1]

A convergent scalar can still be the wrong counter#

The report's counter example shows why satisfying a convergence pattern is not enough to meet an arbitrary type specification. A single integer with merge by maximum and local increments forms a monotonic semilattice. Yet if two replicas start at zero and each increments to one, their maximum is one. If the desired counter should reflect both increments, that result loses one of them. The replicas can agree perfectly on an answer that fails the intended count. [W061, §3.1.2]

The state-based grow-only counter instead gives each source replica an entry in a vector. A replica increments its own entry; merge takes the componentwise maximum; query sums the entries. For two replicas, independent local states (1,0) and (0,1) merge to (1,1), and the query returns two. Receiving the same vector again makes no further change. Source-specific components preserve distinctions that a single maximum discarded. The explanation depends on the specified source identities and ownership of increments; arbitrary writers changing another source's entry would be a different operation set. [W061, §3.1.2, specification 6; two-replica explanatory instance]

This version also assumes the payload does not overflow and the set of replicas is known. It is a precise example of using a richer maintained representation to simplify combination. The vector retains additional state. The report states that its portfolio specifications favor clarity over efficiency and notes that equivalent representations can use less space. Choosing an actual representation and its maintenance still involves work beyond recognizing the semilattice property. [W061, §3 introduction and §3.1.2]

Operation-based replication has another channel obligation#

For an operation-based commutative replicated data type, operations not ordered by the required delivery relation must commute. Their permitted execution orders then give equivalent abstract states. The report assumes reliable broadcast that delivers operations in the order specified by the type. For the studied types, causal delivery is sufficient; some types can use weaker order if more pairs commute. The state-based tolerance of repeated state messages is therefore not a license to apply arbitrary operation messages twice without checking their semantics. [W061, §2.3.2]

Suitable operation algebra and delivery order replace a need to agree on a single order for all concurrent operations. The surrounding implementation still has to ensure that updates reach replicas and obey the chosen constraints.

Immediate updates, storage and later synchronization#

The report itself identifies costs beyond foreground synchronization. Some proposed designs grow without bound, and garbage collection can require a weaker form of synchronization. It treats that synchronization as off the critical path and not essential to liveness, rather than claiming the storage issue absent. It also discusses the need for synchronization for some less common operations as a direction beyond the main study. [W061, introduction]

The preceding coordination-avoidance account gives the separate application-validity question a concrete form. Idempotent union can preserve repeated-merge behavior while violating a unique-identifier invariant. Here, similarly, an integer maximum can converge while failing the intended total count.

Dijkstra's recovery property remains distinct again: agreement among replicas says nothing by itself about recovery from arbitrary corrupt initial states.

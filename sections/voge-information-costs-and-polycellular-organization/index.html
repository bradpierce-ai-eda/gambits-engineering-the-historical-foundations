An original article and a chain of development#

Jean Voge's 1985 Crise, information et communication dans l'économie américaine connects organizational architecture with communication, selection, regulation and costs. The abstract of his 1983 article identifies the related information-economy inquiry, while later accounts interpret his 1997 Le complexe de Babel. [UX-W031; W094, abstract; RGC-W031, through UX-W028 and RGC-W030]

Hubert Landier's Polycellular Human Organisation develops this line of thought organizationally. Le Moigne's book review, the French Senate's discussion and Villiers's information-economy argument document other routes of development. [UX-W030; UX-W028; UX-W029; RGC-W030]

Voge: information work can offset productive gains#

Voge's 1985 article divides material production from informational activity and asks how gains within material production can coexist with weak overall productivity. His proposed explanation includes rising organizational information and communication costs. Technical improvements to processing alone need not settle the issue if the organization creates increasing quantities of work to select, route and use information. The article concerns the arrangement of activity as well as its technical tools. [UX-W031, printed 39–47]

The historical macroeconomic argument uses a particular classification. In the notes Voge explicitly assumes, following Jonscher, that government information services such as education, research, documentation, regulation and administration should be treated with material final demand because material goods and services are their principal beneficiaries. That decision affects the resulting account. It is an assumption about classification, not an observed natural boundary dividing all beneficial information from overhead. [UX-W031, 46, notes]

The article's statistics, explanation of the early-1980s recovery and prediction of its duration are historical claims. The selected contribution is an explicit hypothesis about productive work and organizational demands, with its classification visible. [UX-W031, 39–46]

Branching and meshed communications differ#

Voge contrasts a branching communication structure with a cellular mesh. In the first, a downward message follows prescribed branches, while a message traveling upward follows a limited route through intermediaries. Selection is concentrated, and upper points can become bottlenecks. In the mesh, alternative routes and selective choices can exist at several points. The distinction is about where a choice of path is available. [UX-W031, 44–45]

His telephone comparison concerns particular quantities. He contrasts the number of switchable contacts needed for a caller to reach others, scaling linearly with subscriber count in the described pyramid and potentially logarithmically in the cellular arrangement. He separately contrasts operating costs as call volume grows. These are different measures. The comparison depends on switch design, ports, capacity, blocking, reliability and traffic. [UX-W031, 45]

The proposed organizational transfer decentralizes selection and supplies feedback at several levels. Voge favors interwoven serial and parallel processes and interactive dialogue instead of treating an organization solely as the execution channel for leaders' ideas. This is a connected architectural proposal. A communication diagram alone does not establish whether its human participants share purposes, possess relevant skills or can bear the assigned work. [UX-W031, 45–47]

The thermodynamic correspondence is a stronger claim#

Voge relates his economic account to thermodynamic models in which dissipative terms depend quadratically on forces or flows and useful power can peak at a particular point. He transfers the correspondence to informational production and a fifty-percent maximum. These are the author's analogies and proposed generalizations. Entropy production, dissipated power and monetary value are not treated here as interchangeable physical quantities. [UX-W031, 43–44]

Voge’s extension from a thermodynamic analogy to universal minimization of entropy production requires premises beyond the comparison. The architectural account instead specifies where information work occurs and how it can offset productive gains. [UX-W031, 43–47]

Landier's book: ideal types and actual informal work#

Landier's 2024 book revises material from the distinct 1987 L'entreprise polycellulaire through a later transdisciplinary framework. [UX-W030, prefatory material, PDF 13]

The pyramid and polycellular organization function as ideal types. Real organizations already contain informal relations, local improvisation and support work missing from their official diagrams. Landier's account of a strict pyramid says that employees often make it function by departing from impractical instructions. Each level can hide local accommodations from another because an intervention that appears rational above can be disruptive below. The actual coordination is richer than the formal reporting lines. [UX-W030, PDF 14–32, 51–59]

The proposed change therefore concerns both organization and representation. Calling an organization pyramidal can obscure the initiatives that sustain it; renaming it a network does not necessarily change its practices. Restructuring also encounters job losses, learning requirements, uncertainty and resistance tied to status or power. [UX-W030, opening chapters and PDF 57–59]

Landier draws the Voge argument directly from the 1985 article. His discussion of overflowing inboxes and intermediaries gives a contemporary-to-the-book interpretation of the same information-burden concern. The repeated thermodynamic and fifty-percent formulations remain dependent reception of that argument, not independent physical demonstrations. [UX-W030, PDF 57–59]

Proliferation, multiple routes and conditional autonomy#

The polycellular proposal has three connected principles. Units develop around a common project rather than simply filling a fixed chart. Communication has multiple possible paths. Local units retain operational autonomy conditional on effectiveness in relation to the shared project. The conditions distinguish the proposal from an arbitrary aggregate of independent teams. [UX-W030, PDF 60–64]

Multiple routes can permit communication after some failures, but require resources and appropriate surviving paths. Survival depends on node failures, loads and shared dependencies. The benefit must be specified against the disturbances and loads at issue. [UX-W030, PDF 62–63, fig. 9]

The autonomy principle explicitly retains central and intermediate responsibility. Management affirms the common project, monitors effectiveness and can intervene, reorganize or eliminate an ineffective unit. Apart from this corrective responsibility, it is meant to leave internal arrangements to the unit. Thus the proposal reallocates decisions. [UX-W030, PDF 64]

Development also involves initiative from the center and the periphery. A local unit can encounter possibilities unknown above, while broader management must be able to recognize and connect them. The intended organization depends on responses in both directions. [UX-W030, PDF 65–70]

Local knowledge substitutes for some directives#

Salespeople, technicians, buyers and production workers encounter different parts of an organization's environment. Their knowledge can be lost or delayed when every appropriate response must await a distant directive. Landier proposes local initiative because the person at the point of encounter can have relevant information unavailable to management. The argument concerns a particular distribution of knowledge and decision opportunity. [UX-W030, PDF 99–100]

The substitution increases other demands. Local members must know the organization's purposes and relate their actions to them. Internal communication, shared standards, selection and evaluation replace some detailed instructions. Landier also says the balance differs between activities such as accounting control and research. [UX-W030, PDF 99–102]

Common codes require work. When one level's information becomes unintelligible at another, the proposed responses include adopting a code, developing a compromise or negotiating a new relation. Surveys, audits, training and decoding support that adjustment. Disruption can destroy the organization or provoke intervention from outside it. [UX-W030, PDF 88–103, especially 94]

Leadership and arbitration remain functions#

Landier explicitly rejects the inference that local autonomy automatically gives an optimal outcome. Apparently equal relations can conceal age, status or other informal hierarchies, and conflicts can require legitimate arbitration. Participants can also have personal interests inconsistent with the common project, while relevant information is not automatically transparent. Removing an official supervisor does not remove these questions. [UX-W030, PDF 81–82]

His team-climate account associates better conditions with available leadership, opportunities for peer exchange and information situating the team in the wider organization. Worse conditions include inaccessible managers, absent exchange and deficient information. The passage supplies the author's practical account of retained support. [UX-W030, PDF 82–83]

Networks themselves also have several possible characters. They predate the Internet and can support secrecy, clientelism or coercion as well as cooperation. Technology can enable a relation without ensuring its purpose or quality. The book's political or organizational forecasts are proposals and interpretations. [UX-W030, PDF 73–87]

A common project is made and maintained#

Landier criticizes a corporate-project exercise that ends in slogans and a brochure. A management statement is not automatically the project of all participants. Bringing it to life requires taking seriously the perspectives of individuals, teams, establishments and the organization, together with capital providers, customers, suppliers and public authorities. Participatory development permits outcomes management cannot fully specify in advance. [UX-W030, PDF 112–123]

The process can take years as an identity develops. It is iterative across levels: a site can formulate a project before a corporate document exists, and local work can contribute to the broader formulation. The relation is therefore not simply a complete goal flowing downward through a chart. Negotiation, mediation, labor representation and continuing revision are part of the intended arrangement. [UX-W030, PDF 112–123]

The expressions A = f(C) and C = f(A) represent a mutual dependence between autonomy and coherence. Appropriate initiative requires room to act, while continued autonomy depends on effectiveness and

relation to the common purpose. The functions, units and empirical optimum are not specified. The notation states a conceptual dependency. [UX-W030, PDF 118–119]

The individual is also more than an organizational component. Family, personal existence and other commitments have purposes that cannot be absorbed without remainder into a corporate aim. Landier makes manipulation and sacrifice of participants ethical problems. His biological analogy is expressly limited by the status of human beings as subjects. [UX-W030, PDF 70–72, 117–118]

Institutional boundaries do not contain every capability#

Outsourcing can assign a function to a complementary organization rather than develop it internally. Landier's account retains the provider's viability and the reciprocal conditions of continued exchange. A client can weaken a supplier through its terms, but doing so can damage the wider productive relation on which the client depends. The local disappearance of a function is therefore not a disappearance of its provision. [UX-W030, PDF 69–70, 85–86]

Cross-firm cooperation has explicit conditions: transaction costs, continuity, confidentiality, trust, shared concepts and tools, and an appropriate institutional setting. Legal and public institutions participate in the supporting arrangement. A nominally independent network of suppliers still requires negotiation and may contain severe asymmetries. The book's expected gains are conditional organizational proposals. [UX-W030, PDF 85–86]

The time discussion identifies waiting, repeated reporting and conflicts among horizons as effects of architecture. Managers must bridge several time scales, and plans need adjustment. Its Toyota account retains stocks, signals, foreman regulation and learning between upstream and downstream activities. [UX-W030, PDF 104–111]

The broader societal discussion includes externalities and a reported generalized travel-time calculation that counts acquisition, earning and maintenance as well as movement. The particular historical calculation is mediated. Its relevance is the stated boundary of the account. Landier's planetary and human-care commitments remain normative positions. [UX-W030, PDF 124–139, especially 136]

Le Moigne's review of Babel#

The review of Le complexe de Babel praises its transdisciplinary reach and recommends the chapter on diminishing returns to information and human capital. Le Moigne emphasizes cooperative production of meaning rather than sheer proliferation of signs. He also objects that Voge gives some contemporary management fashions too much credit. [UX-W028]

The Senate section adds supporting institutions#

The pertinent section of the French Senate's 1998 report reproduces the contrast between pyramids and networks and relates it to the Internet. Its conclusion also retains trust, education, market and state regulation, public participation in infrastructure and substantial intangible investment. Technology, a public, enterprises and a culture must come together for a medium to develop. The report therefore does not explain a communication society by the network alone. [UX-W029, section 2]

Its expectations about democracy, peace, technological progress and European opportunity are historical institutional forecasts. [UX-W029]

Villiers: the fifty-percent result has assumptions#

Villiers's professional essay reuses Babel to examine information employment and productivity. It supposes a proportional relation Q/L = kXi, where Xi is information employment as a share of total employment, and assumes average wages grow proportionally with output per worker. With the chosen division between informational costs and material benefit, the account arrives at a maximum. These are explicit modeling premises. [RGC-W030, information-economy discussion and notes]

The treatment of final informational consumption affects the result. Changing what counts as an intermediate informational cost changes the claimed maximum. Aggregate output, per-worker normalization and wage coefficients also require clarification before numerical reuse. [RGC-W030]

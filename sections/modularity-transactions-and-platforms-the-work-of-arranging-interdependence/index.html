Technology and organizations#

Carliss Baldwin's Design Rules, Volume 2: How Technology Shapes Organizations develops a connected account of how technical arrangements affect organizations, economic relationships and opportunities for innovation. It begins with tasks and transfers, moves through the design of interfaces and transaction boundaries, and then examines complementary investments, production bottlenecks, platforms and software communities. [UX-W040, chs. 1–4, 17]

The relevant economy is often a reduction in the communication or negotiation required for particular work. But the book also explains why organizational ties remain necessary, why integrated production can outperform dispersed production, why firms may need knowledge beyond what they make, and how a focal firm's favorable results can involve costs borne elsewhere. [UX-W040, chs. 2–3, 5–7, 11–12, 14–17]

Parnas: choosing what a module hides#

David Parnas asks which criteria should govern decomposition of a system into modules. In the selected sections of his 1972 article, the important alternative is between dividing a program according to successive processing stages and dividing it according to design decisions that other parts should not have to know. A module is assigned responsibility for a decision liable to change; its interface allows other modules to use the relevant service without depending on the concealed implementation. The resulting economy concerns the spread of design knowledge and the reach of subsequent changes. It does not follow merely from giving different groups different pieces of a flowchart. [W004, “The Criteria, ” “Conclusion”]

Parnas's comparison uses a keyword-in-context indexing program. A decomposition corresponding closely to processing stages can expose choices about representations and intermediate structures to several modules. The alternative arranges responsibility so that such choices can change with less disturbance elsewhere. Even an interface can reveal too much: requiring a particular ordering of circular shifts, for example, commits users to an implementation-related decision that they may not need. The question is what another module must be entitled to assume. Once an unnecessary assumption becomes part of the interface, changes propagate through that assumption. [W004, decomposition comparison and “The Criteria”]

He keeps two further issues separate. A hierarchical relation among modules does not establish that the modules were decomposed well; hierarchy and effective information hiding are distinct properties. Implementation efficiency also requires attention. A conceptually attractive interface can impose costs if realized in an unsuitable way. These programming examples therefore support an account of limiting particular dependencies and change propagation. [W004, “Efficiency and Implementation, ” “Hierarchical Structure”]

From technical recipes to networks of tasks and transfers#

Baldwin starts from technology as knowledge of a demonstrated way to serve a human purpose. A technical recipe specifies actions and their relationships. Its implementation requires agents capable of performing the tasks and the transfers between them. Transfers can concern material, energy or information. Depicting only transformation tasks while omitting the actions needed to move things between them would leave out part of the recipe. This representation directs attention to what must actually happen for a result to be obtained. [UX-W040, chs. 1–2]

The task network can be represented in a design structure matrix. Tasks occupy corresponding rows and columns; entries record declared dependencies. Densely connected blocks and the connections between blocks give a more informative picture than an undifferentiated list of stages. A sequence, a reciprocal design exchange and a group linked by shared rules impose different requirements on the people carrying out the work. The matrix is a selected representation of those requirements. It does not itself establish that every consequential dependency has been found. [UX-W040, ch. 2]

The DSM community's tutorial supplies the elementary reading conventions. Parallel activities have no dependency of the represented kind between them. A sequential relation orders activities; reciprocal relations indicate coupling and potentially iteration. Clustering and sequencing can help identify groups that should be managed together or expose iterations concealed by a simple forward schedule. The tutorial also warns that transposed matrix conventions are both used. A reader must establish whether a marked cell means that its row receives from its column or the reverse before interpreting a diagram. [SG-W021, main tutorial]

Independence in a dependency matrix is relative to the relationship represented. A matrix of information requirements can leave material dependencies, shared-resource contention, and the strength and frequency of interactions unspecified. Those relations require their own description. [SG-W021, matrix conventions and parallel/sequential/coupled relations]

Baldwin's example of a disk-drive producer and a laptop producer makes the distinction concrete. Their manufacturing processes can be substantially separate while their designs remain highly interdependent. Questions about commands, timing, connections and compatibility may require repeated exchanges before either design is settled. An answer can depend on another uncertain design choice. Such transfers are not equivalent to handing over a finished, specified object. Leaving out an important exchange may produce a drive that does not work inside the computer. [UX-W040, pp. 24–25]

The technical architecture in this account says what depends on what. The organizational architecture assigns the tasks to people or teams and supplies the ties through which they coordinate. Communication, colocation, common employment and dispute-resolution arrangements can make required transfers and adaptations possible. They are costly to establish and place continuing demands on participants. Baldwin's argument for placing ties where they are needed therefore begins with concrete technical relationships. [UX-W040, pp. 33–34]

Design rules replace selected lateral dependencies#

Modularization changes the network rather than accepting every dependency as fixed. In the disk-drive example, an agreed standard settles some interoperability questions before the two designs proceed. Each team can consult that standard in place of repeatedly negotiating the same decisions with the other team. Lateral dependencies are replaced by dependencies on rules. The rule-making work becomes a distinct part of the architecture, and each participating design remains bound by its requirements. [UX-W040, p. 25]

Information hiding follows from this allocation of responsibility. A supplier need not disclose every internal decision if the delivered component satisfies an adequate interface. Other participants can rely on what the interface specifies. That reliance is conditional: a previously hidden interaction, an incomplete specification or a changed technical possibility can make consultation necessary again. Baldwin explicitly draws the disk-drive modularization as incomplete. Standards can lag technology, and vendors may prefer to leave some features unsettled. Standard-setting organizations themselves can experience delay and opportunistic obstruction. [UX-W040, pp. 25–26]

Some questions can be answered once through a design rule and reused in several designs. In Baldwin's modular architecture, rule dependencies connect the modules, which are brought together and tested as a system. [UX-W040, chs. 2, 7]

This explains why modularization can support change without making change costless. A designer may explore an internal alternative while preserving the external commitments. If the alternative alters a commitment or reveals an unrepresented dependency, its effects can extend beyond the module. The architecture has supplied a conditional area for independent action. It has not licensed arbitrary changes to every part of the technical recipe. [UX-W040, chs. 2, 7, 14]

Transfers, transactions and the work avoided inside a zone#

Baldwin distinguishes a transfer from a transaction. The latter adds work of defining what is exchanged, counting or otherwise measuring it, and arranging compensation. These are mundane transaction costs, distinct from costs associated with opportunism. Even an exchange completed quickly can depend on extensive supporting infrastructure: product identification, records, payment systems and conventions about ownership. Treating the exchange as frictionless would hide the very work that the account seeks to locate. [UX-W040, ch. 2]

Thin crossing points are places where relatively few, well-specified transfers connect otherwise dense parts of the task network. They make the added work of transactions more manageable. A complicated, contingent design discussion is a thick crossing point: defining every requested answer, checking its completeness and pricing it separately would be difficult. Rules can make a crossing thinner, but transactions remain possible at thicker crossings when contracts and relationships supply the necessary support. The claim is comparative cost, not a prohibition against trading across complicated dependencies. [UX-W040, pp. 24–28]

A transaction-free zone permits transfers without making each one an individually defined, counted and compensated exchange. A kitchen can contain numerous contingent actions among workers without a separate sale every time a tool or ingredient changes hands. A supermarket allows shoppers to move goods into and out of their carts before the boundary transaction at checkout. A library's borrowing procedure defines and counts the book and specifies consequences of nonreturn. These examples identify the location and form of transaction work; they do not imply that the interior of a zone lacks all records, controls, transfers or costs. [UX-W040, pp. 27–28]

Baldwin deliberately uses a broad organizational vocabulary. A productive zone can be maintained by a business corporation, a commons organization or an open collaborative project. Unified governance, ownership and hierarchical authority are distinguishable arrangements rather than interchangeable words for organization. A corporation can protect a zone and establish its boundary relationships, but the ability to work productively without pricing every internal action is not confined to that legal form. [UX-W040, ch. 2]

The 2006 working paper by Baldwin and Kim Clark develops the placement of transactions through the same task-network question. Its selected operative passages explain why dense internal transfers need not all be standardized, counted and separately paid for. Its discussion of pinching a network concerns the redesign required to create manageable transaction interfaces. Incomplete interfaces preserve consultation, and the creation of designs and tests incurs setup costs. [UX-W008, pp. 4–6, 13–18, 35–41, 44]

Baldwin illustrates a thin crossing with a medieval smithy and kitchen. Each team makes many transfers of materials and information among its own members, while a completed pot hook passes from one team to the other. The smith and cook can agree on what counts as a usable hook, count the pieces and settle payment without tracking each contribution within the smithy. Baldwin contrasts this with buying a

partly finished implement, which would require more knowledge to specify the transfer and complete the work. [UX-W009, §4.2]

Complementarity: valuable together need not mean inseparable in every respect#

The book next asks why different technologies favor different organizational relationships. Complementarity concerns how the value of one input or action changes with another. Strongly complementary inputs can yield little value separately while producing a valuable result together. Where each actor must incur costs before the joint result exists, dispersed control can expose participants to bargaining problems or leave necessary investments unmade. Consistent decision-making and a capacity to settle disputes can then have substantial value. [UX-W040, ch. 3]

Yet economic complementarity and technical dependency are not identical. Baldwin illustrates the distinction with a recipe. Knowing that the ingredients are valuable together does not tell a cook how their quantities must be adjusted. Too much of one ingredient can ruin the result. Fine tolerances and causal relationships in a technical recipe contain information that the coarse statement of complementarity does not express. The book explicitly preserves this difference rather than translating every engineering dependency into a useful economic complementarity relation. [UX-W040, pp. 51–52]

The spectrum of complementarity supplies Baldwin's explanation of opposing organizational incentives. Dense interdependence, noncontractible effort and cospecialized investment can favor integration. Dispersed knowledge, modularity and opportunities for decentralized exploration can favor autonomy. Between complete independence and unified control lie relationships supported by prices, contracts, platforms, integrators and negotiation. The balance can change when technical designs or strategies change. The book's centripetal and centrifugal language denotes economic incentives. [UX-W040, ch. 3]

Distributed modular complementarity names a more demanding situation than the mere existence of separate firms. Actors must be able to capture value from their contributions; distributed governance must compare favorably with integration; and costs must be aligned with the value available to the actors who incur them. The mathematical appendix distinguishes simultaneous and sequential investment. An actor who can benefit only after another has invested faces a different decision from an actor choosing simultaneously without assurance of the other's participation. [UX-W040, pp. 66–70]

Cost misalignment is particularly consequential for gambits engineering. A system may offer attractive joint value while the participant required to perform one task lacks a sufficient return. The missing investment is then compatible with individually rational action. Joint desirability alone does not cause the task to be performed. [UX-W040, p. 63 and ch. 3 appendix]

Integration and separation also have transition costs. An incumbent cannot necessarily obtain the advantages of a different arrangement simply by redrawing a boundary. Modularizing an integrated product, spinning off a unit, combining separate operations and replacing legacy systems all require changes whose rewards may be uncertain. The book's account of an equilibrium among organizations therefore does not imply an immediate or effortless path to it. [UX-W040, pp. 67–69]

Value structure: finding what constrains a result and what captures its value#

Baldwin's value structure analysis follows the way a technology generates value and then asks who can capture it. A production bottleneck limits throughput. A strategic bottleneck gives an actor leverage over value appropriation. These need not be the same location. A component can become inexpensive to manufacture while control of its design, standard or other indispensable attribute remains strategically valuable. Conversely, doing essential productive work does not automatically secure a favorable share of the result. [UX-W040, ch. 4]

Baldwin's 2023 retrospective explains why she turned from detailed design structure matrices to maps of functional components. Building a matrix meant tracing dependencies that could be known only to the people doing the work, often through interviews. Maps organized around what each component does let her trace contributions and strategic bottlenecks across organizations without cataloguing every task. [UX-W038, §§3, 5.2]

Value structure maps provide a selective description of such relationships. Equations permit more specific reasoning when enough is known about the technology and the valuation. Baldwin explicitly says that radical uncertainty limits their use and that the book employs them to derive principles rather than to estimate specific numerical values or probabilities. [UX-W040, ch. 4; p. 105]

The perspective also allows designs and organizational arrangements to alter the location of a bottleneck. A participant may seek an alternative supplier, make an interface more open, acquire control of a scarce complement or prevent another party from becoming indispensable. These are strategies for changing the distribution of dependence and returns. Their success for a focal actor is a separate issue from the costs of the whole productive network. [UX-W040, chs. 4, 9–13]

Flow production: every necessary step still matters#

The book's first extended organizational paradigm is flow production. When each step is necessary, output is constrained by the step with the lowest capacity. Increasing capacity elsewhere can leave throughput unchanged. Finding the current bottleneck, improving it and then discovering the new limiting step is therefore a continuing managerial task. Detailed knowledge of the process is required: a factory can contain many potential bottlenecks whose capacities are not evident from an organization chart. [UX-W040, ch. 5, especially pp. 103–108]

The formal comparison is bounded. With a fixed set of capacities and every step required, adding another necessary step cannot increase their minimum. In the stochastic treatment, the adverse uncertainty comparison uses the relevant conditional mean-preserving-spread assumptions. A casual claim that any process with a larger variance must have lower throughput is stronger than this argument. The capacities, their relationships and the definition of the productive sequence matter. [UX-W040, ch. 5 and appendix]

Systematic management addresses this structure through measurement, scheduling, buffers, changes in task assignments and the coordination of complementary investments. Production is not improved by an isolated machine in the abstract; the change must fit the surrounding sequence. A faster machine can merely move the queue. Baldwin's historical accounts of inside contracting show how disputes among departments could obstruct a system-level change even when its proponents expected better production. Unified governance can allow action without renegotiating every affected relationship in real time. [UX-W040, ch. 5]

The book does not make unified governance logically indispensable. It states that sufficiently close information sharing and an ability to act quickly on bottlenecks can also be sustained by long-term relational contracts, although building those relationships takes time. There are also offsetting costs when integration weakens incentives or suppresses useful autonomy. The relevant organizational advantage is a capacity to coordinate the specific coupled process. [UX-W040, p. 115]

Ford, General Motors and Toyota provide different arrangements of flow production. In Baldwin's account, GM combines product platforms with decentralized operational responsibility and corporate coordination. Its smaller divisions also serve as places for developing managers who understand whole businesses. This is a form of retained organizational knowledge. [UX-W040, ch. 6, especially pp. 134–140]

Toyota's low-inventory arrangements expose problems that buffers might otherwise conceal. Making the arrangement work requires diagnosis, improvement, training and relations with suppliers. Local signals

can govern the movement of work, but recurring signals are still coordination, and a production problem still has to be understood and repaired. The source's account cannot be reduced to removing inventory while leaving every surrounding capability unchanged. [UX-W040, ch. 6]

Worker experience is part of this history. The book describes tensions arising when management treats workers' local knowledge and initiative as dispensable, and it contrasts arrangements that develop such capabilities. The later article explicitly describes classical Taylor–Ford tasks as physically arduous, repetitive and detached from the completed product. Its account of authority depends on workers accepting and acting within the arrangement. A scheme that appears economical from a managerial description can impose burdens that undermine that acceptance. [UX-W040, ch. 6; UX-W001, “Synchronized flow production, ” including notes 59 and 63]

Platforms: the ability to refuse an option changes the problem#

A platform supports alternatives that can be selected without requiring every one to succeed. In Baldwin's model, the platform exists apart from individual options, and an option can be exercised separately from the others. This is a different value structure from a flow process in which failure of a necessary step prevents the final output. A proposed new feature can be rejected while an adequate fallback remains. [UX-W040, ch. 7, especially pp. 149–156]

That separate exercisability supplies the limited formal reason for a modular option portfolio's advantage. For realized additive increments v1, …, vn and a zero incremental fallback, the positive part of their sum cannot exceed the sum of their separate positive parts: max( Σvi,0) ≤ Σmax(vi,0). The integral choice accepts or rejects the combined result; the separate choices can reject a losing increment without discarding a winning one. It assumes the same component realizations and feasible separate choices; it does not price the interfaces, experiments, maintenance or other costs required to create those choices. [UX-W040, ch. 7 appendix A, especially pp. 175–176]

The weak inequality can be an equality. A strict gain requires relevant outcomes in which accepting some components and rejecting others improves on the combined choice. Modularity by name does not establish that circumstance. Nor does the ability to reject an option protect every participant from its cost. The book explicitly observes that the provider may suffer harm even when a user can reject a poor outcome. [UX-W040, pp. 156–158 and appendix A]

The chapter also advances broader claims about risk, modularity and increasing differences. Its general P4(b) argument moves from separate monotonicities to a multiplicative value representation without establishing the additional structure required. This leaves a gap in the argument for the unrestricted claim. [UX-W040, pp. 176–182; governing notes to ch. 7]

The substantive cost question remains open in the source account. Rule design, testing, buffers and experimentation can be expensive. Gross option value and the net saving after interface and experimentation costs are different comparisons. A technical change may also alter the fallback or couple choices previously treated as separate. These conditions explain why an option argument does not license unlimited fragmentation of actual organizations. [UX-W040, chs. 7–8]

Semiconductor production and the continuing core of integration#

The semiconductor history gives the platform account a demanding setting. Components and designs can support decentralized innovation downstream while fabrication remains tightly coupled, capital intensive and dependent on specialized knowledge. Standards, manufacturing discipline and inspection help sustain the interfaces on which other participants rely. Distributed innovation at one level does not imply that every underlying process has become loosely coupled. [UX-W040, ch. 8]

Baldwin's discussion of replication practices and supplier relationships likewise retains coordination. Preserving a successful process across sites can require disciplined control of differences rather than spontaneous local substitution. A stable interface rests on work that prevents or detects incompatibility. [UX-W040, ch. 8]

The IBM PC and subsequent standards-based platforms extend the argument from engineering compatibility to value capture. Opening a system to complementary suppliers can expand the available alternatives while leaving the sponsor vulnerable to parties controlling strategic bottlenecks. Baldwin's Wintel discussion follows that distinction. The creation of a productive ecosystem and the appropriation of its returns need not favor the same actor. [UX-W040, chs. 9–10]

Dell: inventory and finance across an organizational boundary#

Dell is an especially revealing case because the book gives the operational and financial boundary together. In the historical arrangement described, orders entered an information system; machines were scheduled when the required parts were available; nearby logistics centers supplied components to a tightly timed assembly process. Dell's internal flow was actively measured and improved. The apparent flexibility of configuring a computer for an individual order rested on a substantial shared logistics and information arrangement. [UX-W040, ch. 11, pp. 292–294]

The components in the logistics centers belonged to suppliers until they crossed Dell's receiving boundary. Suppliers were required to hold inventory and faced consequences when stocks fell below the agreed standard. They also bore devaluation exposure and waited for payment. Baldwin states directly that Dell's supply-chain practices made Dell efficient without thereby making its suppliers efficient. Dell's small reported inventory therefore did not mean that the productive network had stopped holding inventory. [UX-W040, pp. 292–293]

Delayed payment also affected the financing of operations. The book describes a negative cash-conversion cycle in which Dell collected from customers before paying suppliers. Its historical numerical illustration combines receivable days, inventory days and payable days to show the direction of the financing relationship. Suppliers consequently financed part of Dell’s activity. [UX-W040, pp. 294–297]

This arrangement carried vulnerabilities as well as advantages. Suppliers could be constrained by demand changes and inventory requirements; Dell's cash position could come under pressure when purchases and orders declined. Multiple suppliers helped protect against some disruptions, but strategic sole-source dependencies remained. The chapter follows competitive performance over time rather than treating a favorable configuration as permanent. [UX-W040, ch. 11, especially pp. 294–300]

Baldwin describes geographically dispersed modular production as a flow process coordinated end to end by lead firms and assembly providers. Her pandemic discussion follows bottlenecks and cascading disturbances, while leaving the eventual organizational consequences open. [UX-W040, ch. 12, especially p. 325]

Different platforms retain different work#

Baldwin distinguishes standards-based product platforms, logistics platforms, transaction platforms and communication platforms. A product interface supports compatible complements. A logistics platform coordinates material flows. A transaction platform supports matching and exchange. A communication platform connects participants and information. Calling all four platforms does not make their channels, governance problems or exposure to disruption identical. [UX-W040, chs. 9–13]

The exchange-platform cases also distinguish relatively asynchronous development from tightly coupled real-time operation. Many outside contributors can develop complements while a core service must still

execute reliably and consistently when used. Decisions over access, architecture and complementary markets affect who can participate and who captures value. A distributed perimeter can coexist with concentrated control of strategically important functions. [UX-W040, ch. 13]

These accounts connect technology and strategy without making organizational change technologically automatic. Interfaces can be opened or restricted; bottlenecks can be controlled, bypassed or displaced; and institutional arrangements affect which choices are available. Baldwin's later conclusion treats technology and organization as developing together. The direction of change cannot be read from a single partition diagram alone. [UX-W040, chs. 13, 17]

Software: modular before addition can become interdependent afterward#

Software makes the distinction between an available option and an installed dependency especially clear. A feature may initially be developed separately and evaluated against a working codebase. Once incorporated, other code can come to depend on it. Baldwin states that a codebase may be modular ex ante without being modular ex post. The ability to add a feature one at a time does not guarantee that it can later be removed without consequences. [UX-W040, p. 369]

Formal interfaces and dependency tracking can preserve some separability, but they require time and effort. A running program also differs from the process of designing it. Development can involve trial, rejection and revision; executing a working system involves carrying out the resulting technical recipe. The book uses this distinction to explain why the organization of design should not simply be inferred from a mass-production sequence. [UX-W040, ch. 14]

Information hiding here concerns reliance on interfaces and internal decisions. It is distinct from making source code secret. Open access to code can allow inspection, learning and collaboration while useful boundaries between responsibilities remain. Conversely, visible source code alone does not supply a well-maintained interface or an organization capable of resolving incompatible changes. [UX-W040, chs. 14– 16]

Open collaboration: participation, incentives and maintenance#

The book's account of open-source collaboration relies on identifiable conditions. Developers can obtain value as users; tasks can be divided and their contributions evaluated; contributors are not rivals in the relevant benefit; and communication costs are low enough for collaboration to be worthwhile. Under such conditions, sharing useful work can create substantial gains without making every contribution an individually compensated transaction. [UX-W040, ch. 15]

Baldwin expressly says that the necessary conditions are not jointly sufficient for a project to form and endure. Community formation, leadership, conflict resolution and continued usefulness still matter. Technologies that lack divisible, value-separable tasks or require large complementary investments may not support the same arrangement. Features with positive aggregate value can also remain missing when no participant can recover its own contribution cost. A group can benefit from a result that nobody has sufficient individual reason to produce. [UX-W040, pp. 402–404]

This explains why the transaction-free character of collaboration is not simply equivalent to the absence of economic problems. Trying to price every internal action can reintroduce the definition, measurement and bargaining costs the zone avoids. Yet refraining from such transactions can leave some needed work unfunded. The book treats this as a specific organizational difficulty, not as evidence that contributors' labor has no cost. [UX-W040, ch. 15]

Corporate participation changes the arrangement again. Contributing an improvement upstream can reduce the burden of maintaining a private fork and allow repairs to be shared. But corporations can also

become dependent on components maintained by very small groups. Baldwin describes developers who unexpectedly become unpaid maintainers for demanding users, and code that loses support when those developers stop. Widely reused software need not have a large, durable maintenance organization behind it. [UX-W040, ch. 16, especially pp. 443–445]

The book's reported vulnerability and contribution-concentration studies illustrate this concern: reuse can spread the value of development while the responsibility and compensation for continuing care remain unresolved. [UX-W040, pp. 445–446]

Mirroring technical dependencies in organizational ties#

The mirroring hypothesis proposes correspondence between technical dependencies and organizational ties. Colfer and Baldwin's 2016 review argues that such an arrangement can conserve scarce cognitive resources by placing communication and cooperation where technical work requires them. It reviews 142 studies grouped into industries, firms and open collaborative projects. [SB-W005, abstract/introduction and selected methods/results, pp. 717–723]

Partial mirroring is one important exception. Firms can keep knowledge boundaries broader than operational boundaries, knowing about components they do not make. That knowledge can support integration and adaptation as technologies change. Relational contracts can also support strong dependencies across firm boundaries, while a close-knit organization can deliberately develop modular technical components internally. These patterns challenge any inference that a module interface must eliminate broader technical understanding or that ownership must always align with a technical partition. [SB-W005, selected firm studies and discussion, pp. 721–723, 726–730]

Digital tools furnish further modes of coordination in open collaborative projects. Work can be divided among dispersed participants while shared code, records and other tools preserve information needed to coordinate it. The 2016 article's open-project studies, mostly software, were not supportive of classical mirroring as a general description. The authors' response is to characterize boundary conditions and alternative mechanisms. [SB-W005, pp. 726–730]

The sample and methods constrain the results. Information industries, especially software, are heavily represented; process, extractive and service industries are less well represented. Selection was not random. The classifications are deliberately coarse and partly subjective. In the normative studies, successes substantially outnumber failures. The fact that the reviewed partial-mirroring firms performed well does not show that failed adopters do not exist: researchers seeking best practices can disproportionately choose successful firms. [SB-W005, selected methods and limitations, pp. 717–719, 722–723, 729–730]

Baldwin reuses the mirroring research in the later book and article. [SB-W005, introduction, selected methods/results and limitations; UX-W040, ch. 2; UX-W001, “Organizational ties and the mirroring hypothesis”]

The later article: job shops, flow and platforms as representative arrangements#

A theory of technology and organizations provides a later presentation of Baldwin's framework. The article organizes its exposition around job shops, synchronized flow processes and platforms, using task structure matrices to show their different patterns of transfer and coordination. [UX-W001, “Technological paradigms” and subsequent sections]

The job shop adds an instructive case to the book-centered account above. Work arrives in varying quantities and can follow different sequences, with revisits to earlier tasks. Workers assume different roles, and a coordinator redeploys resources as loads change. This versatility supports variety and

responsiveness. Its costs include idle capacity, changing imbalances and repeated adjustment. Resolving today's imbalance need not yield a permanent improvement when tomorrow's job mix differs. Market size and transportation costs help determine whether narrow specialization is viable. [UX-W001, “Job shops”]

The article's flow matrices distinguish a central coordinator from backward local signaling. In the latter, a downstream station signals readiness to its upstream neighbor, potentially through an empty container. Forward material transfers still occur. The conditional self-coordination claim presumes the initial stock and a compatible productive sequence; it does not mean that signals, replenishment or diagnosis have disappeared. The discussion also retains the need to find bottlenecks, rebalance tasks and ensure that workers can perform assigned work within the cycle. [UX-W001, “Synchronized flow production, ” fig. 7]

Its platform matrix separately displays rules, modules and system integration. Providers can work without direct lateral exchanges only insofar as the architecture actually makes their tasks separate and they follow common rules. There may be many integrators, including users. The article's comparison table describes platform efficiency as variable, a useful qualification against reading the sequence of paradigms as an automatic climb toward greater economy. [UX-W001, “Platform organizations, and ecosystems, ” fig. 8 and table 2]

The article finally discusses AI using its contemporary 2025 setting. It distinguishes the organizational work of developing and using systems from the unobserved internal steps of a model, and acknowledges that tasks and transfers may not be an adequate metaphor for those internal processes. This theoretical discussion marks the author's stated limit on extending the representation. [UX-W001, AI discussion and conclusion]

The valuation and scope carried by this account#

Baldwin asks how technologies and organizations create and capture value. Her notes define efficiency in terms of sustainable profit relative to investment, rather than simply lowest cost. This business criterion differs from physical resource use, human effort and welfare. [UX-W040, ch. 1 note 42; ch. 17 note 4; chs. 11, 16]

Baldwin explains why design rules can settle recurring questions, why some transfers are better left inside a transaction-free zone, how complementary investments favor coordination, and how separately exercisable options create value. [UX-W040, chs. 2–7, 11–17; UX-W008; SB-W005; UX-W001]

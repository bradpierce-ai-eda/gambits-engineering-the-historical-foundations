S57 Preserving essential service under failure #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

Subsection linksLiu and colleagues: finish the mandatory computationKlein and colleagues: regulate optional application workMeza and colleagues: maintain emergency feature controlsSarter and Woods: recognize what the changed mode will do

Insufficient capacity need not remove every useful function at once. The accounts here develop ways to retain an acceptable computation or service while relinquishing some work. Scheduling, application design, feedback control and incident response determine what survives. A further problem arises when the changed operation is difficult for its human supervisors to recognize.

S30 treats reliability and fault handling. The following sources concentrate on deliberately reduced service: how the reduction is prepared, selected and operated, and what its recipients lose.

Liu and colleagues: finish the mandatory computation#

Jane Liu and colleagues’ 1991 account of imprecise computation divides a time-critical task into mandatory and optional work. The mandatory part produces an acceptable result; optional execution improves its quality. A scheduler can stop the optional work when its time is exhausted and still return something useful. This requires a program designed to produce such a result. An arbitrary program interrupted before completion does not acquire that property merely because a deadline has arrived. [CG-W013, pp. 58–59]

The authors describe several ways to construct the task. In the milestone method, a computation records intermediate results with indicators of their error. Further execution improves the result, and the indicators let a recipient assess an early answer. A sieve method instead omits complete steps. Their radar example can omit an update to an estimate of background noise and reuse the earlier estimate. A third method provides two versions: a precise primary computation and a shorter, less accurate alternative. The alternatives make different forms of partial work useful. A half-executed sieve step or an unfinished alternate version may contribute nothing to the returned result. [CG-W013, p. 59]

Each construction has an implementation cost. Milestones require recording intermediate values. Sieve methods complicate scheduling when the optional steps must finish as units. Multiple versions need storage and a decision about which version can finish. The scheduler also needs bounds on mandatory execution. If all mandatory portions cannot fit within the available time, discarding optional portions cannot by itself make the task set feasible. The authors make mandatory feasibility a separate test before pursuing better quality. [CG-W013, pp. 59–61]

Their basic model includes ready times, deadlines, processing requirements, importance weights and precedence relations. Additional optional execution is assumed not to increase the result’s error. Several possible objectives remain: minimizing total error, limiting the greatest error, reducing the number of discarded optional tasks, or controlling delay. These choices produce different scheduling problems. Finishing by the deadline is consequently only one part of the specified service; the quality measure and the distribution of optional work also matter. [CG-W013, pp. 59–61]

The later queueing discussion distinguishes responsive service from a service commitment made on admission. A responsive scheme can reduce the service assigned to waiting tasks as congestion develops. It can clear an overload sooner, while changing what an already waiting task will receive. A guaranteed-service scheme decides on arrival which service a task will receive and preserves that decision. Tasks admitted before overload retain their promised full service, at the expense of slower clearance of the backlog. The authors develop these choices through queue models with specified arrival and service assumptions and identify further comparisons to be studied. [CG-W013, pp. 66–67]

Klein and colleagues: regulate optional application work#

Cristian Klein and colleagues’ 2014 Brownout system adjusts a cloud application’s demand by omitting optional response components. A parameter called the dimmer controls the probability that an optional component will execute. Individual requests sample that probability, while a feedback controller changes the parameter from measured response times. The application can thereby reduce the work required per request when its workload grows or its assigned processing capacity declines. [CG-W014, §§2–3]

The application must expose useful divisions of work. In their auction-site implementation, the researchers added a simple recommendation feature that could be omitted while the main response remained available. A script reads a small cached file containing the dimmer, decides whether to generate recommendations and reports response time to a local controller. The controller writes a replacement dimmer file and renames it atomically. This implementation makes the response path, measurement and actuation concrete: optionality is prepared in the code before the controller can use it. [CG-W014, §4]

Their bulletin-board implementation has two optional components, comments and recommendations. It first samples whether to include comments and then, if comments are included, samples again whether to add recommendations. With the same dimmer value for both independent trials, the probability of including comments is the dimmer value, while the probability of including both is its square. The researchers chose that dependency between features. It determines which combinations users can receive and how a controller setting changes the work performed. [CG-W014, §4]

The controller uses a simplified relationship between the dimmer and the maximum response time observed in a sampling window. It estimates changing gain online and applies feedback with protection against accumulated control action beyond the dimmer’s limits. Tuning changes the balance between fast response and sensitivity to noise or model error. If response time remains excessive with all optional work disabled, the requested operating point is infeasible: the controller has no further optional work to relinquish. [CG-W014, §3]

The experiments run an application stack inside a virtual machine on one physical machine, with the CPU as the bottleneck. Tests change processor resources, concurrent users, or both, and measure the time to receive a complete HTTP response. The controller reacts after observing the change, so transient violations remain. More conservative tuning recovers more slowly while reducing aggressive overshoot. The authors compare settings using a constructed revenue measure that awards one unit for a request and an additional half-unit for a recommendation. Their favorable comparisons concern that valuation and the tested workloads. The measurement was not customer sales. Extending the approach to applications distributed over several machines remained future work. [CG-W014, §§5, 7]

Meza and colleagues: maintain emergency feature controls#

Justin Meza and colleagues’ 2023 account of Defcon begins with an operational choice at Meta: reduce the work needed to provide a product by temporarily changing some of its features. Sending a message can remain available while an online-status indicator is omitted. A comment service can return comments while replacing model-based ranking with chronological order. Explicit switches, called knobs, select these changes in the affected control flow. The reductions alter what people receive while allowing the core request to proceed. [CG-W015, §§2.2–2.4, 3.2, pp. 609–612]

Product engineers identify candidate features and connect them to a shared configuration system. A testing framework measures resource savings and changes in user behavior. Operators use a portal showing those results and a command-line interface to activate groups of knobs. Knobs can span source files and binaries, identify an owner and belong to a degradation level. Keeping these definitions useful requires product teams to maintain them as their software changes. [CG-W015, §§3.1–3.3, pp. 611–612]

The actuation path is deliberately kept dependent on few other systems so that it remains usable during partial failures. Server changes can apply in seconds. Client changes can prevent requests from reaching servers, but configuration must first reach the devices. A push mechanism takes around thirty minutes across the client population. Periodic full configuration pulls require bandwidth and server resources; an emergency mechanism pulls selected configuration fields with manual intervention. The location of the switch therefore affects both the work saved and the time needed to obtain that saving. [CG-W015, §3.2, p. 611]

Ordinary operation is Level 4, with degradation knobs disabled. Levels 3, 2 and 1 were designed around target demand reductions of 5, 10 and 20 percent, with progressively greater product impact. These targets reflect Meta’s failure domains and expected disruptions. Responders inspect demand, tested savings and product effects, activate an appropriate level and continue monitoring. The reported October 2022 demand surge prompted successive activation of two levels, followed by withdrawal when the surge passed. This is a reported production response; the savings targets guide its preparation rather than guarantee a fixed reduction in every event. [CG-W015, §1, pp. 607–608; §3.5, pp. 613–614]

Savings cannot be inferred solely from the code that a knob skips. Tests trace changes into dependent services, including services without knobs of their own. Region-drain experiments also show that traffic relocation and cold caches change the comparison. Small controlled tests have a concurrent comparison group; larger exercises can instead use a modeled baseline built from forecast and backcast signals. Those estimates and the directly observed comparisons perform different evidential jobs in the account. [CG-W015, §§4.1–4.5, pp. 614–618]

Users bear some of the cost. One Level 1 product test reduced app usage time by 11 percent; combined tests increased reports of unexpected behavior. Alongside such outcomes, operators need current measurements, regression investigations, rehearsals and training. The paper describes continuing engineering and organizational work to keep the emergency option available. The existence of a switch is only the beginning of that preparation. [CG-W015, table 2, pp. 616–618; §5, pp. 619–620]

Sarter and Woods: recognize what the changed mode will do#

Nadine Sarter and David Woods’s 1995 article develops the human demands of supervising automation with many operating modes. A mode changes how a system responds to input. Transitions can follow an environmental event, a protection function or an earlier command without a new direct instruction. A supervisor needs to know which mode is active, which is armed for later activation and what conditions will cause a transition. A status indication may supply only part of that knowledge. [CG-W016, pp. 5–9]

Their account includes a part-task simulator study involving twenty airline pilots. The scenario introduced abnormal, time-critical events, including loss of the glide-slope signal during final approach. About half the pilots did not understand what that loss meant for the flight-management system; some had difficulty detecting the problem during the simulation. Only five knew when to expect the indication that go-around mode was available. The difficulty involved both recognizing a changed condition and anticipating which action would next become possible. [CG-W016, pp. 11–12]

The aborted-takeoff probe showed another distinction between knowledge and action. Sixty-five percent of participants were unaware that the autothrottle system controlled thrust in that situation. Only four of twenty managed the automation completely correctly, and one of those explained that he was following a procedure rather than understanding the active automation. Some pilots could describe relevant settings afterward without having used that knowledge during the event. The authors call attention to knowledge that remains inert when the situation requires it. [CG-W016, pp. 11–12]

Their proposed responses include feedback that makes present and future behavior more apparent and training that activates knowledge in context. An interaction history or an additional sensory channel

might help, but added information can also burden attention. Requiring consent before every change could keep people involved while making repeated checks automatic or interrupting other work. The article presents these as design directions requiring investigation. Its study explains a specific difficulty in operating changed automation; its proposals address the information and practice needed to use the remaining capability. [CG-W016, pp. 13–16]

S54 develops other accounts of control, reconfiguration and recovery. The present accounts concentrate on a selected reduction of service and on the knowledge required to operate when behavior changes.

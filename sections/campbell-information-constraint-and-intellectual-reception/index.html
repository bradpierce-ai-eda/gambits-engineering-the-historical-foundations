Information, constraint and reception#

Jeremy Campbell's Grammatical Man connects information, entropy, language, life and the possibilities created by constraint. The foreword and afterword in Juan José Utrilla's 1989 translation, El hombre gramatical, examine how constraints enable variety. [WME-W029]

Constraint can enable variety#

The foreword and afterword treat rules and constraints as enabling a rich range of outcomes. An arrangement of rules can make organized possibilities available. The afterword relates Aristotle, form and matter to the question of how form directs what material can do, and discusses costs associated with informational ordering. [WME-W029, foreword, pp. 9–10, and selected afterword, pp. 387–395]

Sless: lucid communication theory, disputed unification#

David Sless's review provides the most substantial scholarly criticism among the reviews discussed here. He praises Campbell's accessible explanation of ideas surrounding mathematical communication theory, while objecting that the actual mathematics is omitted. For Sless, this limits the reader's ability to assess the theory and its applications. [SB-W018, printed pp. 186–188]

Sless describes the book as progressing from communication toward symbols, genes, perception, knowledge and society. He argues that Campbell's enthusiasm underplays reservations, including caution from information theory's founders. His objection is not simply that many disciplines appear in one volume; it is that the breadth can give a stronger impression of established unification than the argument warrants.

The review also has limits of its own. Sless expressly declines a competent assessment of the biological-evolution material. That disclaimer leaves a major part of the proposed connection unevaluated by this reviewer. His assertion restricting Shannon theory to finite systems is too broad to adopt as a technical conclusion.

Sless's treatment of perception is more discriminating than a blanket rejection. He praises the explanation of J. J. Gibson's ideas and objects that R. L. Gregory's contrasting position receives insufficient development. [SB-W018]

His closing discussion concerns the extension to social theory and the afterword's link between Aristotle and DNA. He criticizes the favorable social and political implications attached to the language of information and variety. The review consequently distinguishes explanatory clarity in a particular unit from acceptance of the entire cross-disciplinary progression.

Simpson: compact instruction and an unclear connection#

Fred Simpson's review describes a book full of interesting perspectives whose overall connection he finds frustratingly unclear. His positive response centers particularly on DNA as a compact, reliable yet flexible instruction scheme. In his account, rules constrain transmission while permitting diversity. Simpson emphasizes the relation between a compact inherited scheme, repeated development and a range of possible outcomes. It remains Simpson's reconstruction of Campbell's biological discussion. [SB-W019, printed pp. 40–41; issue PDF pp. 42–43]

Simpson also reports a progression through entropy, information, language, genetics, the brain and Aristotle. He uses broad equations among information, order and the opposite of randomness to describe both the appeal and difficulty of the book. [SB-W019]

Simpson suggests that the book implies a grand design, while noting that Campbell does not quite say this. It is the reviewer's interpretation of the narrative. [SB-W019]

The review ends favorably despite its frustration: Simpson describes returning to sections and finding the perspectives rewarding. That response shows that unclear integration and useful individual material can coexist in a reader's experience. It is a historical reader response to Campbell's book.

Mair: enthusiastic reception and the difficulty of redundancy-poor text#

Victor H. Mair's short notice praises the breadth and informative character of Grammatical Man. He associates the difficulty of reading Classical Chinese with ambiguity and low redundancy. His notice applies an informational perspective to a task requiring interpretation. [SB-W020, B28; collection PDF p. 45]

Mair also describes being reassured by the book's broad account of forces opposed to chance. His comments about molecules and the fate of the universe convey the book's rhetorical impact on this reader.

Kirkus: a favorable account that still acknowledges speculation#

The Kirkus review identifies themes of uncertainty, redundancy, contextual constraint and interaction across communication, genetics, perception and linguistics. It presents Campbell's synthesis favorably, while recognizing speculative elements and weak evidence in parts of the wider discussion. It therefore supplies both a topic map and a contemporaneous response to the book's explanatory ambition. It is a trade review, with a different purpose from a scholarly adjudication of the underlying fields. [WME-W030; WME-W029, review]

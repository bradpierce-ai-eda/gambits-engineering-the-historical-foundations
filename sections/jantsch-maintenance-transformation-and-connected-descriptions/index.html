Evolution and changing organization#

Erich Jantsch's The Self-Organizing Universe offers an extensive account connecting physical organization, biological development, ecological relations and human meaning. Its central concern is evolution as a process that can change not only its products but also the ways in which further change occurs. [WME-W001, 1980, foreword and chs. 1–4]

Organization can persist through continuous activity#

Jantsch begins with a distinction between arrangements that persist as relatively conservative structures and processes that maintain a dynamic organization through exchange. A dissipative structure is not simply a static shape that happens to use energy. Its organized regime depends on continued interactions, including the import of usable resources and the export of products and entropy. What persists can be a pattern of activity even while constituent materials change. [WME-W001, ch. 2, 21–41]

His chemical discussion identifies openness, departure from equilibrium and nonlinear reaction organization, including autocatalytic or cross-catalytic steps. An autocatalytic participant contributes to the production of further participants of its kind; cross-catalysis closes that relation through intermediates. These are specified process relations, rather than an unexplained appeal to a whole acting on its parts. The account describes conditions for the models under discussion. [WME-W001, 31–35]

The maintained organization continues to produce entropy. Jantsch's emphasis is on flows and rates rather than treating internal order as a violation of thermodynamics. A locally persistent regime requires continuing environmental support. If that support fails, the organization can degenerate. “Self” in this account does not mean independence from materials, energy or surroundings. [WME-W001, 31–35, 203]

He distinguishes several aspects of a system: its environmental relations, internal state, logical organization, function, spatial and temporal structure, and total dynamics. These are different questions about the same organized activity. A flow diagram can describe how transformations connect, while a spatial picture describes where they occur. Neither by itself supplies the full account of what makes the organization persist. [WME-W001, 32–35, table 1]

The book uses autopoiesis broadly in this process account. Jantsch extends the term from chemical models and living cells toward institutions. His physical examples give specified ways of understanding organized dynamics. His later social extensions require additional interpretation rather than following as direct experimental deductions from a chemical reaction. [WME-W001, chs. 2–4]

Stability and transformation are different accomplishments#

Within a maintained regime, disturbances can be absorbed. A sufficiently amplified fluctuation can instead lead to a different regime. Jantsch relates structure, function and fluctuation: the organization constrains which changes persist, while changes can eventually modify the organization itself. Maintenance and transformation therefore form connected but different aspects of his evolutionary account. [WME-W001, 41–48]

The distinction between microscopic and macroscopic descriptions is substantive. A macroscopic analysis can identify instability thresholds and possible resulting regimes. A microscopic account follows the formation, size and spread of fluctuations. Crossing a parameter threshold need not mean that any tiny local disturbance actually reorganizes a finite system. Spatial extent, diffusion, packing and coupling affect

whether a fluctuation survives or is damped. Jantsch's presentation of nucleation makes the relation between local events and whole-system change conditional. [WME-W001, 44–48, figs. 10–11]

His chemical examples allow several possible branches after a transition. The selected path depends on the process's history as well as the possibilities available under its conditions. A model's available outcomes and a prediction of the particular outcome are different achievements. Jantsch repeatedly presents such models as aids to understanding; the book's city and population models also have simplifying assumptions. [WME-W001, 47–57, 65–72]

Reorganization can require work. The book's hysteresis discussion associates different forward and return paths with the work invested in changing structure. Once the conditions retreat, the organization need not simply trace an identical reversible course. Calling this memory identifies a dependence on prior change. [WME-W001, 48–49, figs. 11–12]

Jantsch connects an established regime with economy of operation and a transition with expenditure on restructuring. That is a useful distinction between using an arrangement and making another arrangement possible. His novelty/confirmation and entropy-production diagrams organize the conceptual story; they are not calibrated accounts in one unit of lifecycle cost. [WME-W001, 42–53, figs. 12–14]

The book's schematic contrasts between maximum and minimum entropy production extend beyond the conditions under which those principles hold. They do not establish an optimizing law across physical, biological and social regimes. [WME-W001, chs. 2–3]

Co-evolution changes the surrounding possibilities#

Jantsch's macro–micro narrative makes organisms and their surroundings participants in a connected history. The environment supplies conditions for local processes, while the accumulated effects of those processes alter the environment encountered by later participants. Adaptation to a fixed setting is therefore only one part of the account. The rules and opportunities of further evolution can also change. [WME-W001, chs. 4–8]

The value of this claim lies partly in its causal direction. A feature that appears economical inside one organism may be possible because other processes now supply what it no longer produces. The account of reduced internal biosynthetic capability, illustrated through vitamin supply, locates a corresponding environmental dependence. Its relevant point is that missing internal production and independence are different things. [WME-W001, ch. 7, 133–138]

The architect's plan provides an especially accessible illustration. A plan sent to a distant building site is effective only if local conditions permit realization: materials must be available or obtainable, skills must exist, and infrastructure and environmental relations must support construction. Sending the plan does not transport the completed building or create skilled masons. Jantsch uses the example in explaining the transfer of process programmes rather than all the material conditions of their execution. [WME-W001, 133]

He also discusses the other side of adaptation. Acquired ecological information and an established way of life can increase present capability while narrowing later possibilities. A relation that once opened opportunities can become a dependency. Autonomy and flexibility do not therefore increase together in every step of the evolutionary narrative. The book's changing levels and criteria prevent its account from being reduced to monotonic improvement on one scale. [WME-W001, 141–142]

The biological and cosmic chapters range widely. Their original dates, evolutionary reconstructions, particle-physics models and neurobiological frameworks are historical premises of the 1980 account. [WME-W001, chs. 5–9, 11]

A record becomes useful through selective activation#

Jantsch distinguishes ongoing processes from relatively conservative storage. DNA, books, tools and representations can preserve possibilities, yet preserved form becomes effective only through its use in an active context. His term “epigenealogical” describes the relation between stored information and its selective retrieval in living, changing processes. The book's diagram makes both the record and its interpreting context visible. [WME-W001, ch. 11, 195–202, fig. 38]

The musical score illustrates the point. Its marks preserve an ordered possibility, while a performance involves learned conventions, interpretation and present activity. A book likewise does not make its knowledge available independently of readers and their purposes. Selection changes which stored possibilities become operative. Storage can support reuse without executing itself. [WME-W001, 202; ch. 18, 286–296]

This is more than a claim that a container holds information. Jantsch places novelty and confirmation in relation: prior patterns can guide activity, while a changed context can activate them differently. His evolutionary learning process has no permanently fixed school-like target. Existing histories and traditions channel what can happen, but a channeled process is not the same as a sequence directed toward a single externally supplied goal. [WME-W001, 202]

The account also retains implementation work in cultural creation. Vision must acquire rational form, and form must be interpreted and realized through learned and manual capabilities. A notation or representation can make an intention transportable and repeatable while depending on the skills and materials of performance. [WME-W001, ch. 18]

Interaction, communication, symbiosis and fusion#

Jantsch distinguishes several kinds of relation rather than treating all coupling as one thing. Interaction names exchange with an environment not organized as a comparable participant at the selected level. Communication involves one organized process evoking activity in another. Symbiosis involves the mutual use of products or services, while fusion describes the limiting case in which the former participants lose their separate autonomous organization. These distinctions concern the processes and autonomy retained, not merely the number of connecting arrows. [WME-W001, 203–207, fig. 39]

In his communication account, the recipient must be capable of the activity evoked. A description of a sunset works through a listener's experience and capacity to form meaning; it does not transfer the sunset itself. A stimulating signal also does not provide all the recipient's energy. Jantsch explicitly retains each system's responsibility for its own structural problems and energy flow. [WME-W001, 203–205]

Dialogue can in turn reorganize knowledge rather than merely exchange already completed packages. The claim is that mutual stimulation can change the participants' cognitive domains. That remains a conceptual account of generative communication. [WME-W001, 205–206]

Symbiosis introduces a different dependency. Participants use one another's transformation products or services. Each can relinquish some individual autonomy while participating in a larger organization with capabilities at another level. The lower-level processes do not necessarily vanish. This is why Jantsch distinguishes a partially autonomous relation from complete fusion. His own account also allows excessive mutual dependence, in which the relation narrows possibilities without producing the anticipated novelty. [WME-W001, 206–207]

His application of “co-operative” to whole ecological processes needs its stated viewpoint. A predator– prey relation may contribute to an ecosystem's pattern while destroying particular prey. Organization at the larger level does not imply benefit to each individual. Jantsch explicitly recognizes that affected

participants might not experience the relation as cooperation. The qualification matters whenever larger-system persistence is used as a criterion. [WME-W001, 135–136, 206]

Multiple levels retain different tests and responsibilities#

The book's account of emergent levels includes distinct criteria: energetic penetration, error correction, variety, flexibility, consistency and connectedness. These are not interchangeable numerical forms of one variable. Jantsch describes higher-level openness supported by more settled lower-level activities, but that construction does not make the lower levels inert or eliminate their conditions of existence. [WME-W001, ch. 12, 218–230, figs. 41–43]

His time- and space-binding account relates stored experience, present interactions and anticipation. A present organization can employ records of the past and images of a possible future. [WME-W001, ch. 13, 231–238, fig. 44 and table 5]

Stratified autonomy is his alternative to a picture in which information merely travels upward and orders travel downward. Each organized level can maintain some direct relation with a wider environment. A cell's surroundings, in his illustration, include more than adjacent cells. In the proposed social extension, local participants retain contact with their own circumstances while a broader level performs synthesis. The practical issue is the distribution of initiative and integration. [WME-W001, ch. 14, 239–250]

Jantsch's formulation of decentralized initiative and centralized synthesis therefore retains a management role. The manager as catalyst is an assigned activity, not an absence of organization. He also argues that an institutional pattern can suppress novelty at precisely the levels where broader changes need consideration. These are the author's organizational proposals and criticisms. Jantsch quotes Mesarović in discussing control hierarchies and relative frequencies. [WME-W001, 248–250]

The argument about intermediate organizational levels supplies a further distinction. A choice between very large and very small units can omit forms that connect local capability with wider provision. His machinery illustration, attributed to Schumacher, describes technical development reinforcing an expected disappearance of small producers. It is a historical reported example of the relation between technological options and organizational scale. [WME-W001, 249]

Planning can change what counts as satisfactory#

Jantsch separates tactical planning for an already recognized output from strategic, policy and value questions. At the tactical level, efficient realization within a relatively stable structure can be a reasonable aim. At the strategic level, alternatives are imagined, examined and prepared. Policy places the organization's activity in a wider social context, while values influence which futures deserve pursuit. The planning task changes as the level changes. [WME-W001, ch. 16, 266–274, table 6]

It follows within his account that a more open planning process can deliberately introduce options, uncertainty and complexity. Minimizing immediate effort or maximizing short-term profit does not settle whether a new social function should be developed or a harmful one discontinued. He distinguishes the survival of a particular organization from continuity of the social processes it serves. The book thus contains a substantive challenge to using organizational survival as the only criterion. [WME-W001, 266– 267]

The evolutionary proposal is not simply enthusiasm for destructive discontinuity. Jantsch recognizes that large transitions can damage the biological and cultural substrate on which further life depends. His proposed “gliding” evolution imagines partially overlapping changes and plural paths instead of requiring an entire civilization to change through a single rupture. [WME-W001, ch. 15, 251–262]

Part IV explicitly offers perspectives rather than solutions to every problem. Its ethical account places several levels of obligation and experience in tension. The passage on Antigone illustrates conflict between personal moral commitment and the law of the larger social order, not a method that automatically reconciles them. Jantsch's wish to ground an evolutionary ethics is a normative and philosophical proposal. Biological or social survival does not by itself establish a moral duty. [WME-W001, ch. 16, 263–274]

The technology discussion also limits a direct transfer from ecology to human economy. Exosomatic tools, restructuring of environments and increased energy use change the conditions of the comparison. A human arrangement can become more capable by expanding its material dependencies. [WME-W001, ch. 17, 275–285]

Connected descriptions without a supermodel#

Near the end, Jantsch explicitly rejects one supermodel combining all autonomous levels into a single description. He favors an account of the web of relations among descriptions. His contrast between macroscopic and microscopic approaches returns here: descriptions may be complementary because they answer different questions, rather than waiting to be replaced by one all-purpose vocabulary. [WME-W001, ch. 19, especially 303]

Jantsch brings maintenance, transformation, environmental capability, stored forms, communication and changing purposes into a connected evolutionary account. The epilogue extends his inquiry to meaning. [WME-W001, ch. 19 and epilogue 307–311]

The earlier dissipative-structures excerpt#

An excerpt from Jantsch's earlier Design for Evolution (1975) proposes the extension from physical to human systems as a hypothesis. Its examples concern a freeway changing urban activity, a newcomer stimulating a bureaucratic office, and a revolution followed by new structures and restrictions. In each, an initial alteration is followed by reorganization and a more established regime. [RGC-W001, excerpt and linked figure]

The freeway example is particularly informative because the new regime can become congested again. Opening capacity changes activity rather than permanently removing every source of congestion. The office example likewise makes the surrounding condition decisive: colleagues can absorb and exclude the newcomer, or existing tensions can permit a wider change. These are illustrative mechanisms and conditional stories, not controlled evaluations of road building or office reform. [RGC-W001]

The revolution example connects initial participation with the later consolidation of power and narrowing of permitted contributions. Jantsch uses entropy, power and rigidity in explaining that trajectory. Those social correspondences are not measured thermodynamic equalities. The examples help identify the proposed sequence and the retained costs of restructuring. [RGC-W001]

Greene's later reconstruction and intellectual reception#

Herman Greene's 2018 review reads Jantsch from an ecozoic perspective and traces a connection with Brian Swimme and Thomas Berry. He explains the book through process, self-organization, exchange and co-evolution, and relates those terms to differentiation, interiority and communion in the later writers. This is substantive evidence of a sympathetic intellectual reception. [WME-W002, 363–372]

Greene quotes and interprets Swimme and Berry as well as Jantsch. Their expanded use of autopoiesis and subjectivity belongs to Greene's account of the relationship among these authors; he points to The Universe Story in support. [WME-W002, especially 363–364, 370–372]

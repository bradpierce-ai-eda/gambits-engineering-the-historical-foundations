S91 Acquiring, maintaining and contesting power #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

Getting a proposal approved, controlling a resource and holding an office present different problems of influence. An office supplies certain rights; practical dependence can give someone influence well beyond those rights. Support may have to be assembled before a decision, renewed after it or protected against a rival. The accounts developed here examine how people acquire room to act, obtain cooperation, establish dependence and sustain positions of control.

Political skill and willingness to act#

Liam Maher, Aqsa Ejaz, Chi Lan Nguyen and Gerald Ferris distinguish the ability to act politically from the willingness to do so. Their review describes political skill through four connected capacities: understanding other people and situations, influencing interpersonal encounters, building networks, and appearing sincere. Social astuteness helps someone recognize an opportunity; relationships make resources accessible; interpersonal influence concerns using the understanding effectively in an encounter. Apparent sincerity concerns how the conduct is received. The same overt attempt to please a superior may be accepted as appropriate or dismissed as transparent flattery. [PWR-W001, pp. 6–10]

The distinction matters for interpreting a repertoire of influence tactics. Knowing a tactic's name does not establish an ability to perform it successfully, and possessing that ability does not establish a willingness to incur the relational or reputational risks of using it. The review follows political skill into relationships, reputation and evaluations by others, including supervisors and colleagues. These outcomes explain much of its relevance to careers: an employee's work is performed and assessed among people whose cooperation, information and judgments affect what becomes possible. [PWR-W001, pp. 5–10]

Dependence outside the hierarchy#

Michel Crozier's investigation of French factories gives practical dependence a concrete organizational form. Elaborate rules specify work, rights and relations between occupational groups. Seniority governs movement within a category, while recruitment from outside limits promotion between categories. Security and impersonal procedures reduce the personal discretion of supervisors. Yet machine breakdowns interrupt the otherwise regulated activity, and repairs depend on the maintenance workers. Production workers consequently experience dependence on people who are not their formal superiors. [PWR-W002]

Crozier asks why breakdowns become so consequential in these particular factories. When much of working life is specified beforehand, the events escaping the rules acquire exceptional importance. The maintenance workers control the response to one such uncertainty. Foremen object to the relationship but cannot resolve it through their own technical competence. Their position in the official hierarchy does not supply mastery of the practical problem on which production depends. [PWR-W002]

The maintenance group's position also has protection higher in the organization. An operations engineer possesses practical knowledge that formally superior engineers need, while his career prospects remain subordinate to theirs. Crozier interprets his support for the maintenance workers as protecting his own advantage. The resulting relationship runs across ranks: practical competence, dependence and backing reinforce a pattern that the official chart does not describe. Baldwin's strategic bottlenecks in S07 and Stinchcombe's occupational authority in S13 provide neighboring accounts of dependence and knowledge; Crozier develops the relations among the people occupying these particular positions. [PWR-W002]

Robert Greene's The 48 Laws of Power presents dependence as a deliberate objective. Its numbered prescriptions recommend keeping others in need of one's contribution and avoiding teaching them enough to dispense with one's services. Other prescriptions concern allowing a superior to feel secure, protecting reputation, controlling disclosure and taking credit for other people's work. These recommendations direct attention to different objects of political action: the distribution of capability, the attribution of achievement and others' beliefs about an actor. Greene treats preserving dependence and managing recognition as conduct to cultivate in the pursuit of power. [PWR-W004, laws 1, 3–7, 11]

Assembling support before a decision#

Jeffrey Pfeffer's Power Play begins with the difficulty of implementing an agenda among people who have competing purposes. Discretion over money, equipment, space or information can supply a means of recruiting support. Access to influential people can also be valuable. His account treats favors and resource distribution as ways of creating reciprocal support, alongside the substantive case for a proposed change. [PWR-W003]

Laura Esserman's efforts to change breast-cancer care illustrate several avenues of support operating over time. At the University of California, San Francisco, she sought more integrated care, easier enrollment in clinical trials and better collection of treatment information. When local changes met resistance, she continued building scientific and clinical visibility and relationships beyond the immediate organization. She also maintained relationships with patients and former patients, some of whom had substantial resources and connections. In Pfeffer's account, these activities kept adding advocates while parts of the program remained obstructed. [PWR-W003]

The account makes the construction of support part of implementing the clinical agenda. A relationship established outside the immediate dispute could become useful within it. Continuing on several fronts meant that resistance to one part did not stop every means of advancing. Pfeffer's reported case supplies this history of persistence and relationship building; the proposal itself was accompanied by efforts to acquire the practical capacity to carry it through. [PWR-W003]

大嶋玲未 and 廣川佳子 examine preparatory influence in new proposals within large Japanese firms. Their 2020 study draws on interviews with seven managers, analyzed using the KJ method. They identify

understanding the informal decision process, preparing the surrounding conditions and 根回し, or advance approaches to relevant participants. The managers needed to know who actually mattered, what concerns would arise and which parts of the approval process could be influenced through prior discussion. [PWR-W005, pp. 61–64]

The actions are specific. A manager might tell a key person about an impending proposal and ask which points mattered. Participants could develop a compromise before a formal confrontation, exchange concessions or speak beforehand to someone likely to obstruct the discussion. An intermediary could approach a person more effectively than the proposer could. Table 2 includes an account of asking a vice-president to supplement a presentation to the president where additional support was needed. The sequence of approaches and the choice of messenger form part of the work. [PWR-W005, Table 2]

Formal procedures continue alongside these activities. The managers' preparation sought both timely approval and cooperation once an approved proposal had to be implemented. The authors describe functions of coordinating intentions, avoiding stalled decisions and making subsequent collaboration easier. Their evidence concerns proposal situations in hierarchical firms. They also observe that short, single interviews may make improper political conduct difficult to disclose. [PWR-W005, pp. 68–69]

Opponents, superiors and subordinates#

Pfeffer describes incorporating opponents into an institution as one way of changing a conflict. In his university example, a committee, modest resources and an official role gave critics a stake in the organization they had been challenging. Their attention came to include sustaining the committee itself. Another example concerns helping rivals obtain attractive positions elsewhere: Willie Brown supported assembly rivals' moves to other elected offices. A departure could remove local opposition while giving the departing person an opportunity worth accepting. These cases concern changes in participants' positions and interests, as well as arguments addressed to them. [PWR-W003]

Fong Keng-Highberger, Zhiyu Feng, Kai Chi Yam, Xiao-Ping Chen and Hu Li study a different configuration: a middle manager situated between a superior and subordinates. Their account distinguishes coercive power over subordinates from relational empowerment supplied by a senior manager. They propose that Machiavellian middle managers can cultivate close upward relationships while using abusive supervision downward. The two relations concern different dependencies and different sources of power. [PWR-W006, §§2–3]

The proposed coercive mechanism involves the expectation of punishment. A subordinate may comply to avoid abuse, making the withholding of an undesirable consequence valuable. Upward closeness supplies a different route, through the superior's relational support and empowerment. Perceived threats to the existing hierarchy enter the authors' explanation of why both strategies may intensify. In the laboratory manipulation, subordinates challenged the middle manager's usefulness and threatened to report the manager to the senior manager. [PWR-W006, §§3–4]

The research combines a laboratory study with 338 students in Singapore, a multiwave and multisource field study in China, and a two-wave study of 716 managers from the United States and United Kingdom. The results did not support every proposed relationship uniformly. The association between Machiavellianism and abusive supervision appeared in the laboratory and US/UK study but not in the China field study. The authors' general discussion preserves that inconsistency alongside the broader support for their model. Laboratory responses to a manipulated threat and reports of relationships at work are distinct parts of the evidence. [PWR-W006, §§4–7]

Positions that control access#

Alexander Beresford's account of gatekeeper politics examines the African National Congress through party documents, interviews and observations around its 2012 centenary policy conference. Party or public positions can place their occupants in control of access to resources and opportunities. Granting or denying that access helps form relations of support and dependence. Rivals consequently compete over the positions through which distribution is controlled. [PWR-W010]

Beresford follows this relation into patronage networks, accumulation and factional struggles. Access is important to aspiring elites, but also to ordinary people seeking opportunities amid inequality, unemployment and poverty. The relationships are therefore sustained by several kinds of need. His account does not restrict patronage to kinship or inherited traditional authority: loyalties can run through modern party organization and become volatile as alliances and positions change. Holding the gate and fighting over who holds it are connected parts of the political process he describes. [PWR-W010]

This gives organizational reform a difficult setting. Integrity measures, political education and membership procedures address conduct within the party, while access to office and resources continues to support the relationships under examination. Beresford questions whether disciplinary measures alone can resolve that problem. He places gatekeeping within a broader political economy of patronage

and accumulation, explicitly resisting an interpretation of it as a uniquely African deviation. [PWR-W010, conclusion]

В. П. Пугачев's 2006 discussion of public human-resource management uses a narrower conception of micropolitics. He identifies sustained action to obtain, use and preserve power or resource-distribution positions for personal interests conflicting with official organizational purposes. Scarce resources, weak accountability, areas left to discretion and mismatches between individual and organizational interests help explain the opportunities for such action in his diagnosis. [PWR-W008]

Pugachev also recognizes defensive motives. Employees who experience injustice or neglect may seek protection for themselves and those close to them. His account follows increasing suspicion into additional preparation, safeguards and monitoring, with energy diverted into protecting positions and countering other people's moves. This adverse conception of micropolitics differs from the broader treatment of informal coordination in the proposal study. The difference concerns which activities the authors include in the category, as well as how they evaluate them. [PWR-W008]

Ezequiel Alpuche de la Cruz, José Luis Bernal López and Cesaire Chiatchoua examine organizational power through property rights, contracts and hierarchy. In their institutional account, ownership supports appropriation of the benefits attached to rights, while contracts organize exchanges and the allocation of resources among competing uses. Control is consequently embedded in legal and organizational forms as well as exercised through personal relationships. Their discussion connects these forms with bounded rationality, transaction costs and control of uncertainty. S15 develops the neighboring history of property and residual authority in greater detail. [PWR-W012]

Historical networks and room to maneuver#

John Padgett and Christopher Ansell reconstruct the rise of the Medici through the marriage, economic and patronage networks of early fifteenth-century Florence. Cosimo's position linked relations that did not coincide neatly with a single unified faction. Their interpretation of robust action centers on multivocality: the same conduct could have coherent but different meanings for different participants. Ambiguity could preserve options when others sought to narrow them. [PWR-W011, pp. 1262–1264]

The historical development of the position is central to their explanation. They argue that Cosimo did not design the centralized party from the outset. Earlier oligarchic efforts to consolidate control helped produce the network configuration around the Medici. The political potential of that configuration became apparent through subsequent events. The authors therefore describe a process in which actors learned to use relationships formed partly through their opponents' actions. [PWR-W011, pp. 1286– 1287]

Cosimo's restrained public role and multiple identities also enter their account of control. Different participants could interpret his actions through their particular relations with him, while he maintained discretion and an appearance above the immediate conflict of interests. The authors' explanation combines the relational position with its historical formation and the style of action it made possible. It does not depend on Cosimo having specified the eventual outcome in advance. [PWR-W011, conclusion]

Personality, conduct and responses within the organization#

Cameron Anderson, Daron Sharps, Christopher Soto and Oliver John investigate whether disagreeable people attain more power at work. Their preregistered longitudinal studies connect personality measured before entry to the labor market with power assessed about fourteen years later. In Study 1, the 457 participants reported workplace power, control over subordinates and organizational rank. Disagreeableness did not predict greater power overall. [PWR-W007]

The authors' examination of conduct helps interpret the result. Dominant-aggressive behavior was associated with greater power, while less communal and generous conduct was associated with less power. The opposing associations could offset one another at the level of personality. This concerns a different predictor and time horizon from a particular upward or downward influence tactic. The study examines attained workplace power rather than treating a single successful confrontation as the outcome. [PWR-W007]

Juliana Mansur and Filipe Sobral examine employees' experience of organizational politics in three private firms based in Rio de Janeiro. Their 2011 study analyzed 157 valid questionnaire responses. It related perceived politics to satisfaction, reported performance evaluations, neglect and intentions to leave. The authors found adverse relationships with satisfaction and performance and positive relationships with neglect and exit intentions. [PWR-W009, §§3–4]

Their discussion situates these findings in a setting associated with jeitinho, while the observations concern the particular firms and respondents studied. The measured outcomes show how employees experience and respond to a political workplace. The Japanese proposal study, by contrast, develops managers' accounts of using informal preparation to secure decisions and subsequent cooperation. Their objects of inquiry and outcomes differ: one follows employees' perceptions and work responses, the other the conduct of a proposal through an approval process. [PWR-W009, §5; PWR-W005]

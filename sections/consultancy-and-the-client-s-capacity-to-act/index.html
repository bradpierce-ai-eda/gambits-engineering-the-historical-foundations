Inquiring into an engagement together#

Lambrechts and colleagues describe Edgar Schein’s process consultation as joint inquiry. A consultant and client consider an interaction while the participants can still examine how it unfolds. That work differs in purpose from a consultant’s report produced after collecting facts from others. The authors develop Schein’s account through their emphasis on relationships and on choices participants can author together. Their reconstruction gives process consultation a place among different ways of organizing a commission. [T06-W001]

Loizos Heracleous distinguishes interventions that ask a client to explain an episode from those that supply a concept or suggest an action. In his account of working with NASA, he describes a two-hour workshop on how to assess the value of the agency’s relationships with other organizations. The senior sponsor introduced the workshop and left. Heracleous supplied a framework for discussing the relationships, and participants used it to consider what criteria their agency could adopt. They did not finish choosing the criteria within the time allotted. [T06-W002]

The sponsor later expressed dissatisfaction with how little had been settled. The division leader and other participants told Heracleous that the framework had helped them think through the relationships and that they intended to use it in subsequent internal discussions. Heracleous decided against a further intervention. His account records the sponsor’s judgment and the participants’ intended next step. [T06-W002]

Work on both sides of the commission#

Isabel Boni-Le Goff examines management consultants in France through the demands of client service and the career pressures inside consulting firms. Consultants move across assignments and present work to clients while doing other work for colleagues and supervisors. The expectation of advancement or departure creates a second audience for their efforts. The account distinguishes what a client receives from the professional judgments governing a consultant’s career. [T06-W003]

Francesco Ciampi asks what knowledge is created during consulting and which organization can use it. His inquiry includes interviews with 15 medium and large Italian firms in 2015. Client learning and a consultancy’s ability to reuse what it learned are separate outcomes in this account. S53 addresses a related question about a recipient’s capacity to absorb outside knowledge. [T06-W004]

Following an intervention into operations#

In Bloom and colleagues’ field experiment in Indian textile firms, treatment and comparison plants both received a month of diagnostic consulting. The treatment plants received a further four months of intensive help putting recommended practices into use. One practice was to classify and record weaving defects daily. The authors illustrate how the resulting quality index could flag a faulty loom for discussion at the next day’s quality meeting, where it had previously been possible for such a problem to persist for weeks. [T06-W005]

After the implementation phase, the authors estimated that the treatment reduced the weighted quality-defect index by about 43 percent relative to the comparison, based on the intervention coefficient in Table II. They also reported lower yarn inventories and higher weaving output. The defect estimate is for the intervention package. Daily logging and review are among the practices the authors use to explain how it could improve quality. [T06-W005]

The authors also tracked practices at five other plants owned by treatment firms. By August 2010, adoption at those plants had risen by 17.5 percentage points. The authors report that firm directors copied practices from the plants that received the intervention. The researchers returned in 2011 to collect longer-run performance and management data. The trial’s comparison establishes an operational result, and the account of copying shows a specific use of the practices elsewhere within the firms. [T06-W005]

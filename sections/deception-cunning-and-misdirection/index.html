S93 Deception, cunning and misdirection #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

A concealed intention, a misleading statement and a sporting feint present different problems. Writers have asked how an opponent interprets appearances, how people sustain deceptive practices together, how an audience reconstructs what it has seen and which forms of confidence may legitimately be invoked. Their accounts include strategic prescriptions, exemplary narratives, organizational interpretations, experiments and legal distinctions.

Assessment, appearances and military strength#

In 孫子兵法, assessment precedes the instructions to present misleading appearances. 始計 compares

rulers, commanders, conditions and discipline. 兵勢 develops the combinations of straightforward and

extraordinary operations; 虛實 connects concealment of one's disposition and inducement of the enemy with the concentration and dispersal of forces. An opponent obliged to prepare in many places must distribute defenses, while a commander who conceals the intended point of engagement can concentrate.

軍爭 adds the management of direct and roundabout routes. Preparation, display and deployment therefore work together in the text's strategic prescriptions. [DC-W01]

Lisa Raphals reconstructs a disagreement within early Chinese thought over deception and military strength. Her Sunzi combines direct and indirect operations; she objects to interpretations that make Chinese strategy uniformly indirect or equate its deliberate preparation with effortless action. Her

reading of 荀子 distinguishes morally constructive artifice from deception. In 議兵, Linwu praises positional advantage and dissimulation, whereas Xunzi makes popular attachment and orderly government the basis of military strength. He argues that deception cannot break the army of a humane ruler. Raphals also identifies substantial agreement on military preparations. The disagreement concerns the sources of strength, deception's acceptability and its efficacy, within a textual dialogue whose teaching rhetoric shapes the presentation. [DC-W02]

Stratagem collections and princely dissimulation#

Frontinus's Strategemata arranges examples for commanders seeking usable precedents. Its preface distinguishes the broad conduct of strategy from particular ingenious stratagems and organizes material around situations before battle, in battle and in siege warfare. Book I.1 tells how Ventidius used an intermediary known to pass information to the Parthians. He displayed fear of the route he wanted them to take and confidence about the route he feared. In Frontinus's telling, the enemy chose the slower crossing, allowing reinforcements to arrive. The intermediary's reporting makes the expression of preference consequential: the intended recipient hears it as intelligence about what Ventidius wants to avoid. Frontinus presents the sequence as an instructive example. [DC-W03]

Chapter XVIII of Machiavelli's Il Principe places dissimulation within the problem of preserving princely rule under changing circumstances. The fox recognizes traps and the lion frightens wolves; neither capacity substitutes for the other. Machiavelli argues that promises may cease to bind a ruler when the conditions that occasioned them disappear. He distinguishes possessing admired qualities from appearing to possess them and remaining able to act otherwise when necessity requires. Alexander VI illustrates the contrast: Machiavelli describes him as exceptionally forceful in his assurances and oaths, exceptionally unreliable in observing them, and nevertheless successful in deceiving people. The example supports his argument that an appearance of fidelity can coexist with conduct that violates it. He also

argues that people judge largely by what they see, while few can test the reality behind appearances. Public recognition and adaptability therefore enter his account of maintaining princely power. [DC-W04]

Practical intelligence and political counsel#

Marcel Meulder's 1975 review presents Détienne and Vernant's account of mētis as practical, resourceful intelligence operating amid unstable circumstances. It enables people to overcome another's strength and find ways around natural obstacles. Its reach includes human crafts as well as deliberate deception. Navigation and chariot guidance connect skilled direction with the construction of ships and chariots; in Meulder's reconstruction, the pilot must know the available passages and anticipate chance and the opportune moment. The relevant intelligence deals with changing, multiple and ambiguous circumstances. Meulder places this practical intelligence against its devaluation in Plato and praises the book for challenging an excessively Platonic picture of Greek antiquity. His review thus gives cunning a place in human skill and practical judgment beyond the production of false beliefs. [DC-W05]

Olga Torres reads al-Harawi's manual as political and military counsel associated with Ayyubid state-building. Its chapter outline places officials, counsel and envoys alongside campaigns and sieges. Torres describes advice to receive an enemy envoy warmly, seek counsel and encourage dissatisfaction with the envoy's own patron. She connects this with an attempted inducement of the Green Knight, who refused. The recommended approach and the reported response reveal different aspects of the account: a prescription for cultivating disaffection and a historical reconstruction in which the intended recipient resisted. Torres also discusses uncertainty over whether the manual's intended patron was Saladin or his son. [DC-W06]

Michel Pretalli's introduction to Ruse et magie de l'Antiquité à nos jours follows an earlier inquiry into military stratagems. He recalls that project's definition of military ruse through ingenious artifice, mistaken representations and surprise, then emphasizes its moral ambiguity. An ingenious escape from a desperate situation can appear honorable in one telling and dishonorable or diabolical in another. Moving into magic changes the setting and the meanings attached to secrecy and extraordinary effects. His historical project brings these practices into conversation while investigating the judgments made about them. [DC-W21]

Strategic assessment and reflexive control#

Richards Heuer applies cognitive psychology to intelligence assessment. Reinforcing an existing interpretation, he argues, is often easier than replacing it: discrepant information can be absorbed into a settled diagnosis, while organizational commitment to earlier reports makes revision harder. He also examines the planner's vulnerability. Familiarity with a planned message can make its meaning seem clearer than it will be to an opponent. Greater suspicion is not his sole answer to counterdeception. Salient stories may encourage analysts to overestimate deception's prevalence, and more collection or alertness may leave interpretation unchanged. He recommends cognitive aids and a counterdeception staff to give the analytic problem sustained attention. [DC-W07]

Georgy Smolyan explains reflexive control as influencing a decision by supplying the grounds on which another participant chooses. He opens with Lefebvre's illustration of Brer Rabbit pleading with Brer Fox to do anything except throw him into the thorn bush. The plea presents a destination as something Rabbit fears. In Smolyan's illustration, that apparent aversion supplies Fox with a reason to choose the very action Rabbit seeks. The influence works through the grounds of the opponent's choice rather than an overt command. Smolyan connects this with concealed direction that preserves a participant's conviction that the choice is free. His conceptual account concerns manipulation of the decision, including what the

target regards as desirable or threatening. Reflexive control in this account differs from the market reflexivity discussed in S59. [DC-W08]

João Ricardo da Cunha Croce Lopes's professional exposition distinguishes demonstrations of force, false information, effects on the decision process and effects on decision timing. Demonstrations include overt threats and readiness displays; false information includes concealed dispositions and apparent strengths. A timing intervention can seek to delay the opponent by directing attention elsewhere. The repertoire he reconstructs through military writers, including Ionov, therefore reaches beyond lying. Pressure and concealment enter alongside attempts to shape interpretation. These distinctions form part of Lopes's interpretation of reflexive control and its military uses. [DC-W09]

Hayden Peake's review describes Whaley's STRATAGEM as a way of reasoning about deception operations, supported by a collection of surprise cases. He distinguishes its discussion of history, theory, ethics and counterdeception from its summaries of 115 instances of wartime surprise, divided into strategic and tactical cases. Peake reports Whaley's objection to a signals-obscured-by-noise explanation of deception: the objection concerns its failure to predict deception, whereas Whaley proposes looking for clues that point to deception itself. Peake presents this as a way of thinking and studying operations, rather than a step-by-step recipe for assessing or conducting them, and notes Whaley's acknowledgment of chance. His appraisal also raises a problem within the case collection: he reports that one celebrated ruse attributed to Richard Meinertzhagen was fabricated, while accepting the principles it illustrates. Peake thereby distinguishes the standing of an illustrative story from that of the principles associated with it. [DC-W10]

Lying within organizations#

Sarah Jenkins and Rick Delbridge relate lying to organizational contexts and social relations. Coercion, compensation and condoning describe different pressures or permissions; cooperative, caring and conflictual relations help explain why employees comply, cope or challenge. Their reconstruction of Sallaz's CallCo study supplies a concrete sequence. Short call-handling targets, a concealed depot contact and promises of same-day redelivery helped staff end calls without resolving customers' problems. Their account distinguishes tolerated coping from an explicit order to lie. Targets, working relationships and the practical handling of calls enter the authors' relational explanation together. [DC-W11]

David Shulman separates notorious criminal fraud, familiar workplace deception and forms easily overlooked in nonprofits. Misleading donors, favoring clients and diverting budgets to preferred causes differ from the crimes that dominate public discussion. His exploratory explanation connects resource pressure, moral commitment, small size, autonomy and limited career paths with opportunities and inducements to mislead. A worker may defend an organization's mission while hiding how its work is carried out. His essay challenges the inference that benevolent purposes make deception unlikely, while warning that American sector divisions and scandal reporting do not establish worldwide rates. [DC-W12]

David Arellano Gault examines corruption as an organizational process. A small lie that passes unnoticed may require further lies to sustain it, while colleagues and authorities can draw others into maintaining the account. Rationalizing language can recast bribery as ordinary assistance to getting work done. Repeated action, shared justification and socialization support his explanation of persistence. His conceptual interpretation concerns how corruption becomes normalized, beyond the identification of an individual's moral defect. Deception enters this account through practices and justifications that participants sustain together. [DC-W13]

Emotion and misleading truths in negotiation#

Joseph Gaspar and Maurice Schweitzer explain deception decisions through present and anticipated emotion, as well as incentives and information asymmetry. Present feelings may arise from the negotiation itself or unrelated events. Their emotion-deception model proposes that these feelings affect both what negotiators think about and which opportunities they notice. Anticipated consequences, including guilt, can influence the decision before it is made. Their discussion of incidental anger proposes a path through reduced trust to defensive deception. Their review also questions the dominance of one-shot role-play studies: later discovery, retaliation and continuing relationships can change what an account of negotiation must explain. [DC-W14]

Todd Rogers and colleagues distinguish paltering, the active use of true statements to create a false impression, from false assertions and silence. In Study 4B, buyers with private knowledge about rezoning negotiated with sellers online. Buyers instructed to palter could answer a question about intended use by invoking their history of residential development. The authors report more mistaken seller beliefs in that condition. Buyers obtained more surplus among completed deals; including impasses removed the profit difference. Sellers also judged discovered paltering more harshly than buyers judged themselves. The study connects an assigned response, the inference it encouraged and measured outcomes in an incentivized role-play negotiation. [DC-W15]

Misdirection and sporting feints#

Gustav Kuhn and colleagues distinguish misdirection affecting perception, memory and reasoning. Attention control is only part of their taxonomy. Separating a method from its apparent effect can make the earlier action difficult to reconstruct. Incidental actions, convincing apparent shuffles and retrospective descriptions can shape what an audience remembers. The spectator must nevertheless see enough of the effect to experience the impossibility; preventing observation altogether would defeat the performance. Their psychologically based taxonomy links craft practices and research through the relation between a concealed method, the visible event and its later reconstruction. [DC-W16]

Mustafa Alhaj Ahmad Alaboud and colleagues investigate whether a head fake's frequency changes its effect. Participants identify pass direction while a pictured player's gaze agrees or conflicts with it; blocks vary the proportion of deceptive trials. The experiments use static and apparent-motion displays. Conflicting gaze interferes with identifying the pass, and the effect is reduced when deceptive displays become frequent. Immediate trial history also enters the analysis, with differing strength across experiments. The measured result concerns identifying a displayed pass under these conditions. [DC-W17]

Plausible requests and security procedures#

Fatima Salahdine and Naima Kaabouch describe security attacks that combine a plausible social situation with a technical or physical action. In pretexting, an invented scenario gives a request for information a reason. In tailgating, a request to hold a door substitutes social cooperation for a credential check. Baiting supplies an apparently desirable object carrying the technical compromise. These sequences differ in what the participant is asked to accept and do. The survey also classifies attacks by contact and medium, organizing a security repertoire that crosses social, physical and technical activity. [DC-W18]

Ruses and protected confidence#

Article 37 of Additional Protocol I distinguishes perfidy from permissible ruses of war. Its prohibition addresses killing, injuring or capturing an adversary through betrayal of confidence in protection under

the applicable law. Feigned surrender is one example. Paragraph 2 permits ruses that mislead or induce recklessness without breaching applicable rules or invoking legally protected confidence; camouflage, decoys, mock operations and misinformation are examples. The operative boundary concerns the confidence invited, the intention to betray it and the prohibited result. The legal classification gives a different question from whether the misleading conduct achieves its purpose. [DC-W19]

吉田靖之 examines how rules developed around visual identification bear on contemporary naval deception. His discussion distinguishes navigation lights, an apparent ship identity and a claim to protected legal status. Lights can indicate movement or vessel characteristics without necessarily asserting immunity from attack. His treatment of false flags distinguishes conduct before an attack from conduct during one and identifies specially protected categories that cannot simply be impersonated as an ordinary ruse. Signals and appearances acquire legal significance through the status they claim and the circumstances in which they are used. His analysis makes those distinctions within the naval rules and manuals he discusses. [DC-W20]

The accounts place judgment in different settings: preparing an enemy's interpretation, sustaining an organization's explanation, reconstructing an apparent impossibility and invoking a protected status. Their practical questions involve what others can observe, what they infer, how they respond and which expectations the participants are entitled to rely on.

S55 Numerical fidelity and computational cost #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

A numerical computation can use different amounts of arithmetic precision in different places. The accounts below explain how programmers and numerical analysts select those places, check the returned result and pay for correction or monitoring. Their tasks range from solving a specified linear system to accepting a measured change in an application’s output. The required answer and the means of judging it enter the procedure alongside the available arithmetic.

S35 develops conditioning, stability and preparation of numerical problems. Here the focus is the allocation of fidelity during computation: what work can become cheaper, what must remain accurate and what happens when the initial allocation proves insufficient.

Buttari and colleagues: a cheap factorization with more accurate correction#

Buttari and colleagues divide the work of solving a dense linear system, Ax = b, between two arithmetic formats. They convert the matrix and right-hand side to single precision, factor the converted matrix and obtain an initial solution. They preserve the original double-precision matrix. Each subsequent correction begins by calculating the residual, b − Ax, using that original matrix in double precision. The existing single-precision factors are then reused to solve for a correction, which is added to the solution in double precision. The analogous procedure uses Cholesky factorization for a symmetric positive-definite system. [CG-W001, §1.1, Algorithms 2–3]

The procedure places the cubic-cost factorization in the faster arithmetic while retaining more accurate residual calculation and solution updates, whose cost per refinement step is quadratic. It also retains two matrix representations and conversions between them. Single precision therefore describes a particular part of the computation; it does not replace the accuracy required of the returned answer. The paper's error analysis makes the result conditional on a sufficiently well-conditioned matrix relative to the correction arithmetic and an adequately stable correction solver. Low-precision errors must still permit refinement to make progress. [CG-W001, §1.1 and Appendix A]

Their implementation comparisons show why the size and architecture matter. On the conventional processors tested, the reported gains reach approximately 1.8 for the nonsymmetric solver and 1.5 for the positive-definite solver when problems are sufficiently large. For small systems, even a few correction steps can outweigh the cheaper factorization. The Cell results use a different comparison: the mixed-precision solver's achieved throughput is compared with the processor's double-precision peak, giving the reported factors of seven and eleven. The experiments provide implementation evidence for this division of arithmetic work; the graph labels and processor configurations specify what was compared. [CG-W001, §1.2, Table 2 and Figures 1–8]

Carson and Higham: correction accuracy and convergence are different requirements#

Carson and Higham make three arithmetic roles independently adjustable: factorization, storage of data and solution, and residual calculation. Their general analysis also distinguishes the effective accuracy with which a correction equation is solved. This fourth parameter is useful when a low-precision factorization supports a more accurate iterative correction. An algorithm can thus store its answer in single precision, factor in half precision and compute residuals in double precision. Their conditions distinguish the rate at which errors decrease from the accuracy eventually attainable. More accurate residuals can lower the final forward-error level without repairing a correction process that fails to converge. [CG-W002, Algorithm 1.1, §§2–4 and 7]

Their GMRES variant uses the low-precision factors as a preconditioner, rather than relying on their triangular solves alone to supply each correction. This can extend the class of systems for which the solution reaches working-precision accuracy. The procedure still needs the original matrix and accurate applications of the preconditioned operator. Half precision's narrow numerical range also requires attention to scaling; when the initial solve overflows, the authors describe starting refinement from zero instead. [CG-W002, §§6 and 8, Algorithm 8.1]

The experiments expose a limit that a count of outer refinement steps would miss. For one generated 100-by-100 system, three outer steps contain 91, 92 and 92 GMRES iterations. A different family, with one small singular value, reaches the required error level with seven GMRES iterations in total despite a very large condition number. Every inner iteration performs additional accurate work. The authors accordingly distinguish successful numerical correction from an economical implementation: enough inner iterations can erase the benefit of a cheaper factorization. These tests use MATLAB, including emulated half precision, to examine error and iteration behavior. The paper's potential speed advantage remains conditional on hardware and correction cost. [CG-W002, §§10–11, Figures 10.7 and 10.9]

Precimonious: search against an explicit acceptance test#

Rubio-González and colleagues turn precision allocation into a search over program variants. The programmer supplies a program, test inputs and an accuracy requirement. Precimonious identifies floating-point variables whose types can change and, where alternatives are supplied, calls that can use another mathematical-function implementation. It generates candidate programs, compiles and runs them, and retains changes only when the results pass the accuracy and exception checks and the program satisfies the performance requirement. Their modified delta-debugging procedure tests groups of changes and progressively refines them. It seeks a locally minimal retained set of higher-precision variables rather than exhaustively finding the best possible allocation. [CG-W003, §§2–3, Figures 3–4]

The acceptance test compares a candidate's output with the original program's output on the supplied inputs. This provides a practical engineering contract, but its reference is the original implementation rather than an independently established mathematical answer. The authors' additional summation example makes the distinction consequential: truncating an infinite series and accumulating roundoff introduce different errors. Arithmetic precision can also change the number of iterations before a program stops, so apparently changing only a variable type can change the amount of work actually performed. [CG-W003, §§3.4 and 4.3]

Their experiments connect precision choices to execution cost. In the arc-length program, the reported speedup reaches 41.7% at two selected error thresholds. Several other programs improve less, while three produce no accepted faster configuration at the tested thresholds. The conversion instructions introduced between formats can outweigh savings elsewhere. A more permissive accuracy threshold consequently need not lead this search to a faster program. The FFT case with the strictest tested threshold explores 663 configurations and takes 116 minutes and 51 seconds. At publication, the suggested changes also required manual mapping back into source code, although transformed intermediate code was generated automatically. [CG-W003, §4.2, Tables 1–3; §5]

The authors test the limits of their input selection directly. They tune the NAS benchmarks using Class A inputs and subsequently try Classes B and C. Several allocations that pass the original test fail these new cases; for the EP benchmark, failures remain even at the most relaxed tested threshold. Its EP performance figure also has a specific scope: the 33.2% headline concerns the Gaussian-deviate phase that was tuned, while the reported improvement for the whole program averages 14%. [CG-W003, §4.2, Table 4 and footnote 6]

FPTuner: allocate precision under a conservative expression-wide bound#

Chiang and colleagues require a precision allocation to meet an error target throughout a declared input domain. Their object is a real-valued expression with a fixed computational structure, not an arbitrary program. They model rounding at operations and conversions, use symbolic Taylor expansions to relate these errors to the expression's output, and obtain conservative bounds over the input intervals. The allocation is then an optimization problem: maximize a weighted count of lower-precision operations while satisfying the error constraint. [CG-W004, §§2–4]

The procedure includes a further check. Its allocation search initially uses first-order error terms. FPTaylor then checks the generated allocation with higher-order effects included. If the candidate violates the target, the procedure can add an allowance for those effects and repeat the search. The authors report that the candidates in their experiments passed this check immediately. Thus the fast search approximation and the conservative acceptance check perform different jobs. Bounds on conversion error are included, and conversion counts can also be constrained. Groups of operations can be assigned the same precision to support vectorization. [CG-W004, §§4.1–5, Figure 4]

Their measurements show why a rigorous accuracy constraint does not make the cost objective exact. The objective's weighted operator count is a guide to allocation, not a direct measurement of execution time or energy. On a Jetson TK1 board, the authors measure single/double-precision versions using current and voltage instrumentation. Four selected benchmarks save energy on average when moved from all-double to one mixed allocation, but other configurations consume more energy or take longer. In the jetEngine expression, mixing formats increases register pressure and causes constants to be spilled and reloaded. The executed instructions defeat an apparent saving visible in the arithmetic allocation alone. [CG-W004, §6.3, Table 5 and Figures 7–8]

A separate timing comparison replaces some software-implemented quadruple-precision work with hardware-supported double precision. Those results have a different baseline from the Jetson comparison, where both formats have hardware support. The all-double version is also insufficient for the stricter error contracts used in the quadruple/double study; its faster time cannot be treated as an accuracy-matched alternative. Tuning itself can take seconds or, in one reported case, twenty minutes, followed by the final verification. [CG-W004, §§6.1–6.3 and 7.1]

The scope of the conservative result remains explicit. FPTuner treats straight-line expressions over bounded input domains. The paper does not implement general treatment of branches and loops: rounding can change a branch decision, and bounding the resulting difference is a separate problem. The contribution is a developed allocation and checking procedure for its stated expression model, accompanied by measurements that reveal when the intended resource gain survives compilation. [CG-W004, §§7.2–7.4]

Green: calibrate the effect on the output and restore accuracy when needed#

Baek and Chilimbi's Green system supports two deliberate approximations: replace an expensive function with a supplied alternative, or stop a loop before its original end. The programmer supplies an output-quality measure, a target and calibration inputs. Calibration relates the approximation to both its output effect and its performance and energy costs. Generated code then selects among function versions according to input ranges or determines when a loop can stop. In the adaptive loop policy, it also observes whether further iterations continue to improve the chosen quality measure. [CG-W005, §§2–3, Figures 1– 3]

The reference computation remains part of the operating arrangement. During sampled recalibration, Green records the quality that an early termination would have produced and then continues the precise

computation so that the difference can be assessed. It can increase the work retained, select a more accurate function version, or relax an approximation that is more accurate than the target requires. For a function input range where no available approximation meets the criterion, it uses the precise function. These comparisons and changes have a sampling cost. [CG-W005, §§2.2.3 and 3.2, Figures 3 and 5–7]

Local acceptance also has to survive combination. In the option-pricing benchmark, the locally selected exponential approximation and logarithm approximation do not together meet the application's target. Green changes the logarithm choice from one polynomial approximation to a more accurate one. The resulting combined version has less than 0.8% loss under the chosen output measure, with reported performance and energy improvements of 28.5% and 28%, respectively. The authors retain the application-level comparison rather than assuming that acceptable individual substitutions add up to an acceptable program. [CG-W005, §§3.4 and 4.4, Figures 8 and 23–24]

The search-engine experiment specifies a different output criterion. It counts queries whose ordered top results differ from the precise backend's results; it does not measure human judgments of relevance. Tests use a production index and query logs, with crawling and index updates disabled. One adaptive configuration improves throughput by 21% and reduces joules per query by 14%, while 0.27% of queries produce a different ordered result. Whole-system energy is measured from the main power cable. [CG-W005, §§4.1–4.3, Figures 10–12]

An intentionally inaccurate calibration model exposes the time required for restoration. The chosen target allows 2% differing queries, but the initial cutoff typically produces about 10%. After each 10,000 queries, Green runs the next 100 precisely and uses their comparison to increase the retained search work. In this experiment it reaches the target after 180,000 queries. More frequent monitoring could accelerate adaptation while consuming more resources. The paper's claims about statistical quality rest on calibration and observed target tracking; this experiment makes the delay visible rather than establishing a bound on every query. [CG-W005, §4.3, Figure 14]

EnerJ: protect the part that must remain precise#

Sampson and colleagues distinguish permission to approximate from the requirement to preserve precise computation. EnerJ extends Java with types that mark data as approximate or precise. Approximate values can use less reliable storage or operations, and approximate objects can select a supplied alternative algorithm. Unannotated data remains precise. The type system prevents approximate information from flowing into precise state without an explicit endorsement by the programmer. [CG-W006, §§2.1–2.5]

That protection includes indirect routes. An approximate condition cannot silently determine a branch that changes precise state. Array indices and lengths remain precise, and the implementation does not approximate pointers. An endorsement permits a selected approximate value to cross this boundary. It does not repair or recompute the value; the programmer accepts responsibility for how the receiving computation uses it. A checksum, for example, can be calculated precisely over data whose earlier production was approximate. Precisely encoding or storing that data preserves the selected value rather than proving it was the right value. [CG-W006, §§2.2, 2.4, 2.6 and 5.1]

The paper states a noninterference property for a core language without endorsements: changing approximate values does not change its precise state. This is an isolation property. Approximate values themselves have no quantitative accuracy guarantee. The conference article develops the language rules, states the properties and describes the implemented checker. [CG-W006, §3]

Their resource study uses a simulated approximation-aware architecture. It combines reduced floating-point mantissas, less reliable operations and storage, and reduced memory refresh. The energy model counts instruction execution and SRAM and DRAM storage; instruction fetch and decode retain their cost.

Its parameters combine earlier measurements with values explicitly identified as estimates. The model omits implementation and switching overheads, including voltage-scaling latency. The article reports potential CPU/memory energy savings ranging from 7% to 38%, not measurements from constructed approximate hardware. [CG-W006, §§4–5 and 6.1, Table 2 and Figure 4]

Application quality varies independently of that range. Means over twenty simulated runs show relatively small losses under mild approximation, while FFT and SOR lose substantial fidelity at stronger settings. The programmer also has work beyond adding declarations. For the barcode reader, the authors changed an out-of-bounds image-coordinate response to return a white pixel, then endorsed approximate coordinates where they became array indices. This adaptation made the computation more tolerant of errors; it was not performed automatically by the type system. EnerJ's developed contribution is the explicit protected boundary, with application-specific approximation and acceptance still supplied around it. [CG-W006, §§6.2–6.3, Figure 5]

The mandatory and optional task portions in S57 address another way of reducing computational work: stopping a refinement or choosing a shorter procedure while still delivering an acceptable result. That account concerns task execution and scheduling; the procedures here specify arithmetic roles, output comparisons and protected computation.

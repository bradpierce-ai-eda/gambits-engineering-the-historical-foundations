S61 Late-mover advantage and strategic waiting #

Entering later can mean arriving after someone else has paid to discover what works. A follower may encounter a proven technology, informed customers, trained workers and an established market. Delaying an investment can also leave a choice open until better information arrives. These possibilities raise different questions about what becomes available with time, who produces the knowledge and whether the opportunity remains open to the actor who waits.

The investment and organizational literatures examine these questions through accounts of pioneering and imitation, irreversible commitments, observable investment decisions and shared experimentation. The treatment of optionality and flexibility elsewhere concerns the choices an actor preserves; the accounts here develop the timing of investment and the relationships between pioneers and followers. Absorptive capacity provides a related explanation of how firms acquire and use external knowledge. [AFC-W017; AB-W001]

What followers can obtain from pioneers#

Marvin Lieberman and David Montgomery’s 1988 First-mover advantages organizes the benefits of entering early around technological leadership, control of scarce assets and buyer switching costs. Its account of first-mover disadvantages explains why pioneering can also create opportunities for followers. A pioneer may pay for research, the discovery of a viable market and the education of buyers, while later entrants use knowledge and demand that those expenditures helped create. The follower does not necessarily reproduce the pioneer’s sequence of unsuccessful trials. [SW-W001]

The authors separate several channels through which this happens. Technical knowledge can spread through published information, imitation and the movement of personnel. Infrastructure and buyer education can serve more than the firm that financed them. A pioneer’s marketing may establish that a category of product is useful, making the task of a subsequent seller easier. How much the pioneer retains depends on the ability to appropriate these gains. Patents, secrecy, cumulative learning and specialized complementary assets can give an early entrant advantages that a follower cannot simply purchase. [SW-W001]

Waiting can also resolve uncertainty about the product and the market. An early entrant must choose a design before knowing which characteristics customers will value or which technical approach will prevail. A follower can observe that experience and concentrate on a more clearly defined target. The opportunity is particularly visible when an industry moves from competing designs toward a dominant design. Early entry may help a firm shape that outcome; later entry may let it avoid commitments to designs that disappear. In Lieberman and Montgomery’s analysis, those are different possible consequences of the same period of uncertainty. [SW-W001]

Technological change and incumbent inertia provide two further routes. A new technology may make an established firm’s knowledge and equipment less valuable while allowing an entrant to start with the newer approach. An incumbent may rationally continue harvesting an existing investment or hesitate to displace its own profitable products. Organizational routines can also make a new opportunity difficult to perceive or pursue. These explanations differ from a follower merely copying a finished invention: they concern the relationship between past success and the firm’s ability to change direction. [SW-W001]

Complementary assets can decide which firm captures an innovation’s returns. Lieberman and Montgomery discuss EMI’s computed-tomography scanner and Pilkington’s float-glass process as contrasting illustrations of the ability to retain the benefits of pioneering. Manufacturing, marketing, distribution and the organization of licensing can matter alongside the invention itself. Their question is consequently more precise than whether being first is good. It concerns which advantage an early

position creates, how durable it is and whether a later entrant possesses assets that make the innovation easier to exploit. [SW-W001]

Entry order and the resources of the entrant#

In their 1998 retrospective, Lieberman and Montgomery give greater emphasis to the two-way relationship between entry timing and resources. Entering early can build customer recognition, experience and privileged access to resources. But a firm’s resources also influence when it can enter and whether it is better suited to pioneering or following. An organization with strong manufacturing and distribution may be able to exploit an opportunity that another firm first developed. A firm without the knowledge needed to enter early may arrive late without having chosen delay as a strategy. [SW-W002]

This distinction changes how evidence about entry order is interpreted. Successful early entrants may have possessed advantages before entering, rather than receiving all of them from being first. Conversely, comparing surviving pioneers with surviving followers can hide pioneers that failed. The retrospective discusses research distinguishing inventors, product pioneers and market pioneers, and shows why the category being defined matters. The first supplier of a technology need not be the first firm to develop the market in which a later product succeeds. [SW-W002]

The authors also locate some resources outside the entrant’s own organization. Customers’ beliefs, habits and switching costs can preserve an early firm’s position, while standards and network effects can make a market more difficult to enter after it has developed. Their resource-based interpretation therefore includes both what a follower can acquire from earlier activity and what that earlier activity may make harder to obtain. [SW-W002]

A profitable investment that is worth postponing#

Robert Pindyck’s 1991 Irreversibility, Uncertainty, and Investment explains why an investment with positive net present value can nevertheless be worth postponing. His opening factory example makes one widget a year forever at no operating cost. Construction costs $800. The widget sells for $100 initially; a year later its price will either rise to $150 or fall to $50, with equal probabilities, and then stay there. At a 10 percent discount rate, immediate construction has a net present value of $300. Waiting a year and building only after the favorable price change is worth about $386. [SW-W004]

The comparison includes the production forgone while waiting. The gain comes from retaining the ability to reject the unfavorable investment. Immediate construction exchanges that choice for an operating factory. Pindyck treats the opportunity given up as part of the cost of committing now. In this example, the difference of about $86 is the value of waiting after allowing for delayed production and discounting. A positive conventional net present value does not by itself establish that immediate construction is the best available action. [SW-W004]

Irreversibility gives the later choice its force. The investor cannot undo construction and recover the full outlay when the price falls. The opportunity to invest must also remain available next year. If construction could be reversed without loss, or if the opportunity would disappear unless taken immediately, the alternatives would change. Pindyck’s example makes the information arrive through the passage of time. His separate account of research and demonstration projects, discussed in Learning before commitment, concerns information that early expenditure itself produces. [SW-W004]

Learning from another investor’s action#

Christophe Chamley and Douglas Gale’s 1994 model investigates delay when investment decisions reveal information. Each agent knows whether it has an investment opportunity, while the distribution of

opportunities contains information about the state of the economy and hence the attractiveness of investment. An agent observes how many others invest and can use that evidence when deciding whether to exercise its own opportunity. Waiting can improve its information without requiring it to undertake the first investment. [SW-W005]

The informational relationship is isolated from ordinary product-market competition. Another agent’s investment does not directly reduce the investor’s payoff by taking a customer or increasing supply. Its action matters because it is informative. That separation makes it possible to examine a pure informational externality: an investment can provide useful evidence to other decision makers, while the investor does not capture all the value of that evidence. [SW-W005]

The timing of information is endogenous. There is no independent announcement that automatically supplies everything the agents want to learn. Information appears through actions, so an agent’s wish to observe others affects the very process from which it hopes to learn. The model produces costly delay and the possibility that investment collapses. An absence of investment can itself discourage further investment, rather than providing a neutral pause in an otherwise unchanged sequence. [SW-W005]

Chamley and Gale also examine the interval between opportunities to revise decisions. Faster reaction does not automatically improve the collective result: their analysis shows how a rapid succession of responses can bring investment to an early halt. It explains a mechanism by which individually useful waiting can reduce the supply of informative actions. [SW-W005]

Experimentation as a shared source of knowledge#

Patrick Bolton and Christopher Harris’s 1999 Strategic Experimentation studies actors who can actively generate information while also learning from one another. Each chooses how much effort to allocate between a safe activity with a known return and a risky activity whose underlying quality is uncertain. The risky activity can be good or bad. Its noisy results gradually reveal which state holds. The players observe one another’s actions and outcomes, so an experiment undertaken by one player informs the others. [SW-W006]

The players share a changing belief about the risky activity. When they are sufficiently optimistic, they devote their effort to it; when sufficiently pessimistic, they abandon it in favor of the safe return. Between those regions, the symmetric equilibrium divides effort between the two activities. Compared with a team that jointly values all the experimental information, independent players have an incentive to rely on the experiments of others. Each receives only part of the total benefit created by its own information-producing effort. [SW-W006]

Bolton and Harris identify an encouragement effect alongside free-riding. A player’s experiment may produce favorable evidence that induces others to experiment in the future. Their subsequent observations then benefit the original experimenter. An action can therefore be attractive partly because it changes the future willingness of other players to supply information. This feedback can sustain experimentation that would be missed by considering only the immediate return and the information generated by one isolated experiment. [SW-W006]

The balance is more complicated than a fixed amount of experimentation divided among more beneficiaries. The equilibrium allocation changes as beliefs change; an additional player changes both the opportunity to free-ride and the prospect of receiving future observations. Bolton and Harris show that individual equilibrium payoffs rise with the number of players, while aggregate experimentation need not vary monotonically. Even an arbitrarily large group does not in general attain the payoff available with full information. These results preserve both the gains from shared experience and the incentives governing its production. [SW-W006]

When competition accelerates commitment#

Shinsuke Kamoto’s 2006 study of market entry under uncertainty examines the competing pressure to enter before a rival. Two firms face irreversible investment and uncertain demand. The first entrant initially serves the market alone. After the second enters, the first retains an advantage through its ability to choose output first. The model makes the attraction of a first position part of the timing problem rather than assuming that each firm can preserve its investment opportunity independently of its competitor. [SW-W007]

The option to wait, familiar from a single-investor calculation, now interacts with preemption. Waiting for a more favorable demand level can expose a firm to being overtaken. The first-mover advantage can bring entry forward relative to the timing that would be chosen without that race. Kamoto’s analysis also considers demand jumps and the risk that demand falls soon after an irreversible outlay. A firm can win the early position while still undertaking the investment at a moment that exposes it to an unfavorable subsequent state. [SW-W007]

This model gives the timing question a different structure from Chamley and Gale’s informational externality. There, other investments are informative without directly changing the agent’s payoff through competition. Here, who enters first changes the competitive relationship itself. The choice preserved by waiting depends on whether the actor will still be able to obtain the position it wants. [SW-W007; SW-W005]

How latecomers acquire the knowledge to compete#

Momoko Kawakami’s 2018 study follows MediaTek and MStar into systems-on-chip for LCD televisions. The transition from analog cathode-ray-tube sets to digital LCD televisions shifted many integration tasks into semiconductor components. A chip supplier could supply a larger part of a television’s functioning system, rather than one narrowly separated part. Kawakami examines how these later entrants acquired both component knowledge and knowledge of the finished television, allowing them to supply what she calls problem-solving core components. [SW-W008]

Her interview-based account identifies five routes for acquiring component knowledge. The firms transferred technologies and integration experience from their existing businesses, bought intellectual property from specialist suppliers, learned through commercial integration with established chip vendors, obtained technical information through television manufacturers and worked directly with manufacturers on tasks such as picture adjustment. Some transfers were paid and formal; others passed through working relationships and informal circulation of technical material. The possibility of learning from earlier suppliers was realized through identifiable channels. [SW-W008]

The existing businesses supplied different starting points. MediaTek brought knowledge from optical-disc-drive chips, while MStar drew on LCD-monitor chips. Suppliers of complementary components sometimes shared integration knowledge to secure a place for their own products in a recommended design. Over time, the Taiwanese firms could replace some of those components with their own developments. Such exchanges connected a supplier’s immediate interest in selling its product with the recipient’s accumulation of technical competence. [SW-W008]

Product-level knowledge developed through helping customers solve problems. Newer television manufacturers faced shortages of chips, limited technical support, differing regional specifications and the need to combine panels and software rapidly. MediaTek and MStar offered extensive support and reference designs, including engineers working near or within important customers’ development activities. Solving a customer’s problem exposed the supplier to requirements and adjustment knowledge

that it could use in later designs. Better support helped it obtain further customers, whose problems opened further learning opportunities. [SW-W008]

Kawakami’s history explains more than access to an already developed technology. It describes the accumulation of an ability to integrate components and satisfy product requirements. The later entrants’ opportunities included purchasable knowledge and transfers from established firms, but their advance also drew on prior experience and continuing engineering with customers. [SW-W008]

Testing latecomer advantage at the regional level#

Dou Lichen and Li Guoping’s 2004 analysis of technological innovation and diffusion examines latecomer advantage across 29 regions of China during 1991–2002. Their question is whether a region’s distance from the technological frontier supplies an opportunity for faster productivity growth. The empirical analysis uses labor productivity to represent technological levels, patents to represent innovation, investment per worker to represent absorptive conditions and foreign direct investment as a route for external technological diffusion. [SW-W009]

The authors investigate a nonlinear relationship between technological distance and the benefit obtainable from diffusion. A gap creates room for catching up, while the ability to receive and use incoming knowledge affects whether that room becomes productive growth. Their regional comparisons find stronger evidence of effective diffusion among more developed regions; the corresponding relationships are weaker or statistically unconvincing for less developed groups. Being further behind is therefore a different property from possessing an effective route for using knowledge developed elsewhere. [SW-W009]

This is an observational regional study, with proxy measures for innovation and absorption. It adds an empirical account of the conditions under which a latecomer position can become useful. The fuller organizational treatment of recognizing, assimilating and applying external knowledge appears in Absorbing new resources and capabilities. [SW-W009; AB-W001]

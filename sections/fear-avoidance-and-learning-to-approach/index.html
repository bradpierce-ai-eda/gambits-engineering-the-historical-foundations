What an avoidance procedure observes#

Krypotos and colleagues distinguish arrangements in which a designated response prevents an aversive event from arrangements in which withholding a response prevents it. The act can precede an event or terminate one already under way, depending on the experimental contingency. Unsignaled avoidance schedules give researchers a way to ask whether a warning cue is necessary for the behavior under study. The procedure specifies the available action, the timing of the aversive event and how the two depend on one another. [T03-W001]

Hofmann and Hay’s account asks what function avoidance serves in its setting. Avoiding an actual threat can protect the person. In other circumstances, a protective action can keep the person from finding out that the feared outcome would have failed to occur even without it. Their discussion brings both functions into clinical assessment. The costs of silence discussed in S51 arise in organizational settings where an adverse response can be a real possibility. [T03-W002]

What is learned during approach#

Craske and colleagues organize exposure around a prediction about a feared event. Their treatment illustrations describe eliciting the prediction, arranging the exposure, observing what occurs and revisiting the expectation. Reduction in distress within the exercise and a change in expectation are different observations. Their account gives varied contexts and retrieval cues a role because the person may need to draw on the learning in circumstances different from those of the treatment. The cases illustrate the procedure and its rationale. [T03-W003]

Shiban, Pauli and Mühlberger compared exposure in one virtual context with exposure in several contexts in a study of spider phobia. The subsequent renewal test asked whether the feared response returned in another context. The authors reported less renewal with the multiple-context arrangement under their test conditions. This result concerns transfer between specified experimental settings. Practical functioning in everyday settings requires its own observations. [T03-W005]

Magdalena Sich’s 2024 dissertation asked 62 patients receiving inpatient or day treatment to rate how willing they would be to undertake exposure in virtual reality or in person. They viewed example videos of the two formats and rated them before and after an experience with the virtual medium. They rated their willingness to undertake virtual exposure higher at both points. In the separate forced choice between formats, 40 initially preferred virtual reality and 38 did afterward. Eleven switched to in-person exposure and nine switched the other way. The study measured willingness and preference for the format. [T03-W004]

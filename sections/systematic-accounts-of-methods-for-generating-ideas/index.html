S63 Systematic accounts of methods for generating ideas #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

Accounts of creativity methods organize different kinds of knowledge. Some compare named techniques, some examine their instructions and some relate procedures to stages of innovation or conditions of use. A method may specify both an operation on ideas and an arrangement of participants, time and materials. The accounts below make those parts available for study and show several ways authors have classified them.

Kirjavainen and Hölttä-Otto and the parts of a method#

Senni Kirjavainen and Katja Hölttä-Otto analyse the instructions of eighty-eight documented idea-generation methods. They identify twenty-five mechanisms used as coding categories and distinguish implementation mechanisms from idea-promoting mechanisms. The first group concerns how an activity is organized; the second concerns sources of ideas and operations during generation. Several mechanisms can occur in one method, and the same mechanism can recur under different method names. [CRE-W001, §§3.3–4.2, tables 1–3]

Their examples begin with actual instructions. Asking participants to draw possible solutions specifies a visual modality. Providing a card with an action prompt can supply stimulation and invite modification of an existing idea. Asking for many possibilities specifies a quantity aim. These are separable features even when one exercise includes all of them. A method's name does not by itself reveal the combination. [CRE-W001, table 1]

The idea-promoting categories include analogy, association, building on other people's contributions, incubation and stimulation. They also include operations such as combining, modifying, framing, classifying, evaluating and selecting ideas. Analogy and association have different definitions in the coding: one concerns a pertinent relationship between domains, while the other can follow a word, image or idea without requiring that relationship. Suspending judgment appears as one component within this larger account. [CRE-W001, table 3]

The implementation categories distinguish individual and team participation, given or open duration, written and spoken expression, physical activity, visualization and thought. Facilitation and special tools are also recorded. A method designed for a team can contain individual phases. The arrangement of participation is therefore more specific than simply calling an exercise collective. [CRE-W001, table 2]

The authors refine their categories through coding and comparison. A second coder examines fifteen percent of the instructions, disagreements lead to revised guidance and the definitions are used in the remaining analysis. Their framework gives a way to inspect and compare the operations present in documented methods. It also makes the organization of a session visible as part of what a method specifies. [CRE-W001, §§3.3–4.2]

Geschka and Zirm and connected procedural families#

Horst Geschka and Andrea Zirm distinguish free association, structured association, configuration, confrontation and imagination. Their descriptions connect these families to ways of carrying out an exercise. In free association, an initial contribution becomes a source for another. Structured association introduces successive directions of thought. Configuration organizes combinations of features. Confrontation introduces material from outside the immediate problem, while imagination uses internally developed images and perspectives. [CRE-W002, §§4.1–4.5]

Their rotating-sheet procedure replaces a single stream of speech with parallel writing. A sheet has three columns, and each participant enters an idea in each column. After roughly five minutes, the sheet passes

to another participant, who develops ideas beneath the earlier entries. The page supplies both material to respond to and a position for recording a continuation. A contribution can be developed without first winning an opportunity to speak. [CRE-W002, §4.1]

In a circulating-card variant, participants put one idea on each card and pass cards in a common direction. During this phase they do not speak. Cards received from a neighbor can stimulate new ideas. The later phases group contributions by possible solution direction, remove duplicates, clarify understanding and make an initial selection through adhesive dots. The exercise includes both the circulation that generates material and a way to organize what has accumulated. [CRE-W002, §4.1, fig. 4-2]

Confrontation techniques use a different sequence. First the problem is clarified and participants briefly express the familiar solutions that occur to them. Only then is unrelated material introduced. A stimulus word is examined for its properties, structure and function; those features can be carried back to the problem. The intended departure is supported by an actual intervening object of attention. [CRE-W002, §4.4]

Visual confrontation can proceed through images, and one described variant combines them with written circulation. Participants inspect picture cards and record ideas before circulating their contributions. Other imagination exercises ask the participant to identify with a problem or picture it in another way. These procedures specify a route between an initial task, a change in attention and a return to possible solutions. [CRE-W002, §§4.4–4.5]

The chapter's families consequently contain different operations and different session arrangements. A confrontation technique may use a technical principle as stimulus; a written technique may circulate a visual or verbal contribution. The descriptions show how procedures can be combined while preserving the particular role of each step. S52 develops experimental accounts of group interaction and production blocking, and S68 develops other material and collaborative methods.

Takahashi and four purposes of creative techniques#

Makoto Takahashi's classification, presented by the Japan Creativity Society, distinguishes divergent, convergent, integrative and attitude methods. Divergent methods produce facts or ideas. Convergent methods bring that material together. Integrative methods are characterized by repeated movement between divergence and convergence. The classification therefore gives organizing material a place inside creative activity. [CRE-W004]

The fourth category concerns the creator's attitude. Such a method is intended to cultivate a disposition rather than directly solve the current problem. Its immediate purpose differs from that of a technique for producing a particular collection of alternatives. Readiness to participate and operations on a present problem can be addressed through different activities. [CRE-W004]

The distinction also changes how a procedure can be understood over time. An integrative method includes recurring transitions, while an attitude method can concern capacities brought to later work. Takahashi's short account provides a classification by those purposes. The particular methods described elsewhere give concrete forms to generating, organizing and developing material. [CRE-W004]

Yao and Chu and methods across an innovation process#

Yao Wei and Chu Zhaowei review systematic innovation methods as a subject wider than TRIZ. They distinguish methods drawing on human inventive experience from those abstracted from natural phenomena. They also follow the use of methods across identifying an opportunity, formulating a problem, developing a response and putting it into use. Their account treats a method's place in the process as part of its practical significance. [CRE-W003, §§1–2]

The integration examples assign different jobs to different methods. Quality function deployment can help articulate requirements; TRIZ can address a technical contradiction; other analyses can examine implementation or failure. Joining methods in this way concerns the output of one activity becoming useful to the next. The important question is what contribution each method makes within the connected process. [CRE-W003, discussion of integrated application]

Computer assistance enters that account through knowledge and retrieval. Case-based assistance can draw on earlier experience within a domain, while inventive principles seek connections across domains. The authors' proposed AI engineer is a prospective development of such assistance. Their review also separates learning about a method from using it effectively in an enterprise. Training, application and practical outcome are examined as different parts of its development and assessment. [CRE-W003, §§3–4]

Aznar and what an exercise is intended to accomplish#

Guy Aznar distinguishes imaginative expression, artistic creation, the production of ideas and innovation. His account of imaginative expression includes collective drawing, storytelling and imagined journeys within a permissive group. The immediate point is participation and the development of expression. A drawing need not become an exhibited artwork for the activity to serve that purpose. [CRE-W005, pp. 26– 28]

Producing ideas for a stated problem has another direction in his account. An idea must become both new and fitted to a situation. The work includes departures from an existing organization and a return toward a possible response. Aznar describes this process through tension and transformation, rather than identify it solely with a relaxed expressive state. [CRE-W005, pp. 28–30]

The distinction helps make the task of a session explicit. An expressive exercise and an inventive working session can share operations while pursuing different accomplishments. Aznar presents artistic creation as responding chiefly to an internal demand and idea production as responding to an external problem. His characterization is one historical account of those purposes. S66 develops collaborative artistic practices in which teachers, performers and shared material have substantial roles. [CRE-W005, pp. 27– 30]

Leopoldino and colleagues and a comparative inventory#

Kleidson Daniel Medeiros Leopoldino and colleagues map fifty-six publications and report 120 creativity techniques. Their detailed comparison treats nine frequently cited techniques. It distinguishes individual and collective application, the type of thinking, session time, operating criteria, material resources and the approach assigned to each technique. The table makes several different aspects of a technique available for comparison. [CRE-W006, §§3–4, tables 1–2]

Their operating criteria are exploration, combination, transformation and validation. The resulting descriptions compare such matters as whether the session is time-bounded, whether materials are needed and whether work is organized around several kinds of operation. These dimensions concern how techniques are described in the selected literature. The frequency with which a technique is cited is another part of the review, separate from the comparative description. [CRE-W006, §4]

The article gives a route into a wider literature and an inspectable comparison of its selected techniques. The reported inventory and the detailed table have different scopes: naming 120 techniques does not supply 120 complete procedures. Its useful contribution includes showing which features a comparative account can make explicit, alongside the instruction-level decomposition developed by Kirjavainen and Hölttä-Otto. [CRE-W006, §§4–5]

Acar and colleagues and different kinds of constraint#

Oguz Acar, Murat Tarakci and Daan van Knippenberg review empirical research on constraints in creativity and innovation. Their taxonomy distinguishes input, process and output constraints. Restricted time or materials limit inputs; a prescribed procedure governs the process; a specification defines what an outcome must contain or achieve. These restrictions act on different parts of the work. [CRE-W045, taxonomy of constraints]

The authors also distinguish motivational, cognitive and social routes through which constraints may affect activity. A restriction can alter willingness to experiment, the information attended to or relationships among participants. Classifying a constraint and explaining its effect are therefore different steps in their review. A time limit and a rule for exchanging ideas can both constrain a session while changing different conditions of action. [CRE-W045, underlying mechanisms]

The evidence discussed includes arguments that resources enable experimentation and findings in which pressure or restriction redirects effort. The framework relates these apparently different results to the object of a restriction and to proposed mediating processes. It also attends to the situation in which the restriction operates, rather than assign every constraint the same effect. [CRE-W045, input constraints]

These systematic accounts provide several ways to examine a method: its instructions, procedural family, immediate purpose, place in innovation and conditions of use. The developed practices in S64–S70 give those distinctions substantial cases. The classifications remain useful as guides to what a particular account explains.

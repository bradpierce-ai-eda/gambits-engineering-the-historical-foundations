Project schedules express dependencies, but they also allocate opportunities to work. A job can be technologically ready while the crew or equipment it needs is occupied elsewhere. A task can have room to move without delaying completion, yet moving it can change inventory, employment or contractual costs. The following accounts develop network calculations, constrained resources and the placement of protective time.

Planning the network and choosing a completion date#

Kelley and Walker's 1959 critical-path account distinguishes planning from scheduling. Planning defines the jobs and their technological order; scheduling supplies durations, costs and timing. Choosing what counts as a job is itself consequential. A delivery, an authorization or a safety requirement may need representation because subsequent work depends on it. The useful level of detail follows the purpose of the analysis, rather than a requirement to divide every project into equally sized tasks. [MG-W001]

The network permits forward and backward calculations. Earliest event times follow from predecessor completion; latest permissible times follow backward from the required completion. Their difference locates room for displacement. Critical work has no such room under the calculated schedule, so a delay there propagates to completion unless another change compensates for it. Float makes some timing choices available without changing that completion date. It is information about a schedule, rather than a reason to treat every noncritical task as unimportant. [MG-W001]

The authors also ask what completion time should be purchased. Expediting a job changes its duration and cost, while a project's completion can have costs beyond the jobs themselves. Their parametric linear-programming formulation develops a family of time/cost choices. The calculation therefore supports comparison among completion dates and combinations of expedited jobs, rather than treating the fastest technically attainable schedule as the only objective. [MG-W001]

Manpower and equipment enter the original account explicitly. A technologically feasible schedule can demand resources that are unavailable. Adding precedence relations to serialize jobs sharing a resource is one response, but the chosen order can exclude alternatives. With three jobs and one crew there are six possible orders; fixing one order hides the other five. The authors' practical resource-leveling discussion instead examines workforce demand and shifts eligible jobs within their float, including separate totals for different crafts. A precedence diagram and a workforce curve expose different constraints on the same work. [MG-W001]

Buffers, releases and operating priorities#

In Sood's account of critical-chain project management, resource contentions are resolved before the constraining sequence is identified. Protective time is organized into a project buffer and feeding buffers, while the release of projects is related to constrained-resource availability. This changes what a task estimate and a local delay mean operationally: attention is directed to protection of the project and its critical chain, rather than every activity carrying an independent allowance that managers try to preserve. [MG-W002]

Buffer management continues after the plan is drawn. Progress and consumption of protective time provide signals for priority decisions. A task's position in relation to the threatened buffer can matter more than its place in a static list of individual due dates. Work release and daily priorities thus form part of the arrangement; adding buffers to a schedule without changing those decisions would leave much of the reported method unexplained. [MG-W002]

Sood describes a construction implementation at Shea Homes in which a shared portal supplied subcontractors with priorities for the following day. The portal joined the scheduling model to the people deciding what work to undertake. His reported cases illustrate that operational connection. They are implementation accounts supplied by a vendor executive, and the useful historical detail lies in how release, protection and coordination were organized. [MG-W002]

Where protective time is placed#

諏訪晴彦, 森田大輔 and 三道弘明 distinguish the total amount of slack from its distribution. Their 2009 model begins with a resource-feasible baseline schedule, with fixed estimated processing times and constant resource availability. It then inserts dummy activities whose durations represent protective time. The sum of these durations is the chosen allowance K; allocating that sum is a further decision. [MG-W003]

The construction places a dummy before the first critical activity and after critical activities, including a terminal dummy. Critical work and affected successors are shifted to the right to accommodate these insertions. Later critical start times incorporate preceding dummy durations; ordinary activities start as early as their technological and resource predecessors permit in the changed schedule. Distinguishing the terminal dummy also separates the last real job's completion from the project's buffered due date. [MG-W003]

The objective concerns displacement from announced start times. Starting earlier can create inventory or other preparation costs; starting later can extend employment or disturb subcontracting arrangements. The model assigns early and late deviations their own weights. Buffer placement is judged through expected weighted displacement costs, so two schedules with the same K can have different consequences. The authors consider allocation rules based on position and successor relationships as well as uniform allocation. [MG-W003]

This formulation makes a concrete choice visible: how much protection to provide and where to provide it are separate scheduling decisions. A buffer's position changes which jobs move and which relationships absorb variation. The operating signals in Sood's account and the displacement-cost model address different parts of managing protective time. Work dependencies and coordination mechanisms receive broader treatment in S14.

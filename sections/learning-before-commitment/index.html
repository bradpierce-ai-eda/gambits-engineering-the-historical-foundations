S62 Learning before commitment #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

An organization can begin learning before it decides exactly what to build or whether to undertake a large investment. It can test a technical principle, investigate customers, develop several compatible designs or build the ability to use a technology whose final form is still changing. These activities alter what a later decision can be based on. Their histories concern the organization of investigation as well as the timing of commitment.

The accounts below develop sequential investment, discovery-driven planning, set-based engineering and software-investment management. Information acquisition, experiments and dual control treats actions that serve both operational and informational purposes. Generating alternatives and organizing exploration examines the production and evaluation of alternatives. Here the emphasis is on what is learned before a particular choice is closed, how the knowledge is retained and how subsequent decisions use it.

Information produced by early expenditure#

In his 1991 discussion of irreversible investment, Robert Pindyck distinguishes waiting for information from spending to obtain it. His exposition of Kevin Roberts and Martin Weitzman’s work on research, development and exploration considers projects that reveal more about their value as successive stages are undertaken. The investor can stop after an unfavorable discovery instead of completing the entire sequence. An initial expenditure can therefore be worthwhile even when a calculation that assumes unconditional completion gives the project a negative net present value. [SW-W004]

The informative activity changes the alternatives available at the next decision. A drug-development program, an aircraft prototype or a demonstration plant can disclose something that the mere passage of time would not reveal. In the sequential-learning formulation Pindyck discusses, the project’s underlying value is fixed but unknown. Learning requires investment. Deferring every stage also defers the acquisition of the information that could justify, redirect or end the project. [SW-W004]

Pindyck also describes a market study costing $50 in a version of his widget example in which the factory opportunity is available only in the current year and costs $1,200. The study reveals whether the future selling price will be high or low, allowing the investor to condition construction on the result. Its value lies in changing the construction decision. This gives the early expenditure a different purpose from buying a smaller amount of production capacity: it purchases a better-informed choice about the larger outlay. [SW-W004]

Planning around assumptions that can be tested#

Rita Gunther McGrath and Ian MacMillan’s 1995 Discovery-Driven Planning addresses ventures whose plans contain many assumptions that cannot yet be treated as facts. Their method connects four working documents: a reverse income statement, a specification of the operations needed to deliver the business, a list of important assumptions and a milestone plan. Together these identify what would have to be true for the venture to justify its investment and when those propositions can be tested. [LC-W002]

The reverse income statement starts with the profit needed to make the venture worthwhile and works backward to required revenue and allowable cost. The operations specification then asks what activities, capacity and resources would be needed to produce that result. Putting those two descriptions beside one another exposes assumptions about prices, market size, production, distribution and the time needed to reach scale. The assumption list makes the uncertain parts explicit so that a detailed forecast does not quietly turn them into established knowledge. [LC-W002]

The milestone plan orders expenditures around opportunities to learn. A milestone is useful when reaching it tests an assumption that matters to continuation. McGrath and MacMillan use Kao’s entry into floppy disks as a retrospective worked illustration, assembled from public information. They show how prototype production, technical testing with prospective buyers, subcontracted production and sales, a pilot plant and full-scale production could form a sequence. Each stage addresses a different question about performance, demand or the production economics required by the intended business. [LC-W002]

The worked plan does not require all of these questions to be settled before any activity begins. It gives each uncertain proposition a place in the sequence before the commitments that depend on it. Evidence from a technical test can alter an operations estimate; a sales result can change the volume assumed in the reverse income statement. The documents are meant to be revised together, so that a newly learned fact changes the plan rather than remaining an isolated observation. [LC-W002]

McGrath and MacMillan assign responsibility for maintaining the assumptions and updating the linked documents. This makes the procedure more than a list of risks written at the start. A changing account of what is known governs the next commitment. [LC-W002]

Keeping compatible designs open#

Allen Ward, Jeffrey Liker, John Cristiano and Durward Sobek’s 1995 account of Toyota describes an engineering process in which a broader early exploration can support a faster later convergence. A point-based process selects a particular design and then revises it as problems appear. Because subsystem decisions interact, revising one part can invalidate another team’s work. The alternative they call set-based concurrent engineering maintains sets of possibilities, communicates the ranges other teams can work within and narrows those ranges as knowledge improves. [LC-W003]

The authors compare coordination to finding a meeting time. Asking each person for a single preferred time can lead to repeated proposals and rejections. Asking for each person’s available times reveals an intersection that can satisfy everyone. In engineering, the corresponding sets describe acceptable dimensions, performance, manufacturing conditions and other requirements. The task is to find combinations that remain compatible across the system while investigating the choices that have not yet been settled. [LC-W003]

Their account links five activities: describing possibilities at both system and subsystem levels; investigating several possibilities in parallel; narrowing them as knowledge accumulates; deriving feasible specifications from that knowledge; and preserving the commitments once the selected set has been settled. Parallel work is supported by information about allowable variation. A team does not need to know one final design to establish that an entire family of proposed shapes would be difficult to manufacture. [LC-W003]

The Nippondenso example concerns advanced component development that begins before a particular Toyota vehicle program. Engineers explore ideas and partial prototypes, combine promising features and progressively reduce the candidates. The reported sequence reaches three designs and five prototypes in its fourth year and a chosen design in the fifth year of an eight-year cycle. Compatible component families and interchangeable parts allow the development to serve several vehicles and customers. The early work creates knowledge that a later vehicle program can draw on. [LC-W003]

Toyota’s lessons-learned books give that knowledge a form that can outlast the project. They describe manufacturing limits and relationships between design choices and outcomes, rather than merely recording the sequence of changes that produced the last vehicle. A body designer can use them to recognize troublesome shapes before asking manufacturing engineers to evaluate a completed design. What is retained is a map of workable possibilities, available for a future design that may occupy a different part of that map. [LC-W003]

Ward and colleagues also explain how progressively narrower communications can make independent work more reliable. A later message refines an earlier range instead of replacing one promised point with an incompatible point. Suppliers know how much variation remains and can judge how far to advance their own work. The authors develop these ideas from observed practices and present the relationship to Toyota’s development performance as a hypothesis. [LC-W003]

Moving investigation ahead of design completion#

Jeffrey Liker and James Morgan’s 2006 account of lean product development explains the timing of Toyota’s early investigation in greater detail. In the body-development comparison they describe, engineering at other manufacturers often begins in earnest after the stylists’ clay model is frozen. At Toyota, substantial investigation of the product and its manufacturing process begins during an earlier study period. Cross-functional teams produce study drawings and examine alternative solutions while the shape and detailed design still have room to change. [LC-W006]

The intended result is system compatibility before individual designs are completed. Structural and manufacturing questions can influence the developing product while alternatives remain available, instead of arriving as demands to revise finished work. The authors connect this front-loading to a more stable execution phase. Their account places delayed selection within an active engineering process: investigation starts earlier even though some final decisions are taken later. [LC-W006]

A cheap test before expensive redesign#

Brian Kennedy, Durward Sobek and Michael Kennedy’s study, first published online in 2013, distinguishes purposeful experimental iterations from rework caused by premature decisions. In their Teledyne TapTone case, engineers used carpenter’s clamps costing $14 on an existing machine to vary rigidity and investigate its effect. A few hours of testing helped establish a relationship between structural stiffness and performance before detailed redesign. The resulting knowledge supported a smaller, less rigid and less costly design than an untested assumption would have suggested. [LC-W011]

The case also describes customer input on cleanable rounded stainless-steel legs and metric components before drawings and tooling fixed the design. The authors advocate expressing ranges and tradeoffs that teams can use to coordinate decisions. The clamp experiment is a concrete instance of learning a design relationship with inexpensive equipment; it did not require first producing a complete candidate machine. [LC-W011]

Representing preferences and feasible ranges#

Haruo Ishikawa and Naoko Sasaki’s 2018 treatment gives set-based design a computational form for simultaneous structural and control design. They represent both required performance and design variables as ranges with preferences within those ranges. A preference value indicates which portions are more desirable, from acceptable boundary regions to the most desired region. The method propagates the design-variable ranges into predicted performance ranges and compares them with the requirements. [LC-W007]

When a predicted range includes unacceptable performance, the method narrows the design-variable ranges. It evaluates candidate subranges using both desirability and robustness, then seeks common ranges that satisfy the multiple performance requirements together. In this formulation, choosing acceptable ranges and expressing preferences are distinct operations. Designers can retain a region of acceptable choices while still indicating that some parts of it are more attractive. [LC-W007]

The worked example is the controlled rotation of a stepped rectangular arm containing holes. Its six design variables include structural dimensions, the number of holes and three controller gains. Four performance requirements concern rise time, overshoot, settling time and mass. The authors compare two preference patterns over the same initial variable ranges: an equal preference throughout each range, and a triangular pattern favoring the midpoint. [LC-W007]

The different preferences produce different retained ranges while meeting the specified performance targets in the example. The initial arm-width range of 0.12–0.18 metres narrows to 0.135–0.150 metres in the first case and 0.150–0.165 metres in the second. The selected ranges of controller gains also differ. Thus the result includes a family of structural and control choices shaped by both the performance limits and the designer’s stated preferences, rather than a single supposedly inevitable optimum. [LC-W007]

Investing in a technological position#

Rita Gunther McGrath’s 1997 paper distinguishes expenditure to establish a technological position from the later decision to commercialize it. An early research investment can build knowledge and a right or practical ability to pursue a business if conditions become favorable. Commercialization is a further exercise of that opportunity. The organization may instead license the result, redirect it or stop. The initial investment and the final business are therefore different decisions with different information available at their respective times. [LC-W004]

McGrath separates uncertainty that the investor’s work can reduce from uncertainty that evolves outside the project. A technical question about whether a design can function may require experiments. An input price or a regulatory decision may become clearer through other events. Some external conditions may also be influenced by strategic action. These distinctions affect whether an organization gains more by waiting, investigating or taking action that changes the conditions under which it might later commercialize. [LC-W004]

Her pollution-control illustration separates the smaller investment needed to establish a technological position from the much larger outlay required for commercialization. The attraction of the later business depends partly on regulation. Initial work can create a position from which the firm can respond if the market develops, without paying the entire commercialization cost at once. Sequential milestones allow the project to absorb information and change direction as this process unfolds. [LC-W004]

The paper develops a real-options logic for these decisions rather than treating the research investment as a miniature version of the eventual business. The relevant opportunity can be created or improved by the investor’s own activity. Its value depends on what that activity makes possible and on the decisions that remain available afterward. [LC-W004]

Connecting tests to decisions that can still change#

Ian MacMillan, Alexander van Putten, Rita McGrath and James Thompson’s 2006 account of real-options discipline connects discovery-driven planning to the management of uncertain technology investments. Their argument requires actual freedom to make a later decision. Dividing an already unavoidable expenditure into accounting stages does not create the same opportunity as funding an investigation whose result can lead to continuation, alteration or abandonment. [LC-W005]

The procedure uses the proposed business, its operating requirements and its assumptions to identify what must be learned before further resources are committed. Its BioBarrier illustration concerns a proposed hospital hand-washing product. Assumptions about the market, production capacity and selling costs influence whether the business could meet its financial requirements. Simulation is used to examine

uncertain outcomes and identify assumptions that deserve attention; milestone tests then address the assumptions that materially affect the next decision. [LC-W005]

The illustrative business makes the connection between analysis and action inspectable. A simulation does not itself improve the product or reveal customer behavior. It helps identify which unknowns matter to the choice of another expenditure. The managerial procedure then links an investigation to the decision it can inform. This extends the earlier discovery-driven account by making the freedom to stop or change the investment part of its real-options interpretation. [LC-W005]

Exploration before a customer has specified the product#

Sylvain Lenfle and Christophe Midler’s 2002 study at Usinor concerns an upstream supplier developing an innovative offer before a customer has defined a particular product. Their hydroforming case involves shaping metal tubes through pressure and deformation, with possible uses across several markets. The project must discover both what the process can accomplish and which applications are worth pursuing. A conventional development plan with one agreed object and a delivery date does not yet describe the work. [LC-W009]

The authors organize the effort as a portfolio of studies and experiments. Research on forming possibilities, process capability and crash behavior can interact with particular customer applications. Bringing these investigations into a common project makes their combined significance visible and can justify shared experimental equipment. A customer study can redirect technical research, while technical knowledge can reveal an application that was not apparent at the start. [LC-W009]

They distinguish two kinds of achievement within this exploration. A study may produce a sale with little new technical knowledge, or it may fail to produce immediate revenue while supplying knowledge important to several later applications. Their proposed evaluation therefore follows both commercial results and the growth of knowledge across the portfolio. An unsuccessful application can still establish a useful boundary of the process; a successful part can serve as evidence about a broader field of possible uses. [LC-W009]

Tests play a central coordinating role because no finished product yet provides an agreed schedule. Experiments, simulations and presentations to customers challenge the project’s starting assumptions and can redirect the exploration. The research program is a provisional organization of what to investigate. It changes as the team learns, rather than requiring a complete specification of the discovery before work begins. [LC-W009]

Lenfle and Midler also describe a masked urgency in this upstream work. A new material or process must be validated in time to enter a customer’s own design sequence. A vehicle may seem six or seven years from the market, while the practical choice to include or exclude the innovation comes within two or three years. Learning has to begin early enough to reach that choice. The late market launch does not mean that the opportunity to influence the design remains open until then. [LC-W009]

Making flexibility exercisable in software investment#

Philip Weihs’s 2008 Steuerung von Flexibilität develops a framework for managing software investments with real options. Its selected project-management and case-study chapters follow investment beyond the initial appraisal. The framework separates initialization from use, includes a continuing search for new options and assigns roles to the investment decision maker, an adviser on real options and the project manager. The decision maker owns the relevant choices and commissions the work; the project manager implements the projects through which the investment is realized. [LC-W010]

The distinction matters when the investment is larger than any single delivery project. A first project may establish a platform or capability from which later projects can be undertaken. Those later choices require identification and management during use, not merely a statement of their possible value at the original approval. Weihs permits ranges or qualitative assessments when a numerical option value cannot be established, with further valuation as the parameters become clearer. [LC-W010]

One retrospective case concerns a configuration-management application intended to organize software, plans and documentation, support version control and enable reuse. The project began in 2004, stopped late that year, resumed with changed requirements in 2005 and was then put into service. Weihs’s analysis found that the needs actually extended across configuration management, project management and customer relationship management. Treating all of them as additions to one project had obscured what the first investment should contain. [LC-W010]

The thesis proposes two alternative interpretations of that history. One would define the larger investment in business-process support, implement a clearly bounded initial application and retain the others as possible subsequent expansions. The other would explicitly recognize a right to interrupt or abandon the narrower project as its requirements became clearer. These are retrospective redesigns of the investment decision; the case does not measure savings from a framework implemented at its start. [LC-W010]

The comparison across the thesis’s cases connects options to practical authority. A project consciously organized in small increments offered more scope for adjustment than projects with fixed long-term plans. Decisions requiring approval through several levels, including a senior body meeting only twice a year, could not be exercised promptly when conditions changed. The ability to choose later consequently depended on who could decide and when, as well as on the technical form of the software. [LC-W010]

A contemporary model of enterprise AI pilots#

Gaurav Tewari’s 2026 preprint Pilot Early, Commit Late formulates an enterprise AI adoption problem with three choices: deploy immediately, wait or conduct a pilot before deciding whether to deploy. The two-date model separates the external technological frontier from the organization’s ability to use it. Deployment produces current operating value and learning by doing. A pilot costs money and produces no full operating return in the first period, but increases capability available for the later decision. Waiting preserves the later choice without creating that internal capability in the model. [LC-W008]

The installed system can capture a specified fraction of subsequent technological improvement. That fraction represents the economic effects of modularity, portability and the ability to upgrade without another sunk implementation cost. Immediate deployment can therefore remain attractive when it captures future progress well, produces substantial current returns or teaches the organization a great deal through production use. The model makes these advantages comparable with the flexibility of choosing a later technology. [LC-W008]

Piloting is preferable to pure waiting when its cost is no greater than the expected increase it creates in the value of the later deployment choice. The increase can come from making adoption profitable in more future states or making profitable adoption more valuable. In this formulation a demonstration with little transferable organizational learning contributes little to that option, however polished the demonstration may be. [LC-W008]

The analysis also derives conditions under which faster expected technological progress makes immediate deployment less attractive relative to waiting or piloting. A low ability to capture future improvements favors that result; strong production learning or a large present operating benefit favors earlier deployment. These are comparisons within a theoretical model that omits strategic competition and financing constraints. [LC-W008]

S89 Constructing portfolios through relationships among investments #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

A portfolio is more than a list of investments. Holdings can move together, duplicate an exposure or supply cash at times when liabilities must be met. The following constructions make relationships among investments part of the decision: covariance in portfolio selection, factor exposure in risk allocation and timing in asset–liability immunization.

Expected returns and joint variability#

Markowitz's 1952 account begins by separating maximizing expected return from choosing a combination of investments. Expected return alone can favor concentration in the holding with the greatest expected return. Diversification requires another consideration. His mean–variance approach represents the portfolio through both its expected return and the variability produced by the holdings together. [MG-W014]

If x_i is the share assigned to asset i and μ_i its expected return, portfolio expected return is the sum of x_i μ_i. Portfolio variance contains terms x_i x_j σ_ij, where σ_ij is the covariance of returns on assets i and j. The cross terms express how holdings vary together. Adding names therefore does not automatically make risk disappear: correlated movements remain in the combination even when the number of holdings grows. [MG-W014]

With nonnegative weights summing to one and specified probability beliefs, feasible combinations occupy an attainable region of expected return and variance. An efficient combination has no attainable competitor offering a higher expected return without greater variance, or lower variance without less expected return. The construction separates identifying these combinations from the investor's choice among them. It also separates the calculation from obtaining credible expectations and covariances, which the paper leaves as further work. [MG-W014]

When another asset repeats the same exposure#

Roncalli and Weisang's 2012 working paper examines the distinction between risk contributions attached to named assets and contributions attached to underlying factors. Their motivating example starts with four assets and introduces a fifth that duplicates one of them. The economic exposure available has not become more diverse merely because the same investment appears under another label. Yet allocating equal risk contributions across the asset list can change the resulting allocation. [MG-W020]

The example tests what the allocation rule treats as its units. Dividing risk equally among five asset labels is a different instruction from dividing it among four underlying exposures. The authors turn toward factor risk contributions to address this dependence on the nominated universe. Their motivating argument gives a reason to inspect exposure beneath asset counts; the particular factors and their representation remain part of specifying the allocation problem. [MG-W020]

Matching assets to liabilities#

Redington's 1952 treatment of life-office valuation asks how assets relate to the payments the institution must make. Immunization concerns the effect of a general interest-rate change on that relationship. The starting calculation values asset proceeds and liability outgo consistently. Equal present values establish a balance at the chosen rate; matching first-order sensitivity makes a small change in that rate have no first-order effect on their difference. [MG-W015]

The second-order condition then matters. Positive second-order behavior of the asset-minus-liability value supplies local protection around the valuation rate. The timing of interest receipts enters the

calculation alongside repayment of principal, so matching asset maturity dates alone does not reproduce the construction. Redington distinguishes the term of an investment from the mean discounted time of all the proceeds it supplies. [MG-W015]

His numerical illustration considers a unit liability payable in ten years at a valuation rate of 2.5%. A single coupon-bearing security can need a maturity of 11.485 years to give the asset proceeds the appropriate mean term. Earlier interest receipts shorten that mean relative to the final maturity. The example shows why a longer-maturing security can perform a matching role that its maturity date, viewed alone, would conceal. [MG-W015]

This is a local construction for a small uniform rate change under the stated valuation setting. Exact matching of cash flows is a different construction. The relationship also changes as time passes, rates change or new business enters, requiring reconsideration of the asset arrangement. Redington's objective is meeting obligations; Markowitz's efficient combinations use expected return and variance. Their different criteria explain why a portfolio can be well constructed for one purpose without settling another. [MG-W015]

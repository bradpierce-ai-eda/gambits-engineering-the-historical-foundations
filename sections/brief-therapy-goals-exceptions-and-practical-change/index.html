When attempted solutions sustain a problem#

In the Palo Alto model of brief therapy, an effort to remove a difficulty can become part of what keeps it going. Michael Rohrbaugh and Varda Shoham's 2001 account calls attention to the interaction between a complaint and the attempted solutions surrounding it. A partner presses for discussion because the other withdraws; the other withdraws further under pressure; the first responds by pressing harder. The complaint need not be imaginary, and neither participant need intend the result. The repeated response to it supplies a pattern that the therapist can try to interrupt. [TD-W001]

The therapist first asks what actually happens, what the participants have tried, and what follows those attempts. A complaint such as poor communication becomes a sequence of observable actions. The intervention is then fitted to that sequence: reducing pursuit, changing the circumstances of a conversation, or asking someone to observe a pattern instead of immediately trying to correct it. The next encounter examines whether the response changed. The point of reconstructing the sequence is to locate an intervention, rather than to require a complete explanation of the person's history before anything useful can be attempted. [TD-W001]

Rohrbaugh and Shoham describe other ironic processes, including reassurance that invites further requests for reassurance and efforts to force sleep that increase wakefulness. Their account distinguishes a complaint's origins from the transactions maintaining it. It also makes the therapist's manner of proposing a change consequential. An instruction that encounters opposition may need to acknowledge the client's position or take an indirect form. The same complaint label can conceal different interaction patterns, so the proposed interruption depends on the attempted solution and the response it evokes. [TD-W001]

Brevity in this tradition did not mean that an arbitrary smaller allowance was always better. The review reports a small experiment comparing limits of five and ten sessions, with thirteen and fourteen cases respectively. Although the clinic's treatment averaged about six sessions, the five-session condition fared worse. An average describes what happened across cases; a cap changes the conditions under which each case can proceed. The authors' discussion of this experiment is particularly useful for understanding why a brief approach can seek economy without treating the shortest permitted treatment as its objective. [TD-W001]

Developing solutions from exceptions#

Steve de Shazer and his colleagues' 1986 paper described a further change of emphasis at the Brief Family Therapy Center. Instead of organizing every intervention around the attempted solution that maintained a complaint, they increasingly developed solutions from clients' goals and from exceptions to the complaint. Their distinction between an explanatory match and a practical fit was deliberate. A useful intervention might fit what the client needed to accomplish without reproducing a detailed model of the problem. The therapist therefore sought descriptions of what would be different, what was already happening on better occasions, and what the client could recognize as progress. [TD-W002]

One family was asked to observe, between visits, what each person did that the others wanted to continue. This assignment changed what the family was looking for. At the next meeting, particular acts worth preserving could be described and discussed, rather than only another inventory of failures. The therapists then asked how the family had helped those events occur and how they might encourage more of them. In the authors' account, the family invented its eventual solution between the first and second sessions; the later work helped it sustain and extend the useful changes. Observation was connected to

action through the selection of concrete events and the participants' account of their own contribution. [TD-W002]

Another task introduced a contrast into parents' efforts to get homework done. Coin-toss days determined when they would refrain from mentioning it, giving them occasions to notice what happened without their usual prompting. If the child did the homework on those days, the next conversation could examine a successful difference already present in the family's experience. The instruction was more specific than a general demand to be positive: alter a recurring response, observe the result, and use the result to decide what to continue. [TD-W002]

The first-session format connected the complaint, exceptions, goals and possible solutions. Concrete questions established who did what, when the difficulty occurred, and how anyone would know that it had improved. After a consultation break, the team delivered a message combining acknowledgments of the client's resources with a task or clue directed toward useful change. Even an initially vague complaint could lead to an observable goal. Asking what a close friend would notice when the person felt more certain of who they were made a broad aspiration answerable in terms of everyday conduct. [TD-W002]

At a subsequent session, the therapists elicited what was better, distinguished new changes from previously overlooked exceptions, and asked how the client had brought them about. A useful difference became material for the next action. When nothing had improved, the search shifted toward what was preventing deterioration or what small difference might still be amplified. When things were worse, the authors described questions about the course of that worsening and signs of an approaching improvement. These were the center's reported conversational practices, rather than results establishing the effectiveness of each question separately. [TD-W002]

Continuation also depended on the developing case. The center usually placed the second session a week after the first, then lengthened intervals when the client reported improvement: two weeks, followed by three weeks to a month. When the team thought the change might be sufficient, it asked the client whether another meeting was needed and when. The center reported about 1,600 cases from 1978 to 1983, averaging six sessions. Follow-up was based on self-report, and only twenty-eight of fifty-six clients in a separate first-task project were reached. Those service observations accompanied the description of an evolving practice; they were not a randomized demonstration that six sessions constituted a minimum effective dose. [TD-W002]

How the conversation is built#

The Solution Focused Brief Therapy Association's 2025 treatment manual develops the conversation itself as a sequence of listening, selecting and building. The therapist listens for the client's descriptions, selects an expression that can be developed toward the preferred future, and asks a question that builds on it. The client's words provide more than rapport. They supply the distinctions through which a change will be recognized and make the next question responsive to this person's situation. A preferred future becomes increasingly specific as the therapist asks who would notice, what they would see, and what would happen next. [TD-W003]

In the manual's mother–daughter example, a successful conversation about a science project supplies a concrete instance of better communication. The useful detail is that the mother listened without simultaneously doing something else. Following that detail reveals an interaction the participants can recognize and potentially repeat. The therapist can ask how it happened, what the daughter noticed, and what the mother did that made a difference. The exception thus supplies actions and circumstances for a goal that would otherwise remain an abstract wish to communicate better. [TD-W003]

Scaling questions develop another kind of specificity. A rating locates the client's current position relative to a desired state, after which the therapist asks what makes that position possible and what a small

further improvement would look like. The work lies in the descriptions attached to the numbers. Asking why the current rating is not lower can elicit resources already in use; asking what the next increment would reveal can identify an attainable difference. The scale is a conversational device for constructing and noticing change, rather than a substitute for every outcome measure. [TD-W003]

The manual's EARS sequence links eliciting a useful change, amplifying its details, reinforcing the client's contribution, and starting the process again. If the person reports no progress, coping questions can explore how they have managed to continue under difficult conditions. A report of worsening calls first for acknowledgment, followed by questions fitted to what has happened. The decision about another meeting remains joint. This later manual gives a developed account of the approach in 2025, including how its procedures adapt during an encounter, without imposing a fixed session count. [TD-W003]

What outcome comparisons measure#

Wallace Gingerich and Lance Peterson's 2013 systematic qualitative review included forty-three controlled outcome studies. Thirty-two reported statistically significant favorable outcomes, ten reported favorable trends, and one reported no improvement. Their inclusion procedure accepted studies using at least one of ten identified solution-focused techniques. The resulting body of evidence therefore encompassed varied interventions, settings and comparators. A comparison with no treatment or usual services answered a different question from a comparison with another active psychotherapy. [TD-W004]

Only three studies in that review directly compared treatment duration. One comparison involved experienced therapists and trainees, making experience relevant to the interpretation of the difference. The review's broad conclusion that the approach was promising and often brief did not arise from forty-three independent dose comparisons. Nor does a nonsignificant difference between therapies establish equivalence in the statistical sense. Reading the review by comparator and outcome makes it possible to distinguish evidence of beneficial change from evidence about the contacts required to obtain it. [TD-W004]

Contacts, elapsed time and lasting improvement#

The Helsinki Psychotherapy Study gives these distinctions concrete form. Paul Knekt and colleagues randomized 326 patients with mood or anxiety disorders among solution-focused therapy, short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy and long-term psychodynamic psychotherapy. The first report appeared in 2007, with the journal issue following in 2008. The solution-focused arm allowed up to twelve sessions over eight months; the short psychodynamic treatment provided twenty weekly sessions over about five to six months; the long treatment met two or three times weekly for approximately three years. These were three different treatment schedules, rather than equal-duration packages with interchangeable labels. [TD-W005]

Among those who started the assigned therapies, mean assigned contacts were 9.8, 18.5 and 232 respectively. Their mean elapsed treatment periods were 7.5, 5.7 and 31.3 months. The solution-focused treatment used fewer contacts than the short psychodynamic treatment while extending over a longer calendar interval. When auxiliary psychotherapy was included, mean total contacts rose to 29.9, 46.9 and 235. Counting only scheduled visits would have missed some of the treatment received during follow-up. [TD-W005]

The short treatments produced improvement earlier, while the long psychodynamic treatment achieved greater symptom improvement by the three-year assessment. No statistically significant difference was found between the two short therapies. The history is consequently intelligible at several scales: the pace of early relief, the number of meetings, the period over which they occurred, additional care, and

symptoms years later. Choosing a single one of these quantities as the meaning of “faster” would discard a substantial part of what this study measured. [TD-W005]

A brief intervention in primary care#

Cooper and colleagues' 2024 primary-care trial tested three weekly solution-focused sessions lasting twenty to thirty minutes, alongside usual care. The comparison group received usual care and problem-focused assessment contacts matched in frequency and duration. The intervention was consequently tested against more than an absence of professional attention. It was not a comparison with cognitive behavioral therapy. The brief schedule gave the researchers a defined intervention to examine, with goals and exceptions developed into actions and progress reviewed across the three weeks. [TD-W006]

Eighty people were randomized. The body of the report gives sixty-nine baseline participants and sixty-eight completing the intervention period. Mean depression scores on the PHQ-9 fell from 18.17 to 9.71 in the solution-focused group, compared with 17.76 to 18.94 in the comparison group; anxiety and well-being measures also favored the intervention. The assessment horizon was three weeks. The trial supports improvement under this short primary-care arrangement, while its design does not identify the fewest sessions that would have worked or establish how long the improvement persisted. [TD-W006]

Constructing alternatives to harmful coping#

邹晓伟 and colleagues' 2021 analysis of solution-focused work with nonsuicidal self-injury begins with the function of a person's coping behavior. An attempt to regulate distress may pursue a comprehensible aim through a harmful means. The proposed therapeutic work recognizes the aim, explores occasions when the person coped differently, and develops safer actions that can serve a similar purpose. Preferred-future and scaling questions make the alternative specific: what would be happening at a slightly better point, and what has already helped the person reach the present one? [TD-W007]

The authors connect conversation with practical aids such as a written repertoire of coping actions, reminders and crisis cards, together with rehearsal of alternatives. The repertoire must be available when the situation arises, rather than remaining an insight confined to the interview. This is a conceptual clinical account drawing on a limited research base, including some combined interventions. Its contribution here is the connected proposal: identify what the action is doing for the person, find an existing exception, construct an accessible substitute, and prepare to use it. [TD-W007]

From a teacher's case knowledge to a workable response#

黒沢幸子 and colleagues' 2015 eleven-step consultation model applies solution-focused work to teachers seeking help with cases. The consultant first establishes the setting and history, then explores resources in the case, exceptions, earlier helpful and unhelpful actions, and the teacher's own capacities. The resource steps allow flexibility in order. The conversation then revisits the teacher's need, specifies a small positive goal, considers helpful actions, offers affirmative feedback, and brings in the consultant's professional proposal. The teacher's knowledge of the classroom and the consultant's expertise are both used. [TD-W008]

Closure includes an explicit test of the proposed response: does the teacher judge it feasible and useful? If either answer is unfavorable, the proposal needs alteration. This prevents the consultation from ending merely because the expert has supplied an intelligible recommendation. What the teacher can carry out in the actual setting becomes part of the construction of that recommendation. The eleven steps thus connect description, resource discovery, a revised goal and a workable next action. [TD-W008]

Twenty-seven teachers brought two cases each to the study, with the order of solution-focused and problem-exploration consultations randomized. Fifty-one consultations were usable for analysis, twenty-six solution-focused and twenty-five problem-exploration, all conducted by one consultant. Immediate ratings favored the solution-focused format on such dimensions as usefulness, reduced difficulty and action planning. These were evaluations of the consultation and the teacher's readiness to act, rather than measurements of children's subsequent recovery. [TD-W008]

The shape of the evidence#

Michele Orr, Sharlette Ryder and Julie Considine's 2026 scoping review of adult community mental-health services illustrates why the shape of a literature matters alongside its size. Twenty included papers did not represent twenty independent trials: fourteen arose from the Helsinki study. The review maps a range of applications and reports uneven specification of the intervention across studies. It directs attention to which populations and comparisons have actually been examined. Treatment benefit, efficient use of contacts, and a procedure that reliably chooses among modalities for a particular person remain distinct empirical questions. The earlier accounts of feedback and practice in S71 and S72 provide neighboring histories of change through activity and review; the therapeutic studies above retain their own interventions and outcomes. [TD-W009]

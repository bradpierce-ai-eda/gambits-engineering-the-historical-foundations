S92 Being strategic at work #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

Being strategic at work can concern the course someone pursues, the understanding brought to a situation, the attention obtained for a problem or the opportunity to participate in deciding what happens. These questions arise in implementing an established direction as well as proposing a different one. The accounts below examine the work through which direction is formed, interpreted and pursued, and the organizational conditions under which a contribution becomes possible or recognized.

Intention and a course of action#

Henry Mintzberg and James Waters distinguish intended strategies from patterns realized through action. Their 1985 account places deliberate and emergent strategies along a continuum. An umbrella strategy sets boundaries while allowing others to determine details. Leaders can specify the kinds of activity they want without possessing the information or control needed to settle every action. A process strategy instead influences who makes decisions and the organizational setting in which they work. [ST-W001, pp. 263–265]

Their National Film Board example concerns Norman McLaren's sustained production of experimental films. His individual course could appear emergent from the organization's perspective. The authors question whether he needed an advance intention to continue for decades: a sequence of worthwhile projects could develop into such a course. Their architectural-firm example similarly distinguishes the partners' broad preferences from the performing-arts commissions clients supplied. Both examples make the actor and level of intention consequential to the interpretation of a strategic pattern. [ST-W001, pp. 265–269]

Carrying a direction through everyday work#

Linda Rouleau and Julia Balogun's 2008 working paper examines practical knowledge in middle managers' strategic sensemaking. At Radio-Canada, Mary entered a technical directorate without its established technical credentials. She acknowledged that she was there to organize the work, listened to technicians' difficulties and related the proposed changes to problems they experienced. The account gives conversation a specific setting: what could be said credibly depended on knowledge of the people and their work. [ST-W003, pp. 15–18]

Mary also prepared production-meeting reports and discussed them with colleagues and employees. These records helped consolidate compromises between program and technical production and could subsequently inform disputes. Visits to television sets supplied further opportunities to learn and establish relationships. Rouleau and Balogun describe enacting conversations and enrolling networks through semantic and sociocultural knowledge. Their vignette locates strategic implementation in continuing interpretation, documentation and relationships, alongside formal appointment and technical expertise. [ST-W003, pp. 15–18]

Christophe Torset's 2005 conceptual account questions a simple progression from reflection through decision to action. Ideas can remain active without producing a discrete decision; decisions about different questions can affect one another; action can contribute to subsequent understanding. His proposed conception of strategic reflection brings these activities into the same inquiry rather than treating thought as an isolated preliminary stage. [ST-W005, §1]

Torset also treats strategic reflection as an object and an instrument of research. Attention to how ideas arise and circulate can bring contributors into view who disappear when inquiry follows only formal decision makers. He includes analysis and planning practices within this account of meaning creation. His

argument changes what a researcher follows through an organization: the development of an idea and its practical influence may cross several decisions and levels. [ST-W005, §3]

Obtaining attention for an issue#

Jane Dutton, Susan Ashford, Regina O'Neill and Katherine Lawrence examine 82 issue-selling accounts from 42 individuals. Their concern is how people influence attention to developments with organizational significance. Presentation can make an issue intelligible through a business case; bundling connects it to valued goals or other concerns. One reported proposal for additional intensive-care space required work with physicians and data specialists on trends, demand and financial considerations. [ST-W002, pp. 716– 724]

Involvement and timing extend the process beyond presenting an argument. Earlier conversations can build support before an opportunity to act, while persistence can include setting an issue aside and returning later. The authors distinguish relational, normative and strategic contextual knowledge: knowing the people, acceptable ways of proceeding and organizational priorities affects the conduct of selling. Preparation also appears in unsuccessful accounts. These retrospective accounts describe how participants sought consideration for an issue and understood the outcome. S51 develops the conditions of voice, and S91 examines the construction of support and dependence. [ST-W002, pp. 724–731]

Participation and access to direction#

Saku Mantere and Eero Vaara study strategy work in twelve organizations, drawing on interviews with 301 people across hierarchical levels and additional organizational material. Their six discourses concern how strategy is understood, who is entitled to define it and what others are expected to do. They associate mystification, disciplining and technologization with restricted participation, and self-actualization, dialogization and concretization with wider participation. These tendencies coexist in several of the organizations. [ST-W004, pp. 344–347, Tables 2–3]

Mystification gives strategy a special status accessible to a privileged few. Visions and missions arrive from senior management, while others are expected to accept their authority and implement them. In the telecommunications firm of their second vignette, access to strategy documents depended on rank. A manager described learning the organization's intended direction only after promotion. Another employee contrasted earlier experience of participating in planning with a new employer's small inner circle and annual presentation. The authors connect restricted information and unquestionable direction with the expectation that employees lack either the standing or the knowledge to contribute. [ST-W004, pp. 347–349]

Disciplining makes hierarchy and obedience central to strategy work. In a retail chain, military language cast managers as commanders and operating personnel as followers; sanctions for noncompliance formed part of that understanding. A governmental office used an educational version: managers appeared as teachers or parents, and attendance at strategy discussions was an obligation. Being present in a discussion could thus accompany a subordinate role rather than an opportunity to help determine direction. [ST-W004, pp. 349–350]

Technologization places a particular system in command of what can be discussed and measured. An insurance firm adopted a balanced scorecard throughout its strategy process. Senior managers welcomed its greater controllability, but some employees found organization-level specifications difficult to use in departmental work. A team leader could not find a measure that would make excessive workload visible. The authors describe tools becoming control devices: employees were responsible for supplying measures within the system while having little opportunity to question its starting points. [ST-W004, pp. 350–351]

The participation-promoting discourses give people different roles. Self-actualization concerns finding meaning and defining objectives through one's own involvement. In an expert governmental office, people interpreted shared goals in relation to their work, and an employee with an idea could seek an appointment with the chief executive. Dialogization instead emphasizes organized exchange between levels and groups. In a financial organization, management supplied broad plans, teams developed action plans and sent them upward, and targets were discussed before being finalized. Senior management retained its role in setting direction while others had a recognized part in developing it. [ST-W004, pp. 351–352]

Concretization makes strategy intelligible through the continuing specification of activities, rules and procedures. In a municipal health authority, practitioners helped craft guidelines for problems encountered in their own work. Specifications were developed at different levels rather than simply arriving from the center. The distinction from disciplining lies in the roles that the rules make possible: the people doing the work can help construct its practical direction. Mantere and Vaara also describe concretization as a demand voiced within organizations where strategy remained remote and obscure. Their analysis follows how these understandings legitimize or restrict participation through meetings, information access, systems and everyday interaction. [ST-W004, pp. 352–355]

Roles connecting decisions and implementation#

李炜文, 高岩 and 李晓彤's 2022 review examines 103 articles published between 1986 and 2020. It organizes middle managers' roles around knowledge management, relationship connections, participation, autonomous decisions and implementation. Knowledge and relationships supply routes through which managers influence decisions, while implementation entails interpreting aims and coordinating their realization. The review brings decision and execution into the same account of middle-management work. [ST-W006, research method and Table 2]

The review distinguishes benefits attributed to participation from a contrary explanation of managerial conduct. Studies it discusses associate middle managers' involvement with correcting senior decision makers' cognitive biases, improving decision quality, aligning objectives and enabling strategic change. It also reports Natarajan and colleagues' finding that managers may make unnecessary resource allocations to demonstrate their management ability and increase personal income. Such activity can reduce operating efficiency. The disagreement concerns what managers do with their position and the consequences of that conduct, rather than whether they take part in decisions at all. [ST-W006, decision participation]

Implementation requires work on both interpretation and activity. The reviewers describe managers developing shared understandings with peers and making strategic aims meaningful in particular settings. They also discuss dividing complex objectives into tasks that employees can understand and carry out. Their reconstruction of Beatty and Lee's study of computer-aided systems in companies in Britain and Canada emphasizes managers solving employees' difficulties through communication, encouragement and control of the implementation process. [ST-W006, strategic implementation]

Emotions enter this account through managers' own commitment and through their responses to other employees. The review describes resistance arising when middle managers question the legitimacy of a major change, alongside accounts of managers responding to employees' negative emotions during implementation. It identifies institutional differences, cultural settings and digital forms of work as questions for further investigation. S86 examines project governance, and S88 follows the continuing responsibilities of product development. [ST-W006, strategic implementation and discussion]

谷口正一郎's 2021 study reconstructs an employee innovation program at Company X, a food manufacturer, through interviews with eight people across functions and ranks. Introduced by a new president in 2011, the program asked employees to report initiatives outside their ordinary duties to directors. Selected initiatives could receive awards and become new businesses or formal company practices. Employees initially differed over what innovation meant for their work. Some associated it with patented products and could see little opportunity for someone in sales to contribute. The president's explanation of an award-winning example helped broaden their understanding of what could qualify. [ST-W008, pp. 26–28, 31–32]

Participation developed through changes to the program and its relationship to ordinary management. One interviewee recalled that it had not initially been required of everyone, but that roughly one initiative per person was encouraged from the second year. Participation became part of appraisal, and a manager reported that subordinates' failure to participate could affect the manager's own evaluation. Employees who preferred routine work consequently experienced an obligation to contribute. A dedicated budget made initiatives possible that otherwise lacked funding; an employee also described approved funding as a commitment to carry the work through. [ST-W008, pp. 32–33]

These arrangements changed supervisors' responsibilities. One interviewee described managers helping teams finish routine work earlier or reduce it so that employees could devote time to innovation. Other departments' assistance and senior managers' attention to individual initiatives made support visible. The program office coordinated the scheme, training and adjustments, while department heads retained appraisal authority. Its office therefore worked with personnel planners on the appraisal system rather than directly assigning employees' evaluations. [ST-W008, pp. 30–34, note 4]

The case also follows difficulty sustaining involvement. Employees could run out of ideas, struggle to combine initiatives with operating targets or feel that another department had better opportunities to win recognition. Adjustments emphasized the reasoning and experience behind an attempt: what need the employee had identified, what hypothesis had been tried and what happened. Reporting formats asked for that explanation, and interviewees described being encouraged to try even when an initiative failed. The author's process model connects these responses with renewed participation. [ST-W008, Table 5, Figure 2, pp. 34–35]

Developing an initiative brought employees into contact with the work of other departments: pricing required finance, volumes required product management and logistics, and an idea could require training staff in stores. Interviewees described improved internal communication, greater visibility for their abilities and links between recognition, promotion and transfers. Some also described reorganizing routine duties to create time for further initiatives. The account follows participation through changes in working relationships, career opportunities and the organization of everyday work. [ST-W008, pp. 35– 36]

What judgments and measures observe#

许志行 and 曹骞's 2019 review distinguishes attention to strategic activities from attention to kinds of thinking. Its landscape includes management-oriented accounts and game-theoretic reasoning. The reviewers show how conceptions of strategic thinking shape the instruments used to recognize it. An attribute scale begins with proposed characteristics, such as a systems perspective or attention to the future, and turns them into questionnaire items. Differences over the characteristics therefore become differences between the scales. [ST-W007, §§III–V]

The cognitive logic approach described in the review is also a questionnaire. It distinguishes ways of gathering information, through intuition or sensation, from ways of evaluating it, through logic or feeling.

Their combinations produce four proposed modes of thought; the instrument treats strategic thinking as their interaction. Written-text analysis takes another route, examining semantic features of an author's work to estimate tendencies such as risk-taking and foresight. Experimental approaches instead observe choices in structured tasks or games, including how participants recognize similarities and respond to risks and rewards. [ST-W007, §V]

The reviewers' objections are specific to these approaches. The proposed division into four cognitive modes remains disputed. Text analysis depends on the quantity and quality of available writing. Experiments can narrow strategic thinking to reasoning within a game. Widely used attribute scales inherit disagreements over what the capability consists of, making findings difficult to accumulate and compare. The reviewers also question scales developed without sufficiently rigorous item refinement and reliability and validity evaluation. Naming desirable characteristics does not by itself establish a sound measure. [ST-W007, §V]

Their criticism of attribute lists also concerns explanation. They criticize one comprehensive model for simply packaging characteristics collected from earlier authors. They also question whether existing attribute definitions distinguish strategic thinking sufficiently from other thought. The reviewers connect concept definition, attributes, instruments and research on effects: a change in what the concept means changes what subsequent measurements can tell us. Their treatment separates individual from organizational influences and asks how they interact over time. S71 discusses practice and skill development. [ST-W007, §V and prospects]

Stefan Kühl's 2017 interview with Martin Claßen supplies a different diagnostic perspective on managerial judgments. Kühl describes pressure on newly appointed managers to display change even where continuity may be appropriate. He distinguishes formal hierarchy from influence arising through expertise, customer access and informal communication. His argument directs attention to organizational expectations that make some conduct visible as an achievement. [ST-W009]

Kühl's account also places the possibility of retaining a course alongside the expectation of intervention. The workplace questions consequently concern what is being pursued, who can contribute to it and how that contribution is recognized. The historical accounts give these questions different practical settings: a sustained individual activity, the interpretation of an established direction, a concern seeking attention and an institution organizing participation.

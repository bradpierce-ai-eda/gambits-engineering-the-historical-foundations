Relations and organizational change#

Alexander Bogdanov's Tektology asks how things are joined, sustained, separated and reorganized. Its proposed subject extends from work and social cooperation to organisms, machines and scientific thought. Bogdanov examines how an arrangement changes what its elements can accomplish, how an environment assists or frustrates that accomplishment, and how organization persists. [TKT-W001; W017]

Bogdanov's revised 1927 energy discussion describes transformations as not fully reversible. [TKT-W002, p. 236]

Organization is a relation between activities#

Bogdanov's starting question is practical: what difference does combining elements make to their capacity to act? Cooperation supplies an accessible case. Two people can sometimes perform a task together that neither can perform separately. A resistance that exceeds either person's effective effort can be overcome when their efforts are appropriately joined. This is more informative than the slogan that a whole exceeds its parts. The relevant comparison concerns what the parts can accomplish separately and what their particular combination can accomplish against a specified resistance. Bogdanov explicitly locates the difference in the relation between their activities and the resistances they encounter. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. II, A §1]

The qualification about the combination is essential. Workers may impede one another, or expend effort in opposed directions. Bogdanov illustrates the latter possibility with people pulling a rope against each other: much of the effort disappears from the resultant available to move the whole arrangement. Even successful cooperation retains some internal interference. His organized, disorganized, and intermediate combinations therefore describe differences in effective relations, rather than a division between flawless organizations and everything else. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. II, A §§1–2]

One of his examples also prevents an easy identification of a collective gain with a gain for every participant. A mother carrying an infant enables both to change location, while the mother's own burden increases. The organizational effect depends on which activities and whose situation are being counted. The example's illustrative coefficients should not be mistaken for measured results. Its substantive point is the difference between the capacity of a combined arrangement and the distribution of effort within it. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. II, A §1]

An activity becomes a resistance when it opposes another activity. Bogdanov makes this change of standpoint explicit through conflict: what is an effort from one participant's position is an obstacle from the other's. The same description cannot silently assign the interests of both to one side. His broad term “activities–resistances” is intended to keep their relationship in view. Some of the accompanying physical explanations belong to the science of his period; the relational distinction does not require adopting those explanations as current physics. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. II, A §2]

The elements of an analysis are similarly relative to the task. A society may be considered through enterprises or people; an organism through cells; a theoretical construction through concepts. An element may in turn be examined as a complex of further elements when the question requires it. This is a method of selecting an analytical scale. It explains why a description of an organization's overall result can leave an important internal burden invisible: the description may have treated the bearer of that burden as an undivided element. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. II, A §2]

Organization is also relative to the activity and environment under consideration. Bogdanov's factory can be effectively arranged for technical production while containing serious social antagonisms. A machine can resist the forces for which it was designed and remain vulnerable to quite different forces. Complexity alone does not make a complex well organized. These passages restrict what can be inferred from a successful performance: the arrangement works in some respect, against some resistance, under some surrounding conditions. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. II, A §3]

Bogdanov relates organization to an activity, the resistance it encounters and the unit whose performance is being considered. The same structure can facilitate one activity while obstructing another.

Specialized experience and the organization of knowledge#

Bogdanov presents the fragmentation of knowledge as an organizational problem as well as an intellectual one. Specialization permits increasingly detailed work, but differentiated fields develop their own objects, techniques, and language. Their distance can obscure relations that might otherwise make experience in one field usable in another. Conversely, a resemblance between words can suggest a relationship that the actual activities do not support. His argument for a general organizational science includes the ambition to recover transferable methods without relying on verbal resemblance alone. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. I, discussion of scientific specialization and unity of methods]

Bogdanov's examples of methods crossing disciplinary boundaries provide reasons to investigate relations between different bodies of experience. His later discussion of analogy, considered below, is relevant because it asks for further observations when a pattern admits more than one explanation. [TKT-W001, part I, chs. I–II and appendix, “The doctrine of analogies”]

Bogdanov includes the arrangement of concepts and accumulated experience among the things that people organize. A scientific classification, a technical method, and the communication needed for cooperation can become part of the conditions under which subsequent work occurs. His explanation does not allow their usefulness to be inferred merely from their generality: the relation to the activity still has to be specified.

Connecting through a suitable intermediary#

Bogdanov calls the joining or interaction of complexes conjugation. A connection need not consist in simply putting two previously separate things next to each other. His account of chain linkage asks what actually enables their activities to connect. In a chain, successive links can have different connecting features: A shares something relevant with B, while B connects to C through another feature. The existence of a long chain consequently does not require every participant to resemble every other participant or even to know that the others exist. His social and cognitive illustrations are intended to disclose the connecting relations, not merely to collect cases of superficial similarity. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, A, “Chain linkage”]

Difference can support a connection when the different capacities meet in a common undertaking. Bogdanov considers a theorist and a practitioner. Their contrast is useful only if the activities are directed compatibly and one can carry forward the effort where the other stops. Merely giving two people complementary labels establishes neither cooperation nor the connecting relation. The relevant commonality concerns the undertaking and the point at which their activities meet. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, A, “Chain linkage”]

Ingression names the introduction of an intermediary through which a connection becomes possible. His clear social example concerns two people who need to agree on a plan but do not share a language. A translator has a different relevant commonality with each: one language with the first person, another

with the second. The intermediary is therefore not just an additional person in the group. Its particular relations to both sides enable an exchange that the initial pair could not carry out directly. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, A, “Ingression”]

The practical interest of this account lies in the specificity of the connection. A useful intermediary must meet the conditions on both sides. In Bogdanov's vocabulary, identifying something as a link does not by itself describe how it links. Nor does the general mechanism imply that adding connections always improves an arrangement. An additional connection may expose a complex to destructive activities, or join participants whose activities interfere. What needs explanation is the effect of the particular linkage upon the particular activities being considered. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, A]

Separation, conflicting interests, and the choice of a whole#

The book treats separation as an organizational event in its own right. Bogdanov's disingression concerns the disruption or cancellation of connections. An apparent destruction at one level can reduce destructive interference at another. An organization already consumed by internal conflict may divide into groups that function more effectively after separation. In his description, some internal oppositions have been replaced by relations between now distinct complexes. Whether the new situation is preferable depends on those relations and on subsequent conditions; separation does not acquire a favorable meaning merely by being described as reorganization. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, A, “Disingression”]

Bogdanov makes the asymmetry particularly explicit through divorce and amputation. The same break can relieve one party and devastate another; an amputation can preserve an organism while destroying the separated part. Their function is to demonstrate why the fate of one element does not settle the fate of the combination, or vice versa. A single description such as liberation, destruction, or organizational improvement can conceal the standpoint from which it was made. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, A, “Disingression”]

He accordingly asks for whom and for what a change is better. He also proposes considering a disputed arrangement within a larger social complex. The latter move describes his method and social orientation; it does not, by itself, settle the ethical priority of the larger whole. A family, organization, class, or society can have interests that differ from those of its members. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, A, “Disingression”]

Bogdanov’s joining and separation can remove recurring friction, enable a blocked activity or redistribute demands among participants. His questions about who benefits and which larger complex is considered distinguish these consequences.

Selection can be performed by an arrangement#

Bogdanov's use of selection is deliberately broader than biological natural selection. He removes reproduction and heredity from the general schema because those are not properties of every complex to which he wishes to apply it. A pendulum's succession of positions is among his illustrations: the positions are not a simultaneously existing population of organisms. Selection, at this level of abstraction, concerns what persists or is eliminated under specified conditions. Its proposed universality belongs to his program of a general organizational science. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, B, “Conservative selection”]

The sieve provides a particularly concrete technical case. Bogdanov distinguishes direct sorting by a person's successive interventions from an arrangement in which material differences and the device's resistances do the discriminating. Smaller particles can pass through an opening that stops larger ones. The distinction between the particles is thereby made effective in their different trajectories. A worker still prepares, moves, and uses the apparatus; the account does not describe a process without activity. It explains how a relation between particle dimensions, openings, and motion can perform a discriminating

operation without a fresh individual judgment about each particle. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, B, “Conservative selection”]

His adjacent gold-washing example changes the relevant relation. Water carries away some material while heavier gold remains. In this account, a useful result depends on a material difference that the process can actually exploit. Calling both operations selection should not erase the difference between aperture-based separation and separation in a moving fluid. Bogdanov also invokes fractional distillation as a staged separation through differences in response to heating. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, B, “Conservative selection”]

The same chapter extends the sieve image to admission conditions, organizational rules, and examinations. These social examples change the criterion of relevance: what is admitted is appropriate to the selecting party's purposes. A hiring condition can select workers suited to an employer, which is a different assertion from selecting an arrangement beneficial to everyone affected. The earlier distinction between activities and resistances therefore continues to matter. Selection records a relationship to a criterion and surrounding conditions; the term does not certify the criterion's justice or the selected outcome's general superiority. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, B, “Conservative selection”]

Selection also enters the search for an arrangement. Bogdanov describes difficult invention and discovery as involving the formation and rejection of many combinations before an acceptable one is found. This is distinct from the later use of a successful discriminating mechanism. The cost of finding a workable arrangement and the activity required to operate it are both present in his examples, even though he does not offer a general calculation comparing them. A description of the successful device alone would omit that history of trial. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, B, “Conservative selection”]

Finally, he does not leave selection as a wholly separate class of forces alongside joining and separation. Assimilating something from an environment joins it to one complex and requires detaching it from relations in which it previously participated. He therefore presents selection's regulating mechanism as a combination of forming mechanisms. This is an explicit connection within Bogdanov's theory. It helps explain why his sieve and his social examples concern both persistence and changing relations. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. III, B, conclusion]

A passage from Bogdanov's 1921 Essays on Universal Organizational Science makes the intended extension especially plain. Its section on the regulating mechanism describes lawful preservation or elimination as the initial schema of selection. It drops “natural” from the name because the proposed organizational science does not treat the natural–artificial distinction as fundamental, and explicitly extends the schema to complexes, systems, connections, and boundaries. This passage supports an account of the breadth Bogdanov claimed for selection. [GL-W097, II.1]

A sieve selects a property, not a purpose#

Bogdanov later returns to the sieve to show a limit that the successful technical example can obscure. A person wants flour with useful material properties, but the sieve discriminates particle size. Under the ordinary preparation conditions he describes, size is associated closely enough with the desired material for the operation to serve its purpose. Size represents the other properties in this procedure. The mechanism does not directly recognize all the qualities that make the material useful. Bogdanov calls this representative selection. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. VII, discussion of representative selection]

Alter the mixture and the distinction becomes decisive. Fine sand can pass through alongside flour. Harmful material of an appropriate size can do so as well. The sieve can perform its size discrimination correctly while the intended selection fails. Bogdanov's point is therefore stronger than saying that a device occasionally breaks down. The device's operative distinction and the user's desired distinction can

cease to correspond. Their earlier correspondence depended on the preparation of the input and the surrounding circumstances. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. VII]

His gold-processing discussion makes the same issue cumulative. A further separation can remove an impurity that the preceding operation could not distinguish, while itself leaving other substances undistinguished. This illustrates the limitations of successive practical criteria. The chemical procedures are historical illustrations, rather than instructions offered here for their present use. Bogdanov explicitly separates the task's criterion from the property upon which the available apparatus can act. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. VII]

Stability depends on exposure as well as strength#

Bogdanov's law of relative least resistance is often easier to understand through its qualifications than through a bare weakest-link formula. Different parts of a structure face different loads. Even links of a hanging chain do not face identical demands when the upper links also carry the weight of those below. The relevant comparison is consequently between the resistance of a part and the activity directed against it, rather than between the parts' absolute strengths alone. A change in loading or a temporary loss of resistance can change the decisive location. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. IV, discussion of relative resistances]

Bogdanov gives this principle a very broad reach. The examples establish why a locally insufficient resistance can matter to an interconnected whole; they do not constitute a proof that the smallest component capacity determines every conceivable system's performance. The consequences of a local rupture still depend on the surrounding organizational conditions. In particular, the simple chain illustration does not describe every parallel arrangement or every way of preserving a function after a part fails. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. IV]

For practical preparation, Bogdanov distinguishes two situations. In one, the kind and magnitude of the relevant demands can be determined with enough regularity to place suitable resistance where it is needed. In the other, the future demands are substantially undetermined. Designing a machine for specified operating conditions and preparing a child for an uncertain social life are his contrasting examples. These are not two entirely separate worlds: an arrangement can face relatively determinate demands in one respect and uncertain demands in another. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. IV, “Practical tasks”]

Importantly, an activity can change the circumstances of the problem. Building a shelter or cultivating land can make aspects of temperature, moisture, or food supply less uncertain for the person acting. Bogdanov presents these changes as a partial movement from the second kind of problem toward the first. The environment is therefore not merely an outside disturbance that the system must absorb in its original form. It can be worked upon, with the resulting arrangement changing what the system subsequently has to withstand or obtain. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. IV, “Practical tasks”]

His contrast between urban and unfamiliar surroundings adds a cognitive example. Street names, house numbers, and clocks reduce the demands of ordinary orientation in a city. A person accustomed to those supports may face a much harder problem in unfamiliar terrain without them, and may need knowledge and instruments that were unnecessary to the familiar task. The source locates part of the difference in the organized surroundings. It does not reduce the city dweller's performance to an individual endowment considered apart from that setting. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. IV, “Practical tasks”]

Bogdanov uses the same discussion to criticize educational preparation that continues to answer an earlier social situation. He argues that training for obedience can become ill suited to circumstances requiring initiative, and that established educational subjects can consume effort after their former practical role has changed. These are his historically and politically situated judgments about education.

Their relevance here lies in the explicit comparison between the preparation supplied and the demands actually encountered. [TKT-W001, part I, ch. IV, “Practical tasks”]

Differentiation produces both capacity and coordination problems#

In the second part of Tektology, differentiation is not simply an increase in the number of different parts. Different activities develop under different conditions and at different rates. Complementary relations can arise: what one part supplies enables another to continue. But differences can also deepen into misalignment. Bogdanov's social discussion of the division of labor therefore includes productive specialization, market conflict, disproportions between branches of production, and losses when enterprises are destroyed. His explanation is framed by his account of class society and production. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. V, discussion of system contradictions]

The problem of recovering mutual understanding receives a more intimate illustration. Bogdanov describes a historically conventional household in which a husband occupied with public affairs and a wife occupied with domestic work become increasingly unable to understand each other's experiences. More communication might restore a relation that specialization has weakened. Yet the remedy requires time and energy. If the participants have none to spare, the attempt can impair their other activities, create fatigue and new failures, and make the situation worse. The organizational difficulty he identifies is that an activity intended to repair coordination also consumes resources already committed elsewhere. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. V, “The mechanism of removing contradictions”]

Bogdanov also describes a successful alternative. An exchange of experience can change how the specialized activities themselves are carried out: one participant may gain practical calculation, another a wider view of possible action. In that case, communication need not merely subtract time from otherwise unchanged functions. It can improve their conduct. The text presents possible courses of development, not a measured rule specifying when one will occur. The account's useful detail lies precisely in keeping both possibilities: mutual understanding is neither free nor necessarily an unproductive overhead. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. V]

Egression: concentrating action through unequal influence#

Bogdanov distinguishes the concentration of activities through a more influential complex from the mere existence of a connection. He calls this egression. The relationship is unequal in its effects: one complex influences the development or movement of another more than the latter influences it in return. His illustrations span physical systems and human leadership. In the social case, the issue concerns the organization of activity through command, example, or other unequal influence. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. VI, §§1–2]

A hierarchy of commanders supplies a direct example. Smaller groups are coordinated through local commanders, whose activities are in turn connected through commanders at another level. This chain can concentrate the action of many participants without requiring the highest commander to deal directly with each person. Bogdanov's powers-of-ten illustration shows the arithmetic possibility of extending such a chain under an assumed span of direct coordination. It does not demonstrate that an actual hierarchy can communicate, understand, or execute every necessary action at those scales. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. VI, §2]

He immediately gives reasons why the concentrating capacity has limits. As a chain lengthens, the most distant participants may be only weakly affected by the nominal center. Apparent authority during a stable situation need not provide effective guidance during rapid development or disintegration. The ruler who seems powerful may be unable to secure obedience or to improve the condition of remote

subjects. The limitation concerns the actual relation through intervening links, rather than the authority represented in a title or diagram. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. VI, §2]

There is also a problem of divergence between organizers and those expected to execute their directions. Their circumstances and experience can become different enough that mutual understanding deteriorates. Bogdanov describes unsuitable orders, unanticipated situations in which obedience supplies no answer, arbitrary acts, and antagonistic responses. These are costs of a particular development of the organization, rather than exceptions that can be removed from an account of concentration. His chapter thus contains both the capacity enabled by hierarchical connections and mechanisms that can undermine that capacity. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. VI, §2]

Degression: protection and fixation without command#

Degression concerns a different relation. A relatively stable formation fixes or protects a more plastic, vulnerable activity. Bogdanov introduces the issue through the advantages and vulnerabilities of plasticity: flexible relations allow new combinations and adaptation, but can also be more readily disrupted. The skeletal illustration is literal in some cases, yet he explicitly warns against treating all such formations as mechanically hard shells. The protective relation concerns particular destructive influences. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. VI, §3]

For example, his account includes a cloud of ink that interferes with a predator's visually directed action. The proposed protection is temporary and concerns a particular way an attack is guided. This helps explain why the term's scope extends beyond rigid structures. The shared question is what stabilizes or protects the relevant activity against an identified influence, not what objects happen to look like skeletons. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. VI, §3]

Words and other symbols receive an extended treatment within the same discussion. A word is comparatively stable beside the shifting, elaborate experiences associated with it. In Bogdanov's account, that stability helps fix experience, make it communicable, and preserve it collectively beyond an individual's life. He explicitly resists interpreting the word as a superior center that determines all the changes in its associated representations. The symbol's fixing function is why he places it under degression, rather than egression. His particular account of the labor origins of language draws on Ludwig Noiré. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. VI, §3]

Protection can also become restraint. In Bogdanov's developmental argument, the protected activity can grow or change faster than its stabilizing formation. A child's clothing and a dwelling with a growing population make the difference visible: something that previously accommodated activity can become restrictive without undergoing a matching change itself. The account at issue here is the possibility of a changing relation between an activity and the arrangement that fixes or protects it. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. VI, §4]

Egression and degression can coexist while referring to different activities. Bogdanov's ship is directed in its movement by the crew and captain, while its hull contains and protects captain, crew, passengers, and cargo. One relation does not have to be redescribed as the other. His seven diagrams similarly distinguish connection, concentration, and fixation. Their lines indicate linkages, not directional arrows; their filled and unfilled circles mark his comparison of organizational levels. He warns that their spatial appearance is schematic: a dark circle drawn at the center need not describe an internal physical skeleton. Reading the diagrams as ordinary organizational charts would therefore miss his stated meaning. [TKT-W001, part II, ch. VI, §5]

Reorganization and the meaning of a crisis#

Bogdanov's treatment of crises examines changes of organizational form. What counts as such a change depends on the features of the form being studied. A broad description of an organism's persistence can treat many changes as phases within one continuing life; a more detailed description of its functions recognizes further transitions. This is not simply a terminological preference. It makes the scale and purpose of an analysis relevant to the changes that the analysis can detect. [TKT-W001, part III, chapter on crises, §1]

He distinguishes connecting crises, which break an existing boundary and establish new relations, from separating crises, which establish boundaries through the disruption of connections. The abstract contrast is simpler than an actual course of reorganization: real processes combine both kinds of change. The label “crisis” also does not require that the process be rapid on an everyday human timescale. [TKT-W001, part III, chapter on crises, §2]

In a later discussion Bogdanov distinguishes quantitative accumulation within a form from structural improvement through reduced internal interference or reduced fruitless expenditure. These can move in opposite directions. His use of Ringelmann's reported cooperation results illustrates his concern with the relationship between group size and effective result, and he acknowledges that the kind of work and mode of coordination matter. Bogdanov also proposes that moral, aesthetic, and cognitive judgments can ultimately be understood as organizational evaluations. [TKT-W001, part III, discussion of structural progress and regress, §§2–3]

Economic interdependence and the limits of a plan#

An earlier appendix on labor and workers' needs makes explicit a burden that descriptions of final output can miss. Bogdanov treats complex work as involving the coordination and mutual checking of many activities, including attention, deliberation, initiative, and regulation. In his physiological explanation these processes require expenditure and replacement of nervous energy. He acknowledges that the relevant multipliers cannot then be measured directly. The passage is evidence that his account includes the worker's continuing support and replenishment; it is not current neuroscientific evidence for a quantitative conversion from skill to energy use. [TKT-W001, part I, appendix, “Labor and the needs of the worker”]

His proposed social calculation consequently turns toward the consumption requirements for sustaining different kinds of labor, rather than claiming to have measured a physiological coefficient. The proposal links the organization of work to the material conditions of maintaining the people who perform it. It is also bound to his theory of value and intended planned economy. [TKT-W001, part I, appendix, “Labor and the needs of the worker”]

An appendix on a unified economic plan shows Bogdanov applying the organizational perspective to linked production. His starting point is the maintenance of people, including workers of different qualifications, people unable to work, and the preparation of future workers. He then traces the goods required for that maintenance back through the materials, fuel, tools, and further productive means needed to supply them. The purpose of the chain is to make the functional dependencies of production explicit. It is not merely an aggregate output target. [TKT-W001, part I, appendix, “A unified economic plan”]

His schematic calculation includes substitutable inputs, such as different fuels, rather than assuming that every input has one irreplaceable form. He compares the resulting requirements with existing productive capacities. If capacities are deficient, the proposed recovery sequence gives special attention to the branches whose deficiencies constrain the rest. Expanding transport fully in advance can be wasteful, in

his example, if other branches cannot yet make use of that carrying capacity. This is Bogdanov's proposed reconstruction procedure under the scheme's assumptions. [TKT-W001, part I, appendix, “A unified economic plan”]

The qualifications within the proposal are substantial. Immediate survival or strategic demands can alter the recovery sequence. Trade requires surpluses and changes the linked requirements. Most strikingly, Bogdanov says that completely accounting for and regulating the vast number of small peasant households is inconceivable, while also rejecting the treatment of them as simply external to the state economy. Food, fuel, labor, and the households' own needs make their relations consequential to the plan. His stated confidence in the problem's tektological solvability should therefore be read alongside the practical limits he acknowledges, rather than used to erase them. [TKT-W001, part I, appendix, “A unified economic plan”]

Analogy requires evidence about a mechanism#

The appendix reviewing Michel Petrovitch's Mécanismes communs aux phénomènes disparates is relevant to how such a wide-ranging organizational program handles comparison. In Bogdanov's account of that book, a pattern in the course of a phenomenon does not uniquely identify the mechanism producing it. Similar movements can result from forces governed differently. Additional observations are needed to reject mechanisms that do not fit. This restriction matters wherever an argument moves from a familiar pattern to an explanation of how an arrangement works. [TKT-W001, part I, appendix, “The doctrine of analogies”; TKT-W003]

Bogdanov’s review describes comparisons across mechanical, electrical, physiological, and social phenomena, including an effort to match the roles that elements play in different processes. Bogdanov finds heuristic value in these comparisons while criticizing some of the economic explanations. An analogy can suggest a mechanism; further evidence is needed to establish the explanation. [TKT-W001, part I, appendix, “The doctrine of analogies”; TKT-W003]

Rispoli's interpretation of environment and collective knowledge#

Giulia Rispoli's study selects a dimension that a treatment confined to devices or organizational charts would miss. She interprets Bogdanov's environment as both a source of conditions upon activity and something changed through organisms' and people's activities. It is neither an empty space awaiting action nor an independently fixed set of determinants. Her discussion of the differing surroundings of a plant's parts and of relations among organisms emphasizes that the relevant environment depends on the relation under examination. Her comparisons with later ecological and systems ideas are the historian's interpretation. [GL-W022, pp. 131–134]

Rispoli then relates this account to knowledge produced through collective work. On her reading, shared practical activity precedes and supports the verbal exchange of experience; skills, tools, practices, and the material setting belong to the production and transmission of knowledge. Her discussion of Proletkult places the proposal within Bogdanov's political and cultural undertaking, including workers' organization of their own cultural activity. [GL-W022, pp. 134–136]

Joining, selection, differentiation and collective knowledge#

The relevant account in Tektology is extensive because its subjects are connected within the author's project. Joining and separation explain changing relations; selection explains persistence through particular conditions; relative resistance makes loading and exposure consequential; differentiation gives rise to both capacities and conflicts; concentration and fixation describe distinct organizational functions.

The economic and cognitive discussions are applications within that project, with their own purposes and limits.

For a reader studying gambits engineering, the most concrete learning lies in the distinctions that the examples make available: what an arrangement accomplishes and who supplies the effort; what property a selector discriminates and what purpose that property represents; what a center can actually influence and what authority it nominally possesses; what protects an activity and what eventually restricts it.

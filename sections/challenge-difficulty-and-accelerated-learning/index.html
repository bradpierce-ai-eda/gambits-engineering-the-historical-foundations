Learning that outlasts a good performance#

Training can make someone perform well while the training conditions remain in place. Richard Schmidt and Robert Bjork’s 1992 account asks what that performance reveals about the capability that will remain when those conditions change. They compare findings from motor and verbal learning and argue that practice should be judged in relation to the later performance for which it prepares a person. A successful attempt during instruction and a successful attempt after a delay answer different questions. [LP-W030]

Their comparison includes arrangements that make acquisition look less successful while improving later performance. Varying or switching tasks, reducing some forms of assistance and distributing activity can change what the learner must do during practice. The authors interpret such findings through the processes required by the eventual task. Their training argument begins with that criterion performance and asks whether practice exercises the processes it will require. [LP-W030]

This is an ambitious historical integration across experimental traditions. Schmidt and Bjork seek common principles in results that had often been considered within separate research paradigms. Their proposed account changes the role of the practice session: its immediate score becomes one observation among others, while retention and transfer become central tests of its value as training. [LP-W030]

Nicholas Soderstrom and Robert Bjork’s 2015 review develops the distinction between performance and learning through a larger literature. They use learning to concern relatively durable changes that support later retention and transfer, while performance is the behavior observed under present conditions. A temporary aid can improve current execution without producing the same improvement when removed. Conversely, a condition that initially disrupts fluent execution can prepare better later performance. [LP-W019]

The distinction also affects learners’ decisions. A fluent response can seem to show that material is mastered; a difficult attempt can seem to show that a method is failing. The review examines cases in which these judgments follow immediate performance more closely than later results. Its verbal- and motor-learning accounts explain why judging a learning procedure requires specifying what should become possible afterward and how that capability will be assessed. The schema-acquisition and guidance studies in S41 develop related instructional questions through their own accounts. [LP-W019]

Effort and the learning edge#

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi and Judith LeFevre’s 1989 study examined the quality of experience in work and leisure. Seventy-eight workers supplied repeated reports during a week, allowing experiences to be related to perceived challenge, perceived skill and the activity being undertaken. Their analysis distinguished situations with high challenge and high skill from those with little of either. Absorption and engagement were examined in relation to what a person was doing and felt capable of doing. [LP-W031]

Work and leisure did not simply divide experience into burdensome and rewarding states. The authors found patterns in which work supplied challenges and opportunities for skill that could be associated with a richer experience, even while participants might prefer to be doing something else. Their subject was the organization and felt quality of experience. That provides the setting for later arguments about flow without making experience-sampling reports measurements of how quickly a skill was acquired. [LP-W031]

Phillip Armour’s 2006 essay The Learning Edge brings competence and task difficulty into a practical argument about software work. His diagrams place routine activity toward boredom, demands far beyond

capability toward anxiety, and a zone of competence between them. The learning edge lies near the boundary of what the person can presently do. As competence grows, an unchanged task can move from challenge into routine; the edge moves with the learner. [LP-W018]

Armour argues that organizations should help people reach and remain near this moving edge. He makes a stronger claim about accelerated learning at its outer boundary: stress can be pushed far enough to force development, provided it does not cause the learner to shut down. Marine recruit training and Outward Bound supply his illustrations of people placed in demanding situations. The essay treats discomfort as potentially productive and challenges managers to attend to learning rather than viewing software work only as the delivery of already mastered activity. [LP-W018]

His account is a practitioner’s argument, supported by these illustrations and a proposed relation among challenge, competence and experience. Its useful practical question is how an organization supplies tasks beyond comfortable repetition while keeping a person able to engage with them. The instructional role of support is developed in S41; the interpersonal risks of exposing uncertainty are examined in S51. [LP-W018]

Elizabeth Ligon Bjork and Robert Bjork’s 2011 account of desirable difficulties concerns demands that can improve later learning even when they impede immediate performance. Their distinction between retrieval strength and storage strength separates current accessibility from the more enduring entrenchment of learning. Material can be easy to bring to mind now because it has just been seen, while that accessibility gives an unreliable impression of what will be available after an interval. [LP-W028]

Generating an answer, attempting recall, spacing encounters and switching among examples change the activity that must be performed. The authors explain their desirability through what those activities can contribute to learning, rather than through unpleasantness itself. Their chapter also addresses judgments made during study: familiar material can invite confidence without requiring the learner to reproduce or use it. The experiments below give particular procedures and outcomes behind several of these recommendations. [LP-W028]

Matching challenge to the learner#

Mark Guadagnoli and Timothy Lee’s 2004 challenge-point framework distinguishes nominal task difficulty from functional difficulty. Nominal difficulty concerns the task’s demands. Functional difficulty also depends on the person attempting it and the conditions under which it is attempted. The same task can thus supply a different challenge at different stages of a learner’s development. [LP-W021]

Their golf example makes the distinction tangible. A pitch shot over water can be a substantial problem for a beginner and a familiar task for a skilled golfer. Keeping the physical shot unchanged does not keep the functional difficulty unchanged. Feedback and practice conditions further alter what information is available and what the performer must process. The account concerns a relation among task, skill and the organization of practice. [LP-W021]

The proposed framework distinguishes potential information from information the learner can interpret and use. Increasing difficulty can supply more opportunity to learn, until processing demands exceed what that learner can manage. Their diagrams consequently distinguish the functional difficulty associated with the best current practice performance from the difficulty associated with the greatest potential learning benefit. A session chosen solely to produce the cleanest immediate performance can occupy a different point on those proposed relations. [LP-W021]

As skill increases, the framework predicts that suitable functional difficulty should increase too. The golfer can attempt a more demanding task or practise under conditions that require more selection and interpretation. Guadagnoli and Lee use this argument to interpret findings about task order and feedback,

including why their effects can depend on task complexity. Their challenge point is a theoretical account of interpretable information and learning, rather than a scale for measuring emotional distress. [LP-W021]

Learning by trying to remember#

A memory test can change the memory it examines. In Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke’s 2006 experiments, students learned prose passages and then either studied them again or tried to recall them. The recall attempts supplied no corrective feedback. A later test compared what remained available after different delays. Replacing another encounter with the text by an attempt to produce it changed the subsequent course of remembering. [LP-W022]

In their first experiment, additional study produced better recall five minutes later: 81 percent of the scored idea units, compared with 75 percent after an initial recall test. Two days later the order reversed. Students who had previously attempted recall remembered 68 percent, compared with 54 percent after additional study. After a week, the corresponding figures were 56 and 42 percent. The apparent winner depended on when the outcome was measured. [LP-W022]

The second experiment arranged four learning periods in three ways: four periods of study; three periods of study followed by one recall test; or one period of study followed by three tests. Students wrote what they could remember rather than reproducing exact wording or a required order. Repeated testing provided further attempts to retrieve the passage without showing it again. It therefore changed both what students did and how often they encountered the original text. [LP-W022]

Again, repeated study led on the five-minute test, whereas repeated testing led after a week. The students who studied throughout read their passage approximately fourteen times, compared with about three and a half readings in the repeated-test condition. Nevertheless, after a week the repeated-test group recalled 61 percent of the idea units and the repeated-study group 40 percent. More exposure to the passage had produced stronger immediate performance but less durable recall in this comparison. [LP-W022]

Students’ expectations pointed in the other direction. Those who repeatedly studied were more confident that they would remember the material a week later. Repeated access to a readable passage could feel reassuring while a closed-text attempt exposed what was difficult to produce. The experiment thus joined a learning procedure to a problem of judgment: the experience that makes study feel successful need not identify the procedure that leaves more available later. [LP-W022]

Wang Zhixun and Zhang Jinkun’s 2017 review compares explanations of retrieval-practice effects. Additional exposure, effort during retrieval, elaboration and changes in episodic context make different claims about why attempting recall can benefit subsequent remembering. Their discussion moves from descriptions of an observed effect to processes that might produce it. In particular, a difficult attempt and a successful learning outcome do not by themselves identify the mechanism connecting them. [LP-W017]

The episodic-context account they discuss links an event with the context encoded when it was encountered. A later retrieval attempt uses present cues to reconstruct the earlier context. Successful retrieval then updates the representation so that elements of the old and new contexts can participate in subsequent remembering. On this account, repeated retrieval changes the cues and search conditions through which the material can later be found. [LP-W017]

Their review also examines how a retrieval cue can become more diagnostic: it can direct a search toward a target while excluding competing items. It compares this kind of account with explanations emphasizing semantic elaboration or the effort involved in retrieval. Wang and Zhang develop the integration of

encoding and retrieval as an explanatory direction. The review’s contribution is this comparison of proposed processes, beyond merely repeating the recommendation to test oneself. [LP-W017]

Returning after an interval#

In the selected experiments from Hermann Ebbinghaus’s 1885 work on memory, learning continued through returns to material on successive days. He learned series of syllables to the point of a first successful reproduction, waited twenty-four hours and relearned them to that criterion. The number of repetitions required on later days made the persistence of previous work observable even when the material could no longer simply be reproduced at once. [LP-W027]

The chapter considers series of different lengths and stanzas from Don Juan. These materials posed different learning demands, but the repeated procedure made later savings comparable with earlier effort for the material being learned. Each new occasion began with whatever remained from the previous occasions and continued until the specified reproduction criterion was reached. Learning was being examined as a history of returns, not only as the progress made within one sitting. [LP-W027]

The number of repetitions needed fell over successive days. Ebbinghaus examined the relation between repetitions performed and later savings, while keeping the outcome criterion stable. His self-experimental procedure made the effort of relearning a measure of persistence. It could reveal retained effects of earlier work that a simple present-versus-absent memory judgment would miss. [LP-W027]

He also distinguished the observed series from an estimate of the isolated contribution of a particular day’s repetitions. To calculate that contribution, one would need to know how much relearning would have been required had those repetitions not occurred. Subtracting consecutive daily totals would not supply that missing comparison. Ebbinghaus set aside uncertain assumptions about it and reported the relations actually observed. [LP-W027]

Nicholas Cepeda, Edward Vul, Doug Rohrer, John Wixted and Harold Pashler’s 2008 study varied two intervals separately: the gap between learning occasions and the later delay before the final test. Its 1,354 participants contributed to twenty-six combinations of study gap and test delay, using learned facts. A spacing schedule was therefore evaluated against a specified future occasion for remembering. [LP-W023]

The procedure can be followed on two clocks. Learn the facts, wait the selected gap and study them again; then wait the separately selected retention interval before testing what can be recalled. Altering the first interval changes when relearning occurs. Altering the second changes how long that learning must remain useful. The design examines their relationship rather than treating all delayed tests as equivalent. [LP-W023]

The best-performing gap changed with the intended retention interval. Cepeda and colleagues described the resulting surface as a temporal ridgeline: effective spacing depended on the horizon over which memory was being assessed. Their empirical account provides a way to investigate a scheduling decision that a simple instruction to spread practice out leaves unspecified. [LP-W023]

Relearning to a criterion#

Katherine Rawson and John Dunlosky’s 2012 review asks when practice testing can support learning that is both durable and efficient. Its question includes the time or effort needed to obtain a later result. Correct recall during an initial session is one possible stopping criterion, but it leaves open what should happen at later encounters with the same material. The authors examine a schedule that combines initial retrieval with returns to successful recall on subsequent occasions. [LP-W029]

This changes the unit in which practice is planned. Instead of deciding only how many additional repetitions to perform before ending the first session, the learner plans several sessions in which the material must again be produced correctly. The review’s accounts connect initial learning, later relearning and the final retention measure. Durability and efficiency are evaluated together, so a schedule can be considered in relation to both what is remembered and what it took to establish that memory. [LP-W029]

Julian Roelle and Tobias Richter’s 2025 review develops successive relearning as the combination of retrieval practice with distributed returns to a mastery criterion. Their example begins with an explanation of a biological mechanism learned until it can be given correctly. The learner then practises the explanation again after two, nine and sixteen days, each time relearning until correct. The return is an attempt to reconstruct the explanation, followed by whatever additional learning is needed to meet the criterion. [LP-W016]

A mathematical procedure can be organized similarly. Having applied it successfully in the first learning phase, the learner returns on several later occasions and continues until successful application is again possible. These examples distinguish a return to a criterion from a return for a fixed number of minutes. The later session responds to what the learner can then produce. [LP-W016]

Roelle and Richter also distinguish learning to recognize similarities within a category from learning to discriminate between categories. Blocking can keep related examples together; interleaving can put contrasting examples close enough to compare. Their review uses these distinctions to explain why retrieval, spacing, successive relearning and interleaving are separate design choices, even when more than one occurs within a learning programme. [LP-W016]

Mixing what must be distinguished#

Nate Kornell and Robert Bjork’s 2008 experiments asked participants to learn painters’ styles from examples. In blocked study, examples by the same artist appeared together. In interleaved study, paintings by different artists were mixed. A later test used previously unseen paintings and asked who had painted them. Success required using what had been learned about a style to classify a new instance. [LP-W024]

Interleaving improved classification in the reported comparisons. Yet participants tended to judge blocked study more effective. Seeing several works by one artist in succession could make the style seem easier to grasp while that sequence was present. The later task required distinguishing among possible artists, which made comparisons across styles important. The authors discussed improved discrimination as an explanation for the interleaving benefit. [LP-W024]

The manipulation also clarifies a difference between interleaving and temporal spacing. Alternating artists increases the interval between examples by one artist, but it fills that interval with potentially informative examples from others. Kornell and Bjork explicitly distinguish their arrangement from simply waiting before showing another example of the same category. Both the time between related examples and the material placed between them belong to the procedure. [LP-W024]

Stanisław Czyż, Aleksandra Wójcik and Petra Solarská’s 2024 review concerns contextual interference and motor transfer. It included forty-two studies, with thirty-four entering its quantitative analysis of delayed transfer. The review separated that outcome from immediate transfer and examined blocked and random practice in relation to performance on a changed task after an interval. Its average result favored random practice, with differences across settings and age groups. [LP-W032]

The review gives a broader empirical setting for motor scheduling experiments such as those in S71. It keeps transfer delay, the transfer task and the practice setting within the comparison, rather than pooling every later score as the same kind of result. Laboratory and applied conditions can organize substantially

different learning situations. The authors’ synthesis makes those distinctions part of interpreting the estimated effect. [LP-W032]

Problem solving and productive failure#

Manu Kapur’s 2008 study placed 309 eleventh-grade students in three-person groups working on Newtonian-kinematics problems in a computer-supported collaborative setting. The students had already completed the relevant curricular unit. Groups were assigned more or less structured problems. Car-accident scenarios supplied situations requiring the students to interpret events, propose approaches and assess their attempts. The manipulation concerned problem structuredness after that prior instruction. [LP-W025]

The ill-structured groups struggled during their collaborative work, but the article reported better later individual near and far transfer. Their discussions provided occasions to generate and examine approaches without following the same well-specified path through the problem. Kapur investigated whether the apparently unsuccessful group activity could prepare resources for a later individual task. Initial solution quality and subsequent learning were deliberately examined at different points. [LP-W025]

The term productive failure gives that difference a practical force. A group’s difficulty in reaching a satisfactory answer need not exhaust what its members have gained from trying. In this study, that argument is tied to the structure of the problems, the prior curriculum and the later tests. It concerns students working on physical problems; S31 develops separate histories of how organizations identify and learn from failures. [LP-W025]

Tanmay Sinha and Kapur’s 2021 meta-analysis examines another explicit comparison: problem solving followed by instruction, against instruction followed by problem solving. The review included fifty-three studies and 166 comparisons. Its overall result favored problem solving first, and it examined how closely interventions followed productive-failure design principles. The sequence includes subsequent instruction as part of the learning arrangement. [LP-W026]

In this research family, learners’ generated approaches can supply material on which instruction builds. Comparing an incomplete or unsuccessful attempt with a developed solution can make relevant features and distinctions available for discussion. The instructional phase is consequently more than a reward supplied after struggle: its connection to the preceding attempts is part of the proposed design. Sinha and Kapur examine implementation along with conceptual knowledge and transfer outcomes. [LP-W026]

Sinha and Kapur also report comparisons favoring instruction first, particularly among younger learners in second through fifth grades and in work on domain-general skills. Their analysis considers grade level, intervention duration and the experimental or quasi-experimental design alongside fidelity to productive-failure principles. It develops the question of when a sequence works, including how instruction makes use of the attempts that precede it. [LP-W026]

Learning techniques in classrooms#

John Dunlosky, Katherine Rawson, Elizabeth Marsh, Mitchell Nathan and Daniel Willingham’s 2013 review evaluates ten learning techniques through several dimensions: learning materials, conditions of learning, learner characteristics and the tasks used to assess the result. A technique’s utility is considered in relation to the breadth of evidence and the situations in which it can be used. This supplies an evaluation framework as well as findings about particular techniques. [LP-W020]

Practice testing and distributed practice receive high utility assessments in that review. Testing includes low-stakes attempts to retrieve information, including self-testing; it need not mean another

consequential examination. Distributed practice concerns how learning opportunities are placed over time. The review’s practical question is how these activities can be incorporated into studying and teaching, with evidence about retention beyond the initial encounter. [LP-W020]

Its interleaving discussion also concerns selecting what to do. A blocked run of one kind of problem can repeatedly cue the same procedure. A mixed set may require identifying the kind of problem before choosing a method. Thus changing the order can change the operation practised, even when the set contains the same problems. The review develops this explanation alongside the state of the evidence, while S70 examines the distinct content and outcomes of training in creative methods. [LP-W020]

Sterre Ruitenburg, Mieke Goos, Guus Lambert, Paul Kirschner, Halszka Jarodzka and Gino Camp’s 2026 classroom meta-analysis examines retrieval practice, distributed practice and interleaving in school settings. It includes fifty-three studies, 233 effect sizes and 7,303 participants. The reported average effects favor the techniques in the selected comparisons. Its analysis also retains information about the alternative activity against which a technique was compared. [LP-W033]

For spacing, a comparison with massed practice differs from a comparison with practice that is already distributed to some degree. The ratio of study interval to retention interval and the form of the later test further distinguish the reported outcomes. For interleaving, the analysis examines whether feedback is immediate, delayed or absent. These are concrete differences in classroom arrangements, rather than interchangeable instances of a technique’s name. [LP-W033]

The classroom review brings the scheduling questions back to activities conducted within teaching. A pupil can be asked to retrieve, to return after an interval or to choose among different problems, while the teacher also determines feedback and assessment. Its findings develop the study of these arrangements in their actual instructional settings. They extend a history in which what is practised, when it is practised and what later performance should show remain central questions. [LP-W033]

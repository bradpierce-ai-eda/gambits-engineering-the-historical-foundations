S52 Generating alternatives and organizing exploration #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

Engineering often requires discovering a possible response before its merits can be assessed. The work includes recognizing a problem differently, producing genuinely different candidates, testing them and deciding when to commit. The sources in this section examine how examples, discussion, representations, feedback and organizational learning affect that work. Their procedures range from short individual design tasks to the allocation of resources across an organization.

Several neighboring accounts address different parts of the problem. Alexander's treatment in S08 preserves the inventive work of general design; TRIZ and facilitated problem-structuring methods appear in S44. Ashby's selection and regulatory variety are developed in S03. Baldwin's modular options in S07 and the flexibility accounts in S34 concern retaining choices that can be exercised later. Here the emphasis is on how alternatives arise and how exploration is organized before a choice becomes settled.

Jansson and Smith: an example can constrain designs without reducing their number#

David Jansson and Steven Smith's 1991 experiments examine design fixation: adherence to a limited set of ideas that constrains conceptual design. They ask whether an example supplied with a task can restrict the designs people produce, even when they have been asked for many possible solutions. Their procedure compares participants who receive a problem alone with participants who receive the same problem and an illustrative design. Similarity to selected features of that example supplies a way to observe restriction that a simple count of sketches might miss. [OI-W015, pp. 3–4]

In the bicycle-rack experiment, 25 senior mechanical-engineering students were randomly divided between these conditions. The task called for a rack that would be easy to mount, avoid damage and accommodate different bicycles and cars. The example showed a roof-mounted design with suction cups and rails supporting the tires. Students who saw it produced approximately the same number of designs as the control group, but used its characteristic features more often. Suction cups appeared in 54 percent of their designs, compared with 6 percent in the control condition. The quantity of output concealed a substantial difference in the kinds of solution considered. [OI-W015, pp. 5–6, table 1]

Other tasks introduced clearly undesirable features. In the measuring-device task, the example failed to offer continuous adjustment or protection against overflow, both pertinent to the requirements. In a spill-proof cup task, the sample used a straw and mouthpiece despite instructions excluding them, and its construction could leak. The groups shown examples again reproduced more of their features, including defects. The cup experiment also assessed flexibility across approaches and originality relative to other participants' designs; both measures were lower in the example condition. Merely asking for multiple designs did not ensure that the underlying approach changed. [OI-W015, pp. 6–9, tables 2–3]

The fourth experiment involved thirteen professional engineers and a device for taking measurements and samples along the intestinal tract. The example used a cable and control box, with an opening at the front of the device. Engineers exposed to it incorporated those features more often than the comparison group. This small professional sample extended the investigation beyond students, while remaining a brief task with a deliberately limited problem. [OI-W015, pp. 9–10, table 4]

Jansson and Smith retain a difficulty important to the interpretation of their experiment: prior knowledge is indispensable to design. Learning from an earlier design and becoming unable to consider a relevant alternative are different things. They ask about useful features, long-term experience and more complex tasks, leaving the conditions of beneficial and harmful influence open. The work demonstrates a barrier under specified conditions and provides a method for examining it; the discussion does not supply a validated general procedure for eliminating it. [OI-W015, p. 10]

Ritchey: organize combinations through dimensions and consistency judgments#

Tom Ritchey's account of general morphological analysis begins by identifying dimensions of a problem and possible conditions within each dimension. A configuration combines one condition from each. The dimensions can concern technical properties, financial arrangements, policy aims or ethical commitments, so the representation does not require every difference to be expressed as a numerical quantity. The first work is therefore defining what matters and specifying meaningful alternatives within each dimension. The account first appeared in 2002 and was revised in 2013. [OI-W014, pp. 1–4]

The resulting morphological field can contain many combinations that nobody would have listed unaided. In Ritchey's reduced teaching example, six dimensions contain four, four, four, three, three and four conditions, producing 2,304 formal configurations. This is a count of combinations inside the defined field. It does not mean that 2,304 policies were implemented, evaluated or found workable. Defining a field makes a particular range of possibilities explicit while retaining the judgment that selected its dimensions and conditions. [OI-W014, pp. 4–5]

Cross-consistency assessment reduces that range. Participants consider pairs of conditions from different dimensions and judge whether they can coexist. A configuration containing a rejected pair can then be excluded. The procedure records the judgments used to obtain the remaining configurations, allowing later inspection of how the result arose. Ritchey distinguishes logical contradiction, empirical incompatibility and normative objections, arguing that judgments about what is possible should not be silently collapsed into judgments about what is desirable. The assessments still require people with relevant knowledge and the ability to make disagreements explicit. [OI-W014, pp. 5–7]

His shelter-policy example concerns Sweden's reconsideration of a civil-defense system after the Cold War. The field includes geographical priorities, functional priorities, crowding, new construction, maintenance and the general philosophy governing provision. A policy of equal shelter quality for everyone conflicts, in the illustrated assessment, with selectively prioritizing some geographical areas. Selecting a condition as an input lets participants inspect which other conditions remain consistent with it. The representation supports investigation of the implications of a commitment, as well as the discovery of previously unlisted combinations. [OI-W014, pp. 4–7, figs. 2–5]

The method's operative result is a set of configurations consistent with recorded judgments. It does not simulate their consequences or resolve political choices through arithmetic. Software helps manage combinations and display the implications of selected conditions, while the definition of the problem and the consistency assessments remain substantive work. Ritchey's claims about traceability and the value of the method are methodological arguments illustrated through applications. They do not amount to a controlled comparison showing that the resulting policies outperform alternatives. [OI-W014, pp. 6–8]

Diehl and Stroebe: giving everyone a chance to speak is a production constraint#

Michael Diehl and Wolfgang Stroebe's 1987 study investigates why interacting brainstorming groups can produce fewer ideas than an equivalent number of people working independently. Their comparison is between an actual group and a nominal group constructed by pooling independent contributions and removing duplicates. A four-person nominal group's output is not the output of one person. Holding the session length constant gives both arrangements the same total person-time, making the organization of interaction a possible explanation for the difference. [OI-W012, pp. 497–501]

Four experiments examine proposed explanations including reduced effort when assessment is collective, fear of evaluation and production blocking. Blocking occurs when one person's opportunity to express an idea depends on somebody else finishing. The first experiments varied assessment and evaluation conditions. These changes affected productivity, but did not remove the large difference between actual

and nominal groups. The findings therefore required an explanation beyond attributing all losses to unwillingness to contribute or fear of criticism. [OI-W012, pp. 499–505]

The fourth experiment made the turn-taking restriction explicit. People in separate rooms used microphones and a light display indicating whether another person was speaking. In blocking conditions, they were to wait until the lights permitted them to speak. One condition retained the restriction while preventing participants from hearing others' ideas. Another allowed participants to ignore the lights and speak whenever they had an idea. Individual assessment was retained across the conditions. This arrangement separated the opportunity to speak from exposure to another person's contribution. [OI-W012, pp. 505–506]

The restricted conditions produced fewer ideas even when participants could not hear one another. Removing the restriction brought output close to the independent control. In that experiment, the mean pooled output of four people working independently was 106 nonredundant ideas, compared with about 56 for an actual four-person group. The result supports production blocking as an important mechanism in those short sessions. It does not by itself identify whether a waiting person's difficulty arose from memory, disrupted development of an idea, motivation or some combination of processes. [OI-W012, pp. 506–508, table 5]

The resources in the comparison matter. Giving an interacting group several times longer would increase its total person-time. Moreover, idea counts, the number of good ideas and average quality are different measures; the later experiments concentrated on counts. The authors suggest independent generation followed by discussion and evaluation, but that combined procedure was not the treatment tested in these experiments. Their participants and brief prompts also differ from an established expert team working over months. The paper identifies a cost of organizing generation around one shared speaking channel and supplies controlled ways to examine it. [OI-W012, pp. 501, 507–508]

Nemeth and colleagues: criticism and generation need not be separate stages#

Charlan Nemeth, Marie Personnaz, Bernard Personnaz and Jack Goncalo's 2003 working paper tests an instruction frequently associated with brainstorming: participants should avoid criticizing one another's ideas. The authors contrast it with instructions encouraging debate and criticism, and with a minimal condition giving only the task. Their question is whether the attempt to remove evaluation can also remove an interaction that stimulates new thinking. The manipulated object is the instruction governing a discussion, rather than whether every participant agrees with the others. [OI-W008, printed pp. 10–12]

Groups worked on reducing traffic congestion, with one participant recording ideas while the others discussed them. The discussion lasted twenty minutes. Afterward, people separately recorded ideas they had considered but not expressed and new ideas occurring after the discussion. This extended the observation beyond the public record of the meeting. An idea absent from that record could have been withheld, crowded out or developed only afterward; the two individual questions attempted to distinguish those possibilities in the participants' reports. [OI-W008, printed pp. 11–14]

The study used samples in Berkeley and Paris. Debate instructions produced more group ideas than minimal instructions in each sample. A direct comparison between debate and conventional brainstorming instructions was less decisive for ideas spoken during the session. In the combined analysis, debate was superior on the authors' total-production measure, which combined a share of the group output with the individual's subsequent contributions. The pattern therefore depends on which output is counted. It is stronger evidence for attending to discussion and its aftermath than for claiming that every instance of criticism improves every measure of creativity. [OI-W008, printed pp. 13–19]

The authors interpret their findings through the stimulating effects of dissent and competing views. Their procedure encouraged criticism of ideas within a short task; it did not manipulate retaliation by a

superior or the consequences of challenging an established organizational commitment. This makes its relevance different from the silence and voice accounts in S51. Its substantive contribution concerns how the terms of discussion can affect the production of ideas, including ideas that become available after the discussion has ended. [OI-W008, printed pp. 17–19]

Dow and colleagues: make several prototypes before receiving feedback#

Steven Dow and colleagues' 2010 journal article compares two ways of distributing the same amount of prototyping and critique. In the serial condition, a designer made one prototype, received feedback and continued to the next. In the parallel condition, a designer first made three alternatives and received feedback on them, then made two more and received another round of feedback. Both conditions produced five prototypes followed by a final design, with equal creation time and the same amount of descriptive critique. Parallel here means developing several alternatives before feedback; it does not mean assigning different prototypes to a team of workers. [OI-W013, pp. 18:5–18:10]

Thirty-three participants completed the task of designing graphic advertisements for the same client. They were nonprofessional designers using a common unfamiliar tool, with assignment balanced for prior experience and gender. The final advertisements were assessed through a live online campaign and by independent client and professional judges. Other raters assessed similarity among each participant's designs. The study thus separated several outcomes: response to the advertisement, judged quality, variation among prototypes and the designer's confidence in performing the task. [OI-W013, pp. 18:5– 18:13]

The parallel condition performed better on click-through rate and expert ratings. Exposure must be included in reading the campaign results because the advertising system changed how often advertisements appeared. During the first five days, when aggregate exposure was approximately balanced, the parallel advertisements received 44 clicks from 79,800 impressions and the serial advertisements 26 clicks from 79,658 impressions. The paper also reports higher expert ratings and greater design diversity in the parallel condition. The live response and the expert ratings were distinct criteria and did not simply reproduce one ranking. [OI-W013, pp. 18:11–18:14]

The authors propose several explanations. Seeing alternatives can make the differences among criticisms easier to understand, while attachment to any one prototype may be reduced when several are available. Serial refinement can instead preserve the assumptions of an early design through successive improvements. The experimental comparison changed the schedule of work and feedback as a package. It did not independently establish the causal contribution of each proposed psychological mechanism. Nor did it show that greater diversity necessarily produced higher quality: more experienced participants could produce less diverse work while achieving better results. [OI-W013, pp. 18:14–18:18]

The task was short, inexpensive and allowed consequences to be measured relatively quickly. The authors discuss the need to investigate longer design processes and teams. Maintaining several expensive prototypes could allocate effort differently from the banner-ad task, and the experimenter administering the conditions was not blind to assignment. The equal time and prototype count nevertheless make the demonstrated comparison informative: the observed advantage cannot simply be attributed to the parallel condition receiving more opportunities to build or more feedback. [OI-W013, pp. 18:18–18:19]

Nutt: searching for a result rather than defending a ready-made solution#

Paul Nutt's 1999 article examines how organizations establish a direction, identify possible responses and implement a decision. Its cases distinguish defining a desired result from beginning with a solution that somebody already favors. His heat-pump example makes the distinction concrete. A firm spent years trying to make a particular heat-wheel technology work, although its broader opportunity concerned

economical heating. Once that solution supplied the definition of the problem, technical effort went into repairing it rather than examining other ways to obtain the desired result. The example concerns how a commitment organizes subsequent search. [OI-W009, pp. 78–81]

Nutt distinguishes several ways of finding options. Copying a single comparison organization imports a package that may fit a different setting. Integrated benchmarking instead examines several organizations and combines pertinent features. A search can also occur in stages: an initial request for proposals teaches the buyer what suppliers can offer, and a later request uses that knowledge to specify a more informed demand. Designing a custom option is another route, requiring design skill, attention to users and the capacity to put the result into operation. His categories describe different work, rather than identifying all search with asking for a longer list. [OI-W009, pp. 81–85]

The underlying evidence is a retrospectively reconstructed collection of decisions in medium and large organizations in the United States and Canada. Interviews and documents were used to establish the process, with subsequent checks of implementation. Success was assessed through such measures as sustained use and full use after two years. Those measures distinguish adoption from abandonment or partial use; they do not by themselves establish profitability, public benefit or the absence of harmful effects. Managers were not randomly assigned to search methods. [OI-W009, pp. 76–77, 89, note 5]

Nutt associates the development of multiple options with more successful implementation and argues that discovering alternatives can reduce later repair work. The multiple-search category contained nine cases, all sustained and fully used after two years. Additional search requires time, access to comparison cases and the ability to combine what is learned. The article also treats participation and implementation separately, so discovering an attractive response does not complete the task of securing its acceptance and use. [OI-W009, pp. 83–89]

March: learning can consume the differences from which it learns#

James March's 1991 article examines exploration and exploitation in organizational learning. Exploration includes experimentation with new alternatives, while exploitation develops and uses established capabilities. Both consume resources, but their returns can arrive at different times and benefit different people or parts of an organization. Procedures for setting targets, rewarding work, allocating spare capacity and choosing investments therefore affect the balance even when nobody explicitly makes a decision called exploration versus exploitation. [OI-W010, p. 71]

March explains why ordinary adaptation can favor existing activities. Greater competence produces more reliable rewards, which make that activity more attractive and support further competence. A less familiar alternative can perform poorly while people learn how to use it, even if its potential is greater. Feedback about established work is often faster, clearer and closer to the people doing it than feedback about exploration. A process that improves short-term performance can consequently narrow the experience from which later possibilities would be discovered. This is the argument developed through the paper's models. [OI-W010, p. 73]

In the mutual-learning model, individuals hold beliefs about an external reality and an organizational code holds accepted beliefs. Individuals can adopt the code's beliefs; the code can learn from a group of individuals stipulated by the model to be more accurate than itself. The model specifies reality so that accuracy can be measured. Individuals do not directly experience that reality or teach one another outside the code. Those simplifications make it possible to study how two learning processes interact, with rates of socialization and code revision treated separately. [OI-W010, pp. 74–75]

Rapid socialization removes differences between individuals and the code. That can improve an individual's knowledge in the short term while reducing the variation from which the code can learn. In the simulations, slower socialization can preserve useful differences long enough for the organization to

absorb them. Mixtures of fast and slow learners can also improve the eventual code relative to a population with a uniform learning rate. The benefits are distributed unevenly: fast learners can acquire knowledge whose development depended disproportionately on people who remained different longer. [OI-W010, pp. 75–77]

Turnover provides another source of variation in the model. A newcomer has less knowledge on average but may hold beliefs that are less redundant with the code. Moderate turnover can therefore improve the code under some learning-rate conditions, while excessive turnover or turnover combined with already slow socialization can undermine the exploitation of what has been learned. When the external reality changes, a population that has fully converged can cease to adapt. Introducing newcomers can prevent that degeneration in the modeled system. [OI-W010, pp. 78–80]

March also distinguishes improvement in average performance from competition to finish first. Reducing variability can make outcomes more reliable while reducing the chance of an unusually good result at the upper end of a distribution. Whether that trade-off matters depends on the competitive objective and the distributions being compared. He uses it to question the assumption that more reliable knowledge necessarily improves every kind of competitive position. The discussion is a model-based argument about the conditions of performance, not an observed comparison of a particular firm's management practices. [OI-W010, pp. 84–85]

The article does not determine one optimal allocation between exploration and exploitation for every organization. Its models explain particular ways that learning rates, recruitment, time horizons and competitive criteria can change the consequences of an allocation. Too much exploration can leave ideas undeveloped and competence insufficient; too little can leave an organization highly practiced at an inferior activity. The work makes those choices and their distribution of costs explicit. It extends the discussion beyond generating candidates during one decision to sustaining the capacity to encounter and develop alternatives over time. [OI-W010, pp. 71, 73, 85]

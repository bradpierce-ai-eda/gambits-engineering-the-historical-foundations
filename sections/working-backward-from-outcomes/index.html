A proposition to be proved, a future to be examined and a performance to be demonstrated each give planning a possible starting point. The following accounts show how authors derive intermediate requirements, construct pathways and decide what evidence would establish an intended result.

S62 examines reverse business calculations and staged assumptions; S85 explains backward calculations in project scheduling.

Starting with the proposition to be established#

Stephanie Dick’s 2015 history follows mathematical heuristics into the construction of the Logic Theory Machine. The program began with a proposition to be proved and sought subproblems from which it could be deduced. It continued generating such subproblems until it could connect the chain to axioms; reversing a successful chain supplied the proof. A search could also fail to find the connection. Dick places Pólya’s working-backward heuristic within this history alongside other aids to discovery. Implementing the procedure on JOHNNIAC required representations and operations adapted to the machine. Her account makes the intended result an active part of the search: it determines which intermediate propositions would be useful. [PM-W001]

Regressing requirements and protecting goals#

Richard Waldinger’s Achieving Several Goals Simultaneously, issued as a technical report in 1975, addresses plans in which satisfying one goal can interfere with another. His procedure first constructs a plan for one goal, then modifies it to achieve the second while preserving required relations. Regression passes a desired relation backward across an action and asks what must have been true before the action for that relation to hold afterward. For an assignment X receives t, the desired relation P(X) becomes P(t) before the assignment. The substitution supplies a requirement that can be examined earlier in the plan. [PM-W002]

The three-block example shows why this operation matters. The target tower has A on B and B on C. Starting with separate blocks, the planner first puts A on B. A one-block-at-a-time arm cannot then put B on C while maintaining that arrangement. Passing the requirement that B be on C backward across the first action permits the planner to put B on C before placing A on B. Protection points identify relations that must hold at particular positions. Skeleton models retain selected facts and use regression to infer others as the plan changes. The action descriptions and protected relations make the modification inspectable. [PM-W002]

Backcasting, participation and the choice of a future#

Jaco Quist and Philip Vergragt’s 2006 account traces energy backcasting as an alternative to extrapolative forecasting and follows its extension into participatory sustainability work. Their proposed framework includes problem orientation, future visions, backcasting, elaboration of follow-up agendas, and embedding and implementation. Participation and iteration can change across these stages. In their historical reconstruction, Robinson’s earlier policy-analysis purpose and later stakeholder-oriented applications give backcasting different practical settings. [PM-W003]

Their SusHouse food-centre scenario illustrates how a vision enters further assessment. A neighbourhood centre for food preparation appeared attractive, but calculations using contemporary restaurant energy data suggested that energy demand might increase. The authors called for better data, and the workshop scenarios underwent environmental, economic and consumer assessment. The desirable image initiated

questions that subsequent investigation could alter. Their two food cases also had different follow-up histories: producing a vision and embedding subsequent activity were distinguishable achievements. [PM-W003]

Emilie Mutombo, Tom Bauler and Grégoire Wallenborn’s 2007 comparative report separates constructing a final image from constructing an evolution toward it. In their analytical grid, putting the final image first gives a backcasting order; putting the evolution first gives a forecasting order. Objectives, measures and monitoring remain further activities, with actual exercises often combining or overlapping the simplified stages. [PM-W004]

The report also examines how a destination is chosen. A vague image can accommodate apparently compatible principles; specifying it more precisely exposes different priorities and values. In its World Water Vision account, polarized conceptions remained unresolved and produced two reports. The staff report nevertheless supported initiatives by actors who adopted its vision. Participation could thus produce material for action without establishing one shared destination. The authors’ sequencing distinctions sit alongside this practical history of agreement and disagreement. [PM-W004]

Sebastian Bollien’s 2021 institutional account begins with a concrete future image and asks which events must precede it. Its example is peace negotiation between hostile parties: the backward questions concern conditions and timing that would make talks possible. Examining those requirements can lead to revising the image or moving it in time. The destination participates in the inquiry and can change as its prerequisites are examined. [PM-W012]

David Bengston, Lynne Westphal and Michael Dockry’s 2020 Backcasting Wheel places a preferred future at the centre of a diagram. Dimensions of success lead outward to signposts, opportunities and obstacles, and concrete management actions. Participants score importance and timing, and subsequent analysis organizes the results into action plans. The outward movement reaches earlier enabling actions, whereas a Futures Wheel explores later consequences of a change. [PM-W013]

Their Forest Service case involved two units preparing to share leadership and a headquarters. Mixed groups developed signposts, obstacles, opportunities and actions, scored them, and assigned leaders to urgent tasks. Participants later described the exercise as useful. Turnover interrupted continuity, leaving the eventual effect on the reorganization unresolved. The case records how an aspiration was translated into actions and assignments within a participatory exercise. [PM-W013]

Yusuke Kishita, Mattias Höjer and Jaco Quist’s 2024 comparison distinguishes participatory, goal-oriented and scenario-design approaches to backcasting. Implementation and stakeholder engagement receive a different emphasis from constructing goal-fulfilling future images. Their comparison retains those differences while developing the authors’ own design-framework questions about when, which, how and what. Backcasting continues to encompass different objects of design and different arrangements for involving participants. [PM-W014]

A backward tree and a forward storyline#

Haruna Wada and colleagues’ 2013 scenario-construction method starts with a target and builds a logic tree of events and actions that could lead to it. Key events divide the material into subscenarios. The writers then develop storylines forward from the present and turn them into scenario text. References and logical jumps are represented explicitly. [PM-W005]

Their worked target is an undesirable collapse of manufacturing in 2050. The tree separates inability to produce from inability to sell. When the resource branch is developed forward, substitution of aluminium for copper wiring introduces a possibility absent from the initial backward tree, one that could interrupt the collapse story. Backward tree construction and forward elaboration therefore perform different work

in the authors’ procedure. Selecting key events and judging the tree’s adequacy remain part of scenario construction; the paper discusses AND/OR operators as a subsequent improvement. [PM-W005]

Designing evidence of learning#

Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe’s Understanding by Design (1998) orders curriculum decisions around desired learning, acceptable evidence and then instruction. Their hypothetical teacher Bob James wants fifth-grade students to use nutrition knowledge to plan balanced diets. He specifies a three-day camp menu and an analysis of a hypothetical family’s diet as performances through which students can demonstrate that understanding. Quizzes supply evidence about prerequisite knowledge. [PM-W006]

These choices guide the teaching resources and activities: interpreting nutrition labels, scaling recipes, consulting a visiting nutritionist, and using a brochure, video and selected textbook passages. Assessment includes evidence gathered during learning through observations, dialogue and other tasks. The example changes the textbook’s role from an obligatory sequence to one resource among several. The authors acknowledge Tyler’s earlier objectives-based logic and supply filters, templates and design questions for putting such decisions into practice. [PM-W006]

Wiggins and McTighe’s 2011 guide distinguishes acquiring knowledge, making meaning and transferring learning. Evidence is chosen in relation to the intended result. Using knowledge independently in a new situation calls for a different demonstration from recalling facts. Their nutrition materials sequence analysis of a hypothetical family’s diet, feedback on misunderstandings and independent preparation of a camp menu. Instruction and practice prepare that performance. The GRASPS prompts make task design explicit through goal, role, audience, situation, product or performance, and standards. These are tools for designing a unit and inspecting its coherence. [PM-W007]

Wang Lingling’s 2023 account applies backward unit design to fifth-grade texts about Venice, Dutch pastures and the pyramids. Intended learning is expressed through performances such as explaining a place and preparing a travel guide. In a proposed tour-guide competition, groups choose locations, develop guide materials and presentations, and are judged on features of content and delivery. Further assessment concerns participation and cooperation. Wang’s exposition constructs the performance activity before separately discussing assessment evidence. The resulting account makes the product, audience and criteria visible within her formulation of unit teaching. [PM-W008]

Planning direction and predictions of completion#

Jessica Wiese, Roger Buehler and Dale Griffin’s 2016 experiments distinguish hours spent working from the calendar date on which a task is completed. Their four studies compare backward, forward and unspecified planning directions. In the fourth, adults nominated a multiday project with a deadline within two weeks, planned its steps and predicted when they would start and finish and how many work hours they would need. A follow-up asked what had happened. [PM-W009]

The initial analysed sample contained 187 participants; 125 reported completed projects and supplied the completion-bias analysis. Backward planners showed less average optimistic bias in predicted completion dates. The intervention shifted predictions without a corresponding reduction in actual completion time. Predicted work hours and starting-time bias had different results. The authors discuss attention to obstacles, additional steps and planning insights as possible processes, with variable support across their studies. [PM-W009]

Jooyoung Park, Fang-Chi Lu and William Hedgcock’s 2017 paper reports comparisons of chronological and reverse-chronological planning for personal goals, treating motivation, expectancy, perceived pressure and performance as distinct outcomes. Their 2018 corrigendum revises effect sizes, intervals

and portions of Study 3’s statistical reporting while stating that the interpretations stand; some corrected comparisons are marginal or nonsignificant. Their reported goal-pursuit findings concern different outcomes from Wiese and colleagues’ completion-time predictions. [PM-W010] [PM-W029]

Transforming computational planning problems#

Allen Ren, Brian Ichter and Anirudha Majumdar’s 2024 computational study examines graph paths, array transformations and block manipulation. A narrow passage near a goal can make searching from that end attractive. Simply asking their model to plan backward, however, performs worse. They instead flip the problem, plan forward in the transformed representation and convert the solution back. Directed edges must be reversed, and actions must have usable inverses for the relevant transformation. [PM-W011]

The experiments generate candidate plans, use model self-verification and apply an external task verifier. Combining ordinary and flipped planning improves success in their tested settings. Transforming the problem and checking the returned plan are substantial parts of the procedure. [PM-W011]

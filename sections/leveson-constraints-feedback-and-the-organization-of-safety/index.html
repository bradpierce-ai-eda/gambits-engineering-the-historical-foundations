The connected safety account#

Nancy Leveson's Engineering a Safer World connects physical design, automated and human control, organizational responsibilities, regulation and change throughout a system's life. Its relevant contribution is an extensive account of preserving acceptable behavior while arranging the information, action and support that preservation requires. The connection is the author's applied synthesis. [UX-W021, 2012]

Leveson develops the framework in Engineering a Safer World, her 2004 accident-model article and a related 2009 formulation. [UX-W021; UX-W020; SB-W017]

Reliable components can participate in unsafe interactions#

The book distinguishes component reliability from system safety. A component can perform its specified function while its interaction with others violates a system constraint. A specification can also be wrong for the surrounding physical situation. Searching only for a failed part can therefore miss the conditions that made an otherwise expected action hazardous. [UX-W021, chs. 1–3]

An event sequence records what happened but can leave out what should have happened, why a control was missing, or which organizational process failed to maintain an assumption. Leveson's proposed account starts with constraints on acceptable behavior and asks how they are established and preserved. Physical barriers, software requirements, operating procedures, management and regulation can each contribute. [UX-W021, chs. 2–4]

The selected chemical-reactor example in the earlier article makes the interaction concrete. Cooling must be available when catalyst flow begins. Software following a locally stated instruction can still be unsafe if the instruction omits the relation between those operations. Executing the code as written is different from satisfying the necessary system-level condition. The example is Leveson's reconstruction. [UX-W020, 7–15]

Safety is also relative to losses judged unacceptable and to the environment and use considered. Several constraints can conflict. A train door, for example, should ordinarily remain closed while moving yet permit evacuation under different conditions. A useful safety account has to state those conditions and competing obligations. The vocabulary of constraints does not make stakeholder judgment unnecessary. [UX-W021, chs. 1, 6–9]

Actual feedback differs from evidence that a command was issued#

The control account distinguishes goals, action capacity, information about the controlled process and the assumptions through which a controller interprets that information. A command can be delayed, fail to execute or achieve only part of its intended effect. Information that power was applied to a valve is not proof that the valve reached its required position. The feedback must concern what the relevant constraint actually requires. [UX-W020, 13–16; UX-W021, ch. 4]

Direct physical coupling can supply information that a remote interface must reconstruct. Vibration, sound, temperature and the behavior of a mechanically connected gauge can reveal both the process and whether the display remains connected to it. Moving control to a remote electronic interface can improve capability while removing those incidental cues. Designers then have to decide what to represent and how the operator will obtain it under abnormal as well as routine conditions. [UX-W020, 14–15]

Delays occur in actuators, process response, measurement and communication. A controller's interpretation can be internally consistent but late or wrong about the actual state. The book's process-model analysis asks what each participant believes or assumes and how feedback changes that account. [UX-W021, chs. 4–5]

Leveson invokes Conant and Ashby to state a general requirement for controllers to contain a process model. The practical method can make hidden assumptions visible, but the universal inference needs the qualifications attached to the original result: its optimal-response premises do not establish that every successful passive constraint contains a rich internal representation. An observer's model of a controller is also different from a stored representation inside it. The original arguments are treated separately in the Ashby section. [UX-W020, 15–16; UX-W021, ch. 4]

Responsibilities can fall into gaps or overlap unsafely#

The development and operating control structures relate organizations as well as physical devices. Assumptions, requirements, reports, maintenance findings and changes must cross their interfaces. A nominal chart can omit who actually supplies or receives relevant information. Conversely, two controllers can each assume that the other has responsibility, or act incompatibly on the same process. [UX-W021, chs. 4–5; UX-W020, 17–25]

Overlap is therefore not always a remedy. The book's boundary-responsibility examples ask which controller handles the case at the edge of two domains and how their actions are coordinated. Removing an overlap can also leave a gap. The issue is the actual relation among responsibilities, knowledge, timing and action, rather than maximum integration or maximum separation in the abstract. [UX-W021, chs. 4, 8]

Asynchronous evolution names a further difficulty: one part changes while related parts retain assumptions about its earlier behavior. Software, equipment, procedures, training, contracts and regulation can change on different schedules. A previously acceptable arrangement can consequently become unsafe without one dramatic component breakdown. Maintenance of the relationships is part of safety work. [UX-W021, chs. 4, 12; UX-W020]

The friendly-fire chapter illustrates differences in local information, schedules, service expectations, liaison and interpretation. It reconstructs testimony and reports, preserving disagreements among sources. An unexplained identification-system failure prevents describing the whole event categorically as an accident without any component failure. The example's relevant contribution is how different local actions and understandings fit into a wider control structure. [UX-W021, ch. 5]

STPA begins with losses, hazards and a control structure#

Systems-Theoretic Process Analysis, STPA, first identifies the losses and hazards to be considered, relevant constraints and the control structure. Its four unsafe-control-action categories concern an action not supplied when needed, an action supplied unsafely, wrong timing or order, and an action stopped too soon or sustained too long. Timing and absence can therefore be analyzed alongside the occurrence of an explicit wrong action. [UX-W021, chs. 6–8]

The next task develops causal scenarios. Analysts examine controller assumptions, feedback, actuators, the controlled process, communications and environmental conditions that can lead to an unsafe action or prevent a necessary one from taking effect. The objective is to make design requirements and potential failures of their enforcement visible before relying on operation to reveal them. [UX-W021, ch. 8]

The analysis can begin early, with a coarse design, and be refined as decisions add detail. A new feature can remove one hazard while creating another. The model is revised with the design, rather than treated

as a document made once after the architecture has already been fixed. This gives analysis an active role in generating and comparing alternatives. [UX-W021, chs. 7–9]

Completeness remains bounded. Examining every listed control action does not prove that the list, the chosen boundary or the causal scenarios contain everything relevant in the real system. Some losses and interactions can have been omitted from the initial model. The book's comparisons with other analyses are author reports. [UX-W021, chs. 6–8]

A physical constraint can change the necessary response chain#

The high-energy door example starts with a requirement that opening the door must not expose a person to power. A design can detect door opening and command a separate interruption, requiring sensors, logic, action and timing. Alternatively, the physical circuit can pass through the door so opening it breaks that circuit. In the modeled example, the arrangement directly removes the identified exposure condition. Its physical integrity and other hazards still need consideration. [UX-W021, 224–225]

Adding an indicator offers another alternative: the operator can see whether power is present. That adds information but also a component that can fail and a display whose safe interpretation must be designed. The book explicitly says that each solution can introduce drawbacks and that the rationale for the choice should be recorded for later certification, reuse and maintenance. [UX-W021, 224–225]

The important distinction is between changing the set of possible physical interactions and asking a participant to detect and correct the same dangerous interaction. Either may be appropriate under particular obligations. Leveson's design process keeps the alternatives and their support requirements available for comparison, rather than identifying safety with a larger number of active checks. [UX-W021, chs. 8–9]

The robot example: a solution changes the problem#

The mobile robot's manipulator can destabilize its base. A sufficiently heavy base is a candidate physical solution, but added mass can worsen collision damage or impede manual movement in an emergency. A wider base can improve stability while preventing movement through doors and crowded spaces. These competing requirements constrain an apparently simple solution. [UX-W021, 257]

Deployable stabilizer legs offer another option. They must be extended before arm movement and retracted for travel. The change introduces additional hazards, synchronization requirements and feedback. An assurance that a motor was energized cannot replace evidence that the legs reached the required position; an obstruction could prevent full deployment. [UX-W021, 258–262]

The analysis can then change the decomposition. Combining the arm and leg controllers can reduce the communications needed to keep their assumptions consistent. This is an explicit example in which integration is considered to manage a strong coupling. [UX-W021, 261–262, figs. 9.3–9.5]

The example begins with a hazard, examines physical alternatives and environmental constraints, analyzes hazards introduced by a candidate solution, and revises the control architecture. Design and safety analysis inform one another through this sequence. [UX-W021, ch. 9]

Automation changes cognitive and contingency demands#

Automation can remove routine actions while leaving harder supervision, diagnosis and recovery tasks. Operators need opportunities to learn, practice and understand unusual conditions. An arrangement that performs ordinary work smoothly can still make an abnormal event difficult to recognize or handle.

Leveson therefore treats training, skill retention and useful feedback as design obligations, rather than assuming fewer routine interventions settle the comparison. [UX-W021, ch. 9]

Display design supplies a more specific economy. If an operator needs to know whether a variable exceeds a limit, a dial with a reference can reduce the extra processing required to compare an absolute number with a remembered threshold. Patterned displays can support recognition, while sequentially requested screens can require remembering one portion while seeking another. The claimed benefit concerns the actual decision task. [UX-W021, 304–305]

The book also preserves a trade-off: an effortlessly interpreted display may do less to develop the knowledge and thinking needed in abnormal conditions. It advocates involving operators, understanding the reasons behind established conventions, and evaluating the display in the work it supports. The design aim is appropriate information use with retained capability, not merely minimum momentary mental effort. [UX-W021, 304–305]

Intent specifications preserve why as well as what#

The intent-specification approach arranges information along intent, part–whole and refinement relations. It makes the rationale for a requirement available together with the requirement and its realization. A decision that appears arbitrary to a later engineer can have depended on an environmental assumption or a rejected alternative. Losing the reason can make maintenance and change hazardous. [UX-W021, ch. 10]

Refinement is not presented as an irreversible waterfall. Later findings can change earlier assumptions and choices. A specification can provide links among purpose, behavior, architecture, implementation and operation so that a revision can be examined for consequences elsewhere. The organization of information supports review and reuse; it still requires people who understand the domain and can judge the implications. [UX-W021, ch. 10]

Black-box behavioral specifications can serve as an interface between an integrator and contractors. The boundary hides selected implementation details while making required behavior and assumptions explicit. Reusing a component does not establish that its environmental assumptions remain satisfied in a new system. Those assumptions need ownership and revalidation; more documentation without such relationships is insufficient. [UX-W021, ch. 10]

The chapter's architecture-screening example uses severity and mitigatibility as comparative design information. Its ordinal scales are not calibrated probabilities or a complete quantitative risk model. Similarly, a promising operational alternative can shift demands to landing precision, infrastructure, communications or other supporting capabilities. Specifying an interface does not eliminate those external conditions. [UX-W021, ch. 10]

CAST reconstructs a wider control failure#

Causal Analysis based on Systems Theory, CAST, reconstructs events and then examines the control structure, constraints, context and decisions around them. It seeks explanations beyond a final error label. Information available somewhere in an organization may not have been observable to the relevant participant through the actual task and interface. An account must follow those relations rather than equate existence of data with usable knowledge. [UX-W021, ch. 11]

Citichem is a fictional teaching scenario. The chemical-overflow and Überlingen accounts are reconstructions from reports. Their practical value lies in making responsibilities and missing controls available for examination. Recommendation selection, priority and implementation remain judgment-laden. [UX-W021, ch. 11]

In the earlier 2004 article, Leveson reports completeness and analysis-effort advantages from informal evaluations and accident analyses. These evaluations compare the analyses rather than measuring a controlled resource intervention. [UX-W020, 26–28]

Designed constraints must survive operation and change#

Operational safety work includes review of planned change, detection of unplanned migration, audits, incident and problem reports, and feedback about the result. A constraint can be correctly designed and subsequently defeated by altered procedures, incentives, equipment or resources. The book asks who will notice and respond when an assumption no longer holds. [UX-W021, ch. 12]

The reporting example changes a small part of the work system. Instead of requiring a separate cumbersome report, a worker contacting those who can fix a problem can copy the safety function into an existing email. This can reduce the act of reporting. Acknowledgment, analysis, action and communication back to the reporter remain necessary; the extra copy is not the resolution. [UX-W021, 405]

Reporting incentives affect the data obtained. Fear of punishment, lack of confidence that anything will happen, confidentiality and time requirements can suppress reports. More reports can therefore indicate a changed reporting environment rather than a higher underlying hazard rate. The book also distinguishes a just culture from blanket immunity for gross negligence or deliberate disregard. The organizational purpose is useful learning and accountability in context. [UX-W021, chs. 12–13]

Generic indicators are not assumed to reveal every system's safety condition. Leading indicators should relate to the actual constraints and processes being maintained. Data collection has to be connected with interpretation and response. Training also needs to preserve reasons and assumptions, rather than only the memorized steps of a procedure. [UX-W021, ch. 12]

Authority, resources and insight require actual bearers#

Safety responsibilities must be matched with authority, resources and feedback. They occur at several levels and can differ by level. Leveson describes working groups as a means of preserving communication while maintaining review independence. Oversight can fail by becoming too entangled with the project or by withdrawing so far that it loses relevant understanding. [UX-W021, ch. 13, especially 435]

The book's warning about a transition from oversight to insight is concrete. A management-by-objectives label can conceal a reduction in personnel and budgets without anyone assuming critical tasks. Local interpretation of objectives needs competence and an actual process for satisfying the constraint. [UX-W021, 435]

Leveson argues for redesigns that improve safety and cost together, rather than treating their conflict as inevitable. Her account also addresses conflicting objectives, limited resources and negotiation. The related 2009 article seeks to reduce avoidable trade-offs. [UX-W021, chs. 8–13; SB-W017]

SUBSAFE illustrates a bounded, maintained institution#

The SUBSAFE account concerns watertight integrity and recovery from flooding. Other hazards, including fire, weapons and reactor safety, have other arrangements. Its assurance system includes material and fabrication evidence, design reviews, tests, calibration, maintenance, audits, training and reentry into controlled work processes. It is an extensive institutional architecture. [UX-W021, ch. 14]

The historical record reported for certified submarines is bounded by that purpose. The book's San Francisco collision account includes death and injuries even though the hull and recovery features performed. [UX-W021, ch. 14]

The example is informative because the design and operation are connected. An assurance claim depends on knowing materials, changes and test conditions and on continuing to enforce them. Certification is not a one-time substitute for maintenance or knowledge. The related 2009 source also identifies unusually favorable institutional resources, limiting simple transfer to a different setting. [UX-W021, ch. 14; SB-W017]

Interfaces can lose end-to-end ownership#

The satellite appendix traces a flight-constant entry through several organizations, formats and tests. A test can use a default rather than the actual flight data while participants assume the relevant assurance has occurred elsewhere. Many reviews do not compensate automatically for an unowned end-to-end process. [UX-W021, appendix B, 469–493]

Mergers, restructuring and staff departures also remove knowledge of why a process was designed as it was. The appendix describes lost history and rationale, no clearly identified process owner and misunderstandings about the coverage of testing. A filter was retained for consistency after a relevant need had changed. Some higher-level explanations remain speculative because the reports lacked data. [UX-W021, appendix B, especially 491–493]

The Walkerton appendix follows physical contamination controls together with monitoring, laboratory feedback, training and institutional change. The book explicitly distinguishes privatization itself from implementing a change without preserving notification and oversight. [UX-W021, appendix C, especially 514]

The system-dynamics appendix supplies another kind of account: qualitative reinforcing and balancing loops with delays. These diagrams identify hypotheses about migration and pressure. Signs alone do not establish exponential growth, equilibrium or instability without rate functions, parameters and conditions. [UX-W021, appendix D]

Related frameworks and a separate 2009 formulation#

La Porte's reconstruction of Perrow explains why interactive complexity and tight coupling can create conflicting demands for centralization and decentralization. A choice about authority depends on both dimensions. [UX-W004, through La Porte, printed 136]

Rosa’s 2005 symposium introduction compares normal-accident and high-reliability frameworks and calls for evidence from cases with and without accidents. Considering both challenges explanations built only from the selection of disasters. [UX-W016, abstract]

Leveson, Dulac, Marais and Carroll's 2009 article argues for examining concrete couplings and hazards rather than treating an industry label as sufficient explanation. Local decisions can be appropriate where coupling is reduced or where participants possess relevant knowledge and prepared responses. The account retains stakeholder judgment and goals in conflict. It is a related formulation by the same author group, with overlapping examples. [SB-W017]

SOHO: differences between analysts’ reconstructions#

Johnson and Holloway's 2003 SOHO analysis uses the same official report for two analysts. They identify useful systemic emphases but differ in model granularity and detailed classification. The comparison asks how analysts choose an appropriate representation, handle time and connect findings to recommendations. A framework does not mechanically determine all those judgments. [SB-W014]

They also acknowledge Leveson's response that constraints were underemphasized in their application. The criticism is therefore part of a methodological exchange, not a verdict based on an uncontested application. Their early concern about practical guidance should not be projected unchanged onto the later detailed 2012 book, which addresses temporal processes, actual versus nominal structures and operational change. [SB-W014; UX-W021]

A controlled method comparison reports a trade-off#

Abdulkhaleq and Wagner's 2015 study compared STPA, failure-mode-and-effects analysis and fault-tree analysis. Twenty-one students worked in three groups on three systems, with methods rotated. The results concern the requirements found and the effort of these participants, not observed future accidents. [SB-W015]

Reported mean recall was 0.443 for STPA, 0.326 for FMEA and 0.231 for FTA. Mean recorded time was 116, 94 and 88 minutes respectively. The method finding more requirements also required more time in this setting. [SB-W015, results and table 6]

The recall denominator was the union of reported requirements, not an independently known complete gold standard. Coverage also used a taxonomy partly drawn from Leveson. Group-level reports, novice participants, small sample and task ordering limit generalization. The paper's coverage table and prose disagree, as do its abstract and body about one applicability comparison. These inconsistencies make the corresponding coverage and applicability comparisons ambiguous. [SB-W015]

An application supplies conditional simulation evidence#

Liu and colleagues' 2020 differential-braking article models seven unsafe control actions and simulates selected scenarios using a cited aircraft model. It demonstrates how the approach is applied to specified control timing and state assumptions. It retains power, speed and torque sensing, position information, human response and the model's conditions. [SB-W016]

Its five-second threshold belongs to the stated dimensions, initial conditions and disturbances. It is not an operational rule or flight-validated universal limit. [SB-W016]

Information and coordinated pathways in selected practice sources#

The National Commission on Forensic Science's 2015 policy-development abstract asks which information belongs in a forensic assessment. A fact can support a conclusion about a suspect while being irrelevant to the inferential connection between an examined pattern and the requested scientific opinion. The proposed context management therefore concerns the arrangement and timing of information access, not simply less information in every task. Deciding relevance itself requires expertise. [GL-W063]

The document is an abstract of a developing proposal, not a final demonstrated intervention. Its reference to cognitive and contextual bias identifies the problem and intended principle. [GL-W063]

Rotter and colleagues' 2025 clinical-pathways review, reports fifty-eight studies and searches through July 2024. Stand-alone multidisciplinary pathways probably reduce in-hospital complications and length of stay, with moderate certainty; mortality, readmission and cost conclusions remain uncertain. Multifaceted implementations have less conclusive results. [GL-W058]

These findings are outcome-specific. A shorter stay does not establish every patient benefit or identify one coordination mechanism as the cause. [GL-W058]

The selected NHS England guidance, updated 23 July 2026, describes longer care packages joining prevention, personalized goals, team competence and continuing review, with funding arrangements intended to support them. The worked follow-up example illustrates planned work over time rather than episodic repair alone. It is an implementation design under a specific institutional arrangement, not comparative outcome evidence. [GL-W064, §§1.1–1.6 and worked example]

The forensic proposal addresses bias in evaluating evidence; the clinical-pathways review compares patient and hospital outcomes; the dental guidance organizes prevention and follow-up. They describe different activities within the conduct and assessment of care.

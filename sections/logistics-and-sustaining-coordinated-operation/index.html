Sustaining operation is a continuing activity#

These selected accounts examine the provision, movement, maintenance and information that allow other activities to continue.

MCDP 4: visibility requires interpretation#

The 2023 edition of Marine Corps Logistics separates demand visibility from supply visibility. The first concerns what forces need in relation to an operation; the second concerns the resources available, including equipment, units, infrastructure and supplies. The document emphasizes that data must be processed and analyzed to create shared understanding. A current snapshot is not equivalent to knowing whether anticipated demands can be met. [MIL-W003, printed pp. 2-6–2-8]

Its sustainability discussion places self-sufficiency and dependency on a spectrum. A force carrying or producing everything it needs can have autonomy over a specified period. An outsourced arrangement can reduce its footprint, but requires access to external providers. The doctrine expects combinations, chosen in relation to mission and context, rather than pure self-sufficiency or pure outsourcing. The external supply relationship remains part of the account even when the supported unit is smaller.

Simplicity is likewise described as a designed and maintained property. Clear command relationships, standardized interoperable procedures, fewer required actions and practiced methods can make execution more manageable. The document does not describe the logistical undertaking as intrinsically simple: planners still estimate needs using usage, equipment, personnel and mission information. It seeks actionable concepts without instructions for every eventuality. [MIL-W003]

Push and pull distribute anticipation differently#

In the selected distribution passage, a push system forecasts requirements and supplies them on a predetermined schedule. Its advantages include regularity and predictability; its limitations include the time required to adjust when requirements change. Forethought, planning and coordination remain necessary. A pull system responds to requirements submitted by the receiving unit. It can reduce provision of unneeded supplies, but the receiver must identify and communicate its requirements. [MIL-W003, printed pp. 2-26–2-27, figure 2-6]

The figure explicitly assigns anticipation to the provider under push and to the receiver under pull. Pull is therefore not an absence of anticipation. It depends on developed procedures, reliable communications and available packages. The doctrine also recommends combinations: routine requirements can favor scheduled support while unusual requirements call for requests.

The logistics snowball discussion addresses inflated requirements and habitual extras. Repeated across a force, such demands burden the system and can divert support away from more urgent needs. The proposed response includes disciplined requirement generation, training, trust and good habits. Emerging digital mapping or machine learning is presented as a possible improvement to visibility and responsiveness. [MIL-W003]

The selected education and training pages retain the institutional investment behind manageable execution. People learn how the system works, how to request support, how occupational fields interact and how logistics limits plans. Training introduces friction and uncertainty and develops initiative. The foreword identifies the document as guidance and distinguishes fictional scenarios. [MIL-W003, printed pp. 4-12–4-14]

Thorpe: define sustaining functions so they are actually assigned#

In the opening of Pure Logistics, Thorpe argues that the definition of logistics determines a division of labor. If that division is incomplete, functions may be omitted; if it overlaps carelessly, effort may be duplicated. His example is care of the wounded: merely possessing medical personnel does not ensure that their numbers, equipment and employment have been estimated for the proposed operation. Assigning a function makes it part of planning. [MIL-W016, 1917 work in the 1997 edition, printed pp. 9– 13]

This historical argument is relevant because it treats classification as consequential for preparation. It does not suggest that a label performs the function. The care still needs people, resources and an estimate related to the planned activity. The selected later organizational passage also debates where intelligence belongs: information serves strategy, tactics and logistics, and must be systematically supplied even when its bureau is located elsewhere. Functional use and administrative location need not coincide. [MIL-W016, printed p. 45]

Thorpe's broader organizational proposals are prescriptive historical arguments. Later favorable editorial comments in the edition are distinguishable from Thorpe's own text.

Eccles through a doctrinal attribution#

The MCDP introduction draws on Eccles's Logistics in the National Defense to connect a national economy with combat operations. It treats logistics as transforming resources and productive capacity into the material means for operations. [MIL-W015, through MIL-W003, introduction]

Kress: intrinsic versatility and structural flexibility#

Kress's 2000 article distinguishes versatility of assets from flexibility supplied by their deployment in a logistics network. Intrinsic flexibility concerns how many relevant demands an asset can serve and how many resources a support asset can handle. The palletized-load example separates a truck from a particular load, allowing a vehicle to switch among resource types. General-purpose maintenance capability supplies another kind of versatility, conditional on staff qualification, equipment and spares. [MIL-W013, printed pp. 44–49]

The intrinsic-flexibility index counts associations between assets and essential demand units. An essential demand unit pairs a supported unit with a resource it can actually consume. The association is not established by a generic name: a maintenance unit supports a particular vehicle type only if it has the capability to do so. The worked examples show how a change in transport or maintenance versatility changes the index. The index measures those defined associations, rather than the complete cost or effectiveness of every asset.

Structural flexibility concerns where resources are held and how they can flow. Kress represents source, intermediate and destination nodes, with edge capacities affected by transport and reliability. Because meaningful lateral flow at a command level is largely excluded in the model, the graph is essentially a tree. This is a substantive assumption. A result for that structure should not be presented as a theorem about an arbitrary network with extensive peer redistribution. [MIL-W013, printed pp. 45–47]

Holding supplies higher in a tree can postpone commitment to a particular subordinate unit. It preserves alternative destinations, provided reliable transport can deliver them in time. Concentration is therefore coupled to flow capacity and lead time. The paper's sufficient comparison of two systems sharing a graph explicitly includes the requirement that the relevant edge capacities not be worse in the more concentrated system. Rearward inventory without usable delivery is not the claimed mechanism.

The flexibility comparison can reverse#

Kress's structural-flexibility measure combines resource sufficiency and timeliness, with stated weighting across command levels. In its worked comparison, posture B is often favored, but not under every supply and demand condition. For one parameter combination the reported indices are 0.574 for B and 0.353 for A. With relatively abundant resources and lower variation in demand, the ordering reverses: the reported comparison gives 0.795 for A and 0.768 for B. [MIL-W013, printed pp. 52–53]

The paper explains the reversal. When both arrangements are highly likely to have enough supplies, the extra benefit associated with concentration becomes less important, while the time to deliver dominates the index. A resource-positioning advantage depends on which constraint binds.

These numbers belong to a constructed model with specified demand, timeliness and weighting assumptions. [MIL-W013]

Identifying and locating a convoy#

Kress illustrates convoy tracking by combining two kinds of data. Radio-frequency tags associate a convoy with its cargo. GPS receivers locate its vehicles. A map can then show where the convoy is and how it is moving alongside its destination and load. Tracking the vehicles answers where the convoy is. Identifying its load lets the logistician see which supplies are on the move. [MIL-W012, example 5.13]

RAND: sense and response retain prediction and command#

The selected RAND report combines prediction, responsiveness and command-and-control capabilities. Its future-work discussion calls for continuous monitoring of resources and process performance, estimates of changing campaign needs, assessment of plan feasibility and review of infrastructure configurations. The report places logistics information early in planning so proposed operations can be supportable. [MIL-W017, summary, printed pp. 9–10 and 79–81]

The selected passages require weighing support alternatives against time, capability, risk and cost. Near-real-time sensing is intended to alert decision makers to deviations before mission capability is affected. Metrics should concern outcomes rather than only outputs. The apparent responsiveness is consequently supported by maintained information, planning, thresholds, configuration and authority. It is a program analysis and proposal.

Shared standards and a shared system#

The official account describes joint support using common technical standards, interfaces, containers and pallets. Its examples contrast incompatible equipment interfaces with arrangements that can be combined across services. A further passage describes several services using a joint logistics information system during an exercise. The relevant mechanism is compatibility prepared in advance, together with

shared information during coordinated operation. [MIL-W026, sections 统一标准 and 共用系统]

The account reports implementation and exercises from an official organizational perspective. Common standards and systems require design, implementation and maintenance.

Fox: threat, environment and one's own organization#

Fox's conceptual essay divides contested-logistics challenges among the threat, the environment and the supported organization's own arrangements. It argues that the categories must be addressed together and calls for attention to transport possibilities, protection, deception and masking. The support system's

exposure is thus part of its usefulness: a technically efficient movement route can still be vulnerable. [MIL-W030, framing and conceptual support-boundary discussion]

The essay questions whether reducing or removing a movement demand could reduce opportunities for interference, while calling for theory as well as technology. This is a conceptual proposal with a hypothetical future vignette.

Sherbrooke: multi-echelon recoverable-item inventory#

Sherbrooke’s METRIC model compares where to hold spare parts in a system of bases and a depot. When an item fails, the base repairs it if it can. Otherwise the item goes to the depot for repair and the base requests a replacement from the depot. For costly parts with low demand, the model starts repair and replenishment one item at a time. It leaves out transfers between bases. [MIL-W031]

Sherbrooke’s program compares stocking plans by the number of requests expected to remain unfilled at a given inventory investment. It can evaluate an existing distribution of spares as well as compare alternatives, so the decision concerns where to stock a part as well as how many to buy. [MIL-W032] [MIL-W031]

A queueing benchmark for pooling#

Van Leeuwaarden, Mathijsen and Zwart's review explains resource pooling using an M/M/s queue. Jobs arrive as a Poisson process, service times are independent exponential variables, servers share a common service capability, and waiting occurs in an unbounded buffer in arrival order. Separate queues can leave one server idle while another has waiting jobs. A common queue removes that particular mismatch under the model's assumptions. [TA-W016, printed pp. 403–411, especially p. 405]

The quality-and-efficiency-driven regime adds capacity of square-root order above offered load as the system grows. Utilization can approach one while the probability of delay approaches a value strictly between zero and one, with mean delay decreasing at the specified scale. Proposition 2.1 states the asymptotic relation for the benchmark setting. Pooling therefore does not mean that every job avoids waiting, nor does high utilization alone establish good service.

The pooling result depends on the model's arrival, service, capacity and limiting assumptions. [TA-W016, introduction and proposition]

IMO: integrate navigation information while retaining integrity work#

The IMO e-navigation framework concerns harmonized collection, integration, exchange, presentation and analysis of marine information aboard ship and ashore. Its five solutions address bridge design, standardized automated reporting, equipment and information integrity, graphical integration, and communication of vessel-traffic services. These are distinguishable components of an intended reduction in administrative burden and improvement of navigation services. [GL-W082, definition and implementation-plan sections]

The plan remains subject to review as user needs, technologies and lessons change. The source states intended benefits rather than measured outcomes.

Positioning supplies and pooling capacity#

The sources explain sustaining operation through assigned responsibilities, versatile assets, retained alternatives, delivery capacity, compatible interfaces, shared information and controlled pooling. Kress's

ranking reversal shows how the advantage of a resource position depends on supply, demand and delivery time; the doctrine distributes anticipation across organizational roles.

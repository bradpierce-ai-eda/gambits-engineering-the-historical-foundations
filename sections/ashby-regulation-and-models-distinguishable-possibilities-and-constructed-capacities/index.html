Behavior, variety and regulation#

Ashby's An Introduction to Cybernetics develops a language for possible behavior, then variety and communication, then regulation and the construction of regulators. The book first asks what system is being described and what distinctions and couplings its description preserves. Its later accounts of regulation rely on those choices. Its final chapters ask how an arrangement can undertake repeated regulation and where its capacities come from. [W001, parts I–III]

A system is a selected state description#

Ashby's pendulum example begins with a failed description. Record the angular position at fixed intervals and it may appear that a position has two different successors. Release the pendulum from ten degrees and its next position differs from the next position when it passes through ten degrees while already moving. The problem is not resolved by insisting that the physical object is the same. Angular position alone omits a distinction that matters to its subsequent behavior. Adding angular velocity supplies the relevant state description for the model under consideration; adding an unrelated variable such as the bob's mass to the original description does not solve that particular omission. [W001, §3/11]

In this usage, a system is a chosen list of variables, with states formed from their possible values. The material object supplies many possible variables; the scientific account selects those relevant to a given interest. This does not license arbitrary simplification. The selected description must support the transition properties claimed for it. If an observed successor depends on an omitted variable, a deterministic account on the reduced state set can fail. Ashby's construction makes finding an adequate description part of investigation, rather than something supplied by pointing at an object and naming it “the system.” [W001, §3/11]

His transformations distinguish a state from the rule under which it changes. A closed transformation sends states in the selected set to states within that set. A single-valued transformation assigns one successor to each state. These are formal properties of the description, not claims that the object is thermodynamically isolated, physically self-sufficient or free of environmental dependence. An input can select among transformations; changing an input then changes the transition applied to the state. Omitting relevant inputs can make the reduced transition uncertain. [W001, chs. 2–4]

Ashby abstracts from material substrate so that the same pattern of transitions can describe differently constituted mechanisms. The behavioral description selects the properties relevant to a particular question. [W001, §§1/2–1/5, 3/11]

Coupling supplies a relation that a parts list lacks#

Ashby considers coupling two machines while retaining their individual transition laws. To do so, each must affect the other through the inputs admitted by those laws. The coupling specifies how one machine's state determines an input of the other. In his elementary construction, both update on a common time scale; the next joint state is determined from the two present states and the selected coupling. The whole can then be described on pairs of component states. A different coupling can produce different joint behavior even when the component transformations are unchanged. [W001, §§4/6–4/10]

The restriction to input and output is consequential. Forcing two objects together so that their mechanisms are damaged would also connect them in ordinary language, but would not preserve the

component laws used in this construction. Likewise, silently changing update timing would change the model being analyzed. Thus “the parts interact” is too incomplete to explain the whole. The manner and conditions of interaction belong to the explanation. The usefulness of an arrangement lies in those actual relations, rather than in a count or inventory of its parts. [W001, §§4/6–4/7]

The book also warns against reading a whole dynamics from a favorable loop label. In its two-variable example, each next value is half the other's present value. Each variable has a positive immediate effect on the other, yet repeated updates converge toward zero. Calling the relation positive feedback is insufficient to prove instability. Conversely, an undesirable state can be stable. Ashby uses a continuing fire as an example: persistence and return after disturbance are not automatically achievements wanted by the observer. The state, disturbance and transition must remain specified before stability becomes a useful judgment. [W001, §§5/9–5/11]

Black-box knowledge does not identify a unique interior#

For Ashby, the experimenter and the black box are coupled: the experimenter's actions change inputs, the box produces observable results, and those results influence subsequent actions. A protocol can support a canonical behavioral description under the assumed determinacy and accessible tests. It does not follow that every relevant transition can be repeatedly tested. A state may become inaccessible after the process passes through it. His examples include an irreversible explosion and an organism that no longer has its original untrained condition. Replacing the specimen introduces an additional assumption about comparability; it is not literally returning the same object to its former state. [W001, §§6/1–6/6]

Even sufficiently described behavior need not determine a unique interior. Ashby's relay networks can realize the same required conducting behavior with different arrangements of contacts. The same object can also have different connection diagrams when studied mechanically and electrically. An insulator can connect two parts mechanically while separating them electrically. Which relation is being investigated therefore matters to the diagram and to any inference about independence. A successful interface description is knowledge of selected behavior, not a transparent picture of every mechanism responsible for it. [W001, §6/7]

Partial knowledge can be complete for a selected operation#

Ashby's parity example gives a precise sense to useful incompleteness. One need not know the exact product of every pair of integers to know whether their product is odd or even. Odd times odd is odd; the other parity combinations give an even product. This little account omits almost everything about numerical magnitude, yet gives a determinate answer to the selected parity question. By contrast, a list of a few exact products is incomplete even within multiplication when it leaves further requested cases unanswered. Partiality and adequacy must therefore be judged with respect to what the account is intended to do. [W001, §6/13]

For a dynamic system, grouping detailed states gives a useful simpler machine only if the grouped transitions remain consistent. Suppose two detailed states receive the same reduced label but move to different reduced labels under the same input. The reduced current label then does not determine the next one. Ashby explicitly contrasts suitable and unsuitable groupings. His homomorphic simplification preserves the operative structure required for the reduced behavior; arbitrary lumping need not. The fewer distinctions are justified by what remains predictable or usable, rather than by their small number alone. [W001, §§6/12–6/14]

This supplies his response to practical largeness. A system is very large relative to an observer when that observer cannot obtain all observations, carry out all calculations or exercise all control with the available resources. More variables or finer discrimination can make the same material object larger in this sense.

The mathematical arguments do not fail because their sets are large, provided their assumptions still hold; their application to a material case remains a separate question. Deliberately chosen aspects can be enough for a practical purpose. Their sufficiency must be established for that purpose, not asserted for every omitted detail. [W001, §§4/17–4/18, 6/14]

What variety counts#

In An Introduction to Cybernetics, W. Ross Ashby gives variety a specified meaning. Consider a sequence containing twelve occurrences of the letters a, b, and c. If order is ignored and only the distinct letters are counted, its variety is three. Counting occurrences, distinguishing types, and retaining an ordered sequence are different descriptions. A numerical statement about variety therefore needs the set of possibilities and the distinctions that define its elements. It is not a count whose object can be left unspecified. [W001, §7/6]

Ashby's semaphore example makes the observer consequential. Two arms with eight positions each can form 64 combinations when their positions are independently set and the arms are individually distinguishable. At a distance, the observer may be unable to distinguish which arm occupies which position. Some combinations then become indistinguishable, leaving 36 distinguishable positions in his example. The physical apparatus has not acquired a different number of arm settings merely because the observer changed. The set of distinctions available in that observation has changed. [W001, §7/6]

This is a useful prerequisite for reading an organizational account that speaks of what a manager or operational unit can distinguish. It directs attention to the relevant possibilities and the means of telling them apart. It does not establish that someone understands a situation merely because a large quantity of data is available.

Counts and bits are different numerical forms#

Ashby uses either the number of distinct elements or its base-two logarithm, with the context specifying which. In the logarithmic form, the unit is the bit. Eight possibilities have a logarithmic variety of three bits; two have one bit. When the distinctions combine independently, the number of combinations multiplies, while these logarithmic quantities add. This explains why a report of “no variety” can mean one remaining possibility: the count is one and its base-two logarithm is zero. Confusing the count with its logarithm changes the meaning of a calculation. [W001, §7/7]

The set need not be a collection of simultaneous states#

A machine is in one state at a time, so why speak of a set of its states? Ashby gives several reasons. A description may concern a population of machines or replicates, each currently in a different state. It may concern one machine's states at different times. Or it may concern the possibilities an observer considers because the machine's actual state is unknown. Those are different grounds for forming a set. A calculation should preserve which ground applies. [W001, §7/23]

This distinction prevents a common misreading. A decrease in the possibilities consistent with an observer's description does not necessarily describe the physical removal of machines or a reduction in the work being performed. Likewise, a collection of observations over time should not be described as though all the recorded states simultaneously existed within one object. Ashby explicitly develops the actual in relation to the possible; that analytical choice is part of the explanation.

How an arrangement can remove distinctions#

Ashby examines a single-valued transformation in which A changes to B, B to C, and C to C. A set initially containing all three possibilities has only two distinct images after one application, B and C. After another application, only C remains. The reason is specific: more than one distinct input can have the same output, while each input has only one output under the transformation. A one-to-one mapping on the inputs actually present would retain their number of distinctions. [W001, §7/22]

This result concerns the stated transformation and set. Losing a distinction need not be beneficial. Ashby's coffee-message illustration shows a case in which convergence toward a common temperature destroys the intended distinction among messages. A person may want an arrangement to remove an unwanted effect or to preserve a useful difference; identifying which is being considered requires the task as well as the count. [W001, §§7/22–7/24]

The response must be appropriate, as well as available#

Ashby's introductory regulation game separates disturbances, responses, and outcomes. One player chooses a row of a table; the responding player, knowing that choice, chooses a column. The selected cell determines the outcome. A desired outcome can always be obtained only when an appropriate entry is available for every relevant row and the responder selects it. Merely having many columns does not establish that they contain what is needed. The arrangement of the table matters. [W001, §§11/3–11/4]

For a particular family of tables, Ashby derives a bound. He assumes that a column contains no repeated outcome, and the responding strategy selects one entry from each row. If there are r rows and c available columns, the selected outcomes cannot have fewer than r/c distinct values. To keep selecting the same outcome, a strategy would need a different column each time; the available columns limit how far it can do so. The bound is a necessary limit under those conditions, rather than a sufficient recipe for constructing every desired regulator. Ashby states the corresponding logarithmic form under the same conditions. [W001, §§11/5–11/7]

This is the setting of his concise statement that response variety can reduce outcome variety. In an organizational application, the set of relevant disturbances, available responses, distinctions, and acceptable outcomes must still be identified. A slogan about matching complexity does not supply those identifications or prove their adequacy. A local arrangement can change what distinctions reach another role, while the capacities and purposes of that arrangement remain substantive questions.

Constraint compares an actual set with alternatives#

Ashby's constraint is a relation between sets: fewer possibilities occur under one condition than under an explicit comparison condition. His traffic signal has three binary lamps, so independent settings would permit eight combinations. The signal uses four. The excluded combinations establish a constraint in that set of simultaneous lamp settings. Each lamp can still be lit or unlit; the restriction concerns which combinations occur together. The presence of constraint must therefore be attached to the joint possibilities under discussion. It cannot be inferred merely from knowing the individual component ranges. [W001, §§7/8–7/11]

He uses an assembled chair to explain why a thing can act as a unit. Four disconnected rigid legs would each have six degrees of freedom of movement; once rigidly joined as a chair, the assembly has six. Knowing the locations of some parts now restricts those of the others. This is his account of coherence as a constraint on their relative possibilities. [W001, §7/16]

The book connects constraint to prediction and compact description. An aircraft cannot occupy an arbitrary new position at each instant; continuity and characteristics of its motion restrict possibilities.

Prediction can exploit such restrictions. Similarly, a long protocol showing consistent machine behavior can be represented more compactly by its transformation and actual inputs. The transformation does not supply the inputs, and a compressed description is not by itself a cheaper physical realization. Ashby is explaining why regularity makes a useful account possible. [W001, §§7/18–7/20]

His learning examples make an environmental condition explicit. An association can be learned only if the observed sequence has relevant regularity; an unchanged maze offers a learnable pattern that an arbitrarily changing maze would not. These are arguments about what supplies the regularity being exploited. [W001, §7/21]

A smaller channel can require a longer process#

Ashby's account of transmission distinguishes what can pass in one step from what a sequence can convey. A channel with two distinguishable values per step can form many distinguishable sequences. Ten binary positions permit up to 1,024 combinations when independent. The restricted alphabet does not impose the same bound on a sequence of ten positions as on one position. A receiver capable of retaining the relevant history can therefore distinguish more possibilities over time than the instantaneous channel alphabet suggests. [W001, §§8/13–8/14]

This is a conditional trade between capacity and duration. It requires usable distinctions, appropriate coding and a receiver/process able to accumulate them. A blocked channel gains no information merely by waiting longer, and distinctions lost before transmission do not reappear by extending the record. Ashby's analysis of delay also retains the intervening stages: if a source affects a receiver only through a chain of parts, some effects reach it later. Redrawing the chain as a single source does not make the delayed internal path instantaneous. The abstraction can change which boundary is described without changing the events. [W001, §§8/14–8/15]

The material environment can participate in such a channel. In his multiplication example, someone unable to complete the task mentally uses pencil and paper. Activity passes from the brain through arm and pencil to marks, then through light and retina back to the brain. This is communication through an external medium between parts of a continuing process. What counts as a channel depends on the behavioral relationship being studied, rather than on whether an anatomical nerve joins the two endpoints. [W001, §9/15]

Redundancy is relative to the message and its realization#

Ashby returns to the traffic lights to ask whether the four required signals could be encoded by two binary lamps rather than three. Four combinations suffice to distinguish stop, prepare to go, go and prepare to stop. Under his stated condition that lamps are expensive, this reduces the lamp cost to two-thirds while preserving those signaling distinctions. [W001, §9/16]

The very same lights supply a different redundancy if examined as a clock-driven sequence. When the four signals always repeat in the same order, the initial phase determines later signals in the sequence. The relevant alternatives are now possible sequences, not simultaneous lamp combinations. The book uses the difference to reject an unqualified question about whether “the lights” are redundant: the same physical object provides different sets with different answers. [W001, §9/16]

Regulation preserves a desired range, not constancy alone#

Ashby's regulation account specifies essential variables and acceptable values. A water bath is not good merely because it stays constant: it must remain in the desired temperature range despite the disturbances considered. In his communication description, a successful barrier prevents differences

among disturbances from producing unacceptable differences in those variables. This does not mean that every part of the system must be deprived of information. The active regulator may need distinctions that its successful action prevents from reaching the protected variables. [W001, §§10/6–10/7]

The tortoise's shell and the fencer illustrate two different ways of obtaining protection. A passive barrier can reduce the effect of blows on protected tissues. Skilled counteraction instead obtains information and varies action to meet the threat. Ashby groups both within the broader regulatory question; he does not define regulation to require a separate active controller in every case. The shell's success remains relative to what it resists, while the fencer's depends on available perception and action. [W001, §10/7]

The response table discussed above represents one family of regulatory limits. The physical or organizational case must supply the table and its assumptions. Ashby explicitly allows the given environment and mechanism to block some disturbance variety before an additional regulatory response acts. In some cases that baseline blocking is enough; in others it leaves work for a responsive regulator. Thus an unconditional equation equating all environmental variety with a required controller size would lose an important part of his own account. Counting relevant disturbances, the preexisting transformation, response distinctions and acceptable outcomes all matter. [W001, §§11/9–11/11]

The book also states where its normative task stops. At the opening of chapter 12, essential variables and their acceptable states are assumed to be given by outside considerations. The problem developed within the book is how to achieve them despite disturbances. The formal machinery does not derive which human purposes should be adopted or who should bear the burden of protecting them. [W001, §12/1]

Sensing, action and timing are separate restrictions#

Ashby distinguishes restrictions on receiving information from restrictions on acting. A driver who cannot see through the windscreen and a rudder that is stuck are different failures of the regulatory path. More information does not supply missing actuation; more possible actions do not identify which one is needed. He also distinguishes knowledge that a disturbance has occurred from knowledge of the system's present state: a signalman can know there is fog while being unable to determine where trains are. These are different required distinctions. [W001, §12/2]

Timing introduces a further condition. The elementary response game gives the regulator time to respond before the outcome is determined. Some regulations can use a warning; others learn of a disturbance through its effects. The thermostat in his water-bath example does not see the cold flask approaching. It receives information after the temperature begins to fall. Calling both processes regulation does not remove the difference in the route or time available for acting. [W001, §§12/3–12/4]

His argument against a perfectly error-controlled regulator is made under a particular static response framing: the response is a determinate function of the present outcome. If that outcome supplies all the disturbance information, holding it exactly constant removes the distinctions available through that channel. Allowing a small deviation can make preventing a much larger deviation possible. A dynamic controller with memory retains additional state that this immediate response table does not represent. [W001, §§12/5–12/7]

An adaptive arrangement must find and retain an acceptable state#

Ashby's hunt-and-stick construction changes what is selected. A regulating component is arranged so that it keeps changing under a “bad” input and can remain at equilibrium under a “good” one. It is coupled to the other component so that an acceptable condition supplies the good input. Any joint equilibrium then has the required property. The regulator vetoes unacceptable equilibria rather than explicitly specifying every detail of the acceptable configuration in advance. [W001, §12/14]

That statement about equilibria is not by itself a guarantee that every trajectory reaches one. The text recognizes trapping in an undesirable stable region and discusses richer transition possibilities. Accessibility of an acceptable state, the absence of other closed classes and the actual coupling remain necessary questions. Enlarging a list of regulatory states does not help if none of the available transitions can obtain the required condition. [W001, §§12/11–12/14]

The homeostat makes the mechanism concrete. Resistor settings, relays and a source of successive values allow changes while the system remains unacceptable; acceptable behavior blocks further changes. Viewed without access to the supplied table of values, the process can appear Markovian. Viewed with those determinants included, a different state description becomes available. Neither description removes the physical switching and supplied values. The explanation locates the variation being selected and the mechanism that retains a satisfactory condition. [W001, §12/15]

His telephone selector gives a simpler determinate example. It tests lines in order and stops at a disengaged line. The sequence supplies the selection without requiring a precomputed answer identifying the first free line. Yet it must encounter an available line if it is to make a successful connection; the construction does not make a free line exist when all are busy. Repeated local testing can replace one kind of prior specification while retaining a criterion, apparatus, time and feasible alternatives. [W001, §12/17]

A reusable rule and a complete response sequence differ#

Chapter 13 asks what happens when similar classes of disturbance recur over a long period. If three disturbances can appear independently on 400 occasions, there are 3^400 possible disturbance sequences. A matching response sequence can have correspondingly many alternatives. Yet a machine with the right input-response rule can generate the appropriate response on each occasion without someone specifying the whole sequence in advance. The rule stays compact because the recurring relationship is reused; the actual disturbance supplies which response is needed each time. [W001, §§13/9–13/10]

This is Ashby's own transition from a regulator's behavior to its construction. The responsible entity can undertake the recurring responses directly or build an arrangement that undertakes them. The small rule does not contain all future disturbance events. It supplies a response relation that operates when events occur. His later mechanics example explicitly retains maintenance. [W001, §§13/10, 14/5]

Design becomes selection among possible machines. In the book's deliberately broad communication account, components, couplings or input specifications distinguish one resulting machine from alternatives. Selection can occur in stages and can be supplemented by other selectors or by processes unrelated to the main system. Ashby distinguishes an abstract amount of selection from how long a search takes. Successive group divisions can find an item much faster than one-at-a-time examination if the required group-membership tests are actually available. His huge-universe example expressly assumes those tests possible; the counting does not construct a detector capable of making them. [W001, §§13/11–13/20]

Amplification retains the source of supplementation#

Ashby's power-amplifier discussion states what is being supplemented. The crane driver's small movement controls a much larger flow of energy, supplied elsewhere. From the designer's account, the output does not exceed total supplied energy; from the user's immediate task, the ability to control the larger output is consequential. Both descriptions can be correct because they account for different boundaries. His analogy for regulatory amplification depends on keeping that distinction visible. [W001, §§14/1–14/3]

One example makes the borrowed capacity explicit: someone unable to correct hundreds of mains-voltage disturbances per second can select a suitable stabilizer from a catalogue. Choosing the product uses the manufacturer's design capacities and the apparatus's continuing response. The person's choice and the ensuing regulation have different alternative sets and time scales. The example demonstrates the logical possibility of obtaining much more regulation than the chooser can perform directly, while openly acknowledging the manufacturer's contribution. [W001, §14/5]

The water-bath example compares an assumed quantity of selection involved in making a thermostat with repeated corrections over a year. Ashby presents a large amplification on that informational account and then explicitly says there need not be amplification in every case. The practical comparison includes the cost, in some sense, of making the machine against doing the job personally. The units used for selection do not automatically measure financial expense, energy, skill or maintenance. Those further quantities require the particular comparison. [W001, §14/5]

His twenty mechanics and two thousand rooms are especially relevant to the work retained by automation. The alternative to manually regulating every room is to make and maintain machines that regulate them. Supplementation comes from other contributors to design and manufacture and from the environment, which supplies current temperature and humidity directly to conditioners rather than through the mechanics. The mechanics become regulators of the regulatory machinery. Ashby also says these relationships need not form one simple hierarchy. [W001, §14/5]

Conant and Ashby: what the “good regulator” result optimizes#

Roger Conant and Ashby's 1970 article asks a different formal question. Given regulatory events R, events S in the reguland, and an outcome determined by the two, it defines an optimal regulator for its theorem by minimal outcome entropy. The distribution of S and the joint outcome mapping are given; conditional probabilities of regulatory events given S are varied. Its simplest optimal regulator associates each relevant S event with one R event, a mapping from S to R. “Model” has that mapped-event sense here. It does not initially mean a predictive computer simulation stored inside a controller. [W050, PDF pp. 8–9]

The proof's operative move is to eliminate unnecessary conditional randomization. Where two actions available for one S event would generate different outcomes, shifting probability can lower outcome entropy, contradicting optimality in the conditions used. Where different actions give the same optimal outcome, one can choose a single action without changing optimality. Repeating this selection yields a deterministic conditional mapping. The paper's simplifying uniqueness assumption on the optimal outcome distribution is explicit. The argument establishes a mapping within that optimization. [W050, PDF pp. 8–9]

The authors themselves state that equally optimal regulators may retain unnecessary complexity. Thus their theorem does not literally establish the strongest reading of the title, that every successful regulator must have the simplest model relation. Moreover, minimum entropy is not a complete valuation of outcomes: one constant bad outcome can have zero entropy. [W050, PDF pp. 2–4, 8–9]

Virgo and colleagues: a model can be an attributed interpretation#

Nathaniel Virgo, Martin Biehl, Manuel Baltieri and Matteo Capucci's 2025 paper explicitly challenges the inference from Conant and Ashby to every good regulator having a nontrivial internal model. Their new theorem uses deterministic discrete-time coupled machines, a chosen agent/environment boundary and a good set of joint states. A regulating set is nonempty, lies within the good set and is forward-closed: starting in it, the coupled evolution stays in it. Existence of that set establishes regulation from appropriate joint initial states, not recovery from every arbitrary disturbance. [RGC-W036, §§2–2.1]

They then associate an agent state with the set of environment states paired with it in the regulating set. This can be interpreted as a possibilistic belief: it identifies what remains possible, without assigning Bayesian probabilities. Their consistency condition allows forgetting, so the newly attributed possibilities can be broader than the tightest update consistent with the action and observation. The lemma identifies this consistency with forward closure; the theorem adds nonemptiness and inclusion within the chosen good set. The model interpretation is constructed from the dynamics and the observer's choices. It is not an extra causal component that must already have been designed inside the agent. [RGC-W036, definitions 3.1–3.3, lemma 3.2, theorem 3.4]

Their doorstop example makes the qualification concrete. A singleton agent state and singleton action and sensor spaces admit a constant interpretation even if the environment and selected good set are complicated. The complicated description can belong to the observer's account, while the doorstop has no corresponding rich internal state. The authors therefore reject the inference that effective regulator design must always begin by building an explicit model. They distinguish such a trivial interpretation from cases with rich ongoing interaction, and leave more precise measures of that distinction for further work. Their suggested relation to the free-energy principle is qualitative and explicitly unresolved. [RGC-W036, §3.2 and discussion]

The deterministic model maps an environment state and action to the next environment state and sensor value. Its proposed stochastic extension is left for further work. [RGC-W036, definitions 2.2–2.3, §2, fig. 1]

Powers: retain the controlled perception and changing reference#

William Powers's 2009 primary summary of perceptual control theory gives another account of what repeated feedback can accomplish. The driver compares a perceived relation between car and road with a reference for how that relation should be. Steering changes as the discrepancy changes. A crosswind can require varying steering even while the car's visible path remains nearly constant. The repeated correction can therefore be active precisely where an observer sees little change in the controlled result. This is the mechanism Powers uses to challenge a description of behavior as a fixed sequence of responses computed in advance. [SG-W027, steps 6–9]

When the driver takes an exit, the reference changes. The lower process need not oppose every departure from the earlier lane position; it acts to bring perception toward the new reference. Powers's hierarchy gives higher systems the role of varying reference perceptions for lower systems, which retain the work of adjusting actions against immediate disturbances. His explanation separates specifying a desired result from specifying every muscle movement. It is especially relevant to how an arrangement can leave detailed action to an ongoing lower process while retaining a higher-level goal. [SG-W027, steps 8–9, 11]

Powers's reference concerns perception. Matching perception to a reference does not by itself establish correspondence to every external fact.

Deciding who should have the conversation#

The Acas guide treats preparation as a decision about who has the authority and information to address a problem. Its timekeeping scenario follows a sales manager who has kept a record of an employee’s late arrivals and long lunches. The manager begins by asking if anything is wrong. The employee says all is well. When the manager produces the record, the employee disputes its accuracy and asks why he is being singled out. As he leaves, he mentions that his wife has been unwell. A few days later he says he will meet again only with a union representative and is considering a grievance. [T08-W001]

Acas uses this instructional scenario to ask whether the manager had raised the issue earlier, told the employee about the monitoring or noticed a change in his behavior. The guide credits the opening question and the use of specific evidence. The scenario ends with the terms of a second meeting in dispute. [T08-W001]

Reasons for avoiding an exchange#

King, Edlington and Williams interviewed 20 clinical supervisors in Australia about avoiding difficult conversations. Participants spoke of feared consequences, dislike of confrontation and limited confidence. The authors used convergent interviews to examine explanations across participants. The organizational work in S51 gives a broader account of the risks that can accompany speaking up. [T08-W002]

Changing the next turn#

初鹿野 and 岩田 examine an interaction in which one person speaks about another while addressing a third. In their second case, Fujii addresses Doi about the reliability of Egawa’s account. Egawa enters the exchange before the selected addressee. The analysis follows gaze, address and the timing of overlapping talk to explain why the entry matters. By taking a turn before Doi’s response, Egawa alters the immediate course of the exchange. The authors interpret that entry as a way of checking the continuation of teasing. [T08-W003]

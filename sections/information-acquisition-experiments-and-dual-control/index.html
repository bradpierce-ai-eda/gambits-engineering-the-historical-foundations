Information for a specified decision#

The selected sources concern the information needed to estimate, decide or control. They also describe situations in which information is not a free input: obtaining it can require an experiment, a control action or a different distribution of knowledge among decision makers.

Fisher: reduction and the criterion of sufficiency#

In the definitions and §§2–4 of his 1922 paper, Fisher separates the population characteristics to be estimated from statistics calculated from a sample. This distinction addresses a practical ambiguity: a sample mean is an observed quantity, while the population parameter is what the statistic is meant to illuminate. He organizes statistical work around specification, estimation and distribution. Specification identifies a mathematical form for the hypothetical population; estimation uses sample data to estimate its parameters; distribution concerns how the resulting statistics vary across samples. [W058, definitions and §§2–4, printed pp. 309–317]

His criterion of sufficiency asks whether any other statistic calculated from the same sample can supply additional information about the parameter being estimated. The relevant reduction is consequently relative to a parameter and a statistical setting. It does not mean that the reduced statistic preserves every conceivable use of the observations. The observations may be compressed without losing the information specified by the criterion.

Fisher's separate criteria prevent several ideas from being treated as synonyms. Consistency, efficiency and sufficiency address different properties. An estimate can be evaluated for sampling behavior without thereby establishing that it contains all the relevant sample information. The historical discussion does not justify declaring every maximum-likelihood estimate sufficient. [W058]

The criterion concerns the statistical information retained for a specified model and parameter. Acquisition, computation, storage, explanation and use remain separate resource questions. A new model or question can require the original observations.

Blackwell's 1951 comparison: attainable losses#

In Comparison of Experiments, Blackwell represents an experiment by a set of probability measures corresponding to possible states or distributions. An observation is used to choose an action. Each action has a loss in each state, and a decision procedure produces a vector of expected losses. Comparing experiments therefore concerns what decision consequences can be attained using their observations, rather than the raw number of observations or their apparent descriptive richness. [W059, §§1–3, printed pp. 93–94]

The construction allows convexification and randomized mixtures of procedures. Its first theorem permits attention to closed convex action sets in the subsequent treatment. Randomized decision capability is part of the mathematical comparison; forbidding it changes the admissible procedure class and can change attainable losses. Blackwell presents equivalent decision criteria for the comparison, including formulations involving expected losses with state weights and worst-state loss. Their meaning depends on the specified class of decision problems.

The standard-experiment construction is especially relevant to information reduction. Blackwell associates an observation with a vector of normalized densities and shows that the corresponding standard experiment has the same attainable loss set in the stated setting. A decision rule on the reduced

representation can be composed with the transformation of the original observation; the converse uses conditional expectation and the convex action set. Thus the claim is not merely that a summary looks informative. It specifies a decision equivalence and the machinery supporting it. [W059, §§4–5, especially theorem 3, printed pp. 94–96]

Blackwell's 1951 summary distinguishes sufficiency-based comparison from the general converse and reports a converse for dichotomies. His later Equivalent Comparisons of Experiments appeared in 1953. [W029; W059]

Börgers: a readable finite reconstruction of dominance#

Börgers's December 2023 notes provide an operative scholarly reconstruction in a finite-state, finite-outcome setting. An experiment P is a Markov matrix: each row gives the distribution of its possible observations in one state. An action is represented by its losses in the states. A decision rule maps observations into actions, and the resulting risk vector gives expected loss state by state. This makes the comparison inspectable in terms of matrices and feasible decisions. [GL-W024, PDF pp. 1–3]

The central garbling condition is PM = Q, where M is a state-independent Markov matrix. It says that the observations of Q can be generated by taking observations of P and applying an additional randomized transformation that does not require knowledge of the true state. A decision maker with P can therefore reproduce the decision procedure available with Q: first carry out the transformation, then apply the Q-based rule. The transformation does not create hidden access to the very state the decision maker is trying to infer. [GL-W024, proposition 1 and explanation, PDF pp. 3–6]

The notes relate this condition to attainable risk vectors and to decision-value comparisons. Their second proposition gives equivalent conditions expressed through decision problems and their values. This gives an explicit bridge from a relation between observation systems to what an actor can achieve.

The notes develop the Blackwell tradition, including Blackwell and Girshick, in a finite setting with convex action possibilities and randomized procedures. [GL-W024, PDF pp. 1–9]

Brooks, Frankel and Kamenica: information already held can change the comparison#

The introduction and early definitions of the March 2024 manuscript distinguish an experiment from a signal. An experiment specifies how data relate to the truth; two separately specified experiments do not determine the joint informational content of their observations. Their use of a signal adds the joint relation to other information. The difference matters when information overlaps or becomes useful only in combination. [W071, introduction and §§2–3.2]

Their newspaper example makes the issue concrete without requiring a theorem proof. If a reader already receives one newspaper, another copy of it need not add as much as a different newspaper. A third publication can also alter the comparison because its reporting overlaps with one source or complements another. An ordering established for sources in isolation need not answer which additional source is more valuable for a reader with existing information.

The authors study comparisons robust both to the decision maker's preferences and to other information the decision maker may possess. Their announced reveal-or-refine condition requires that an observation from the favored signal either reveal the truth or refine what the other signal conveys. [W071]

Information value depends on the knowledge already in place. Two sources can be substitutable or complementary in ways not captured by their separate state–observation distributions.

Saldi and Yüksel: who is allowed to know what#

The introduction to Saldi and Yüksel's review presents information structures as restrictions on which observations each decision maker can use. Their example has several agents, each choosing an action from local information, possibly with an additional independent randomization device. A team cost depends on the underlying state and the agents' actions. The admissible policies must respect the local information rather than draw on an omniscient observer's knowledge. [TA-W004, introduction]

Their proposed probability-theoretic and topological approach studies the distributions induced over states, measurements and actions—strategic measures—and the geometry of feasible decision or control policies. The introduction distinguishes questions of existence, approximation, convexity and numerical bounds. An apparently favorable solution obtained by relaxing the information restrictions need not be implementable by the original agents.

These information structures specify local knowledge, admissible action and a team cost, making an information boundary part of the problem formulation. [TA-W004]

Wittenmark: an action can both control and probe#

Wittenmark's Adaptive Dual Control explains why immediate control and learning about a process can be linked. His stochastic formulation specifies a process model, admissible causal controllers and a loss over a horizon. The model structure and the driving process distribution are assumed known, while some parameters are unknown or changing. The history of inputs and outputs supports parameter estimation. The chosen control signal can affect not only the next output but also the quality of future estimates. [AFC-W012, pp. 1–4]

A certainty-equivalent controller inserts current parameter estimates as though they were the true values in a controller designed for known parameters. A cautious controller additionally responds to uncertainty in those estimates. In the one-step formulation, caution reduces controller gain when parameter uncertainty is high. This can be sensible protection against an unreliable estimate, yet it can also weaken the excitation that supplies information about the process.

Wittenmark describes a resulting turn-off possibility: smaller control signals yield less information; uncertainty can increase; control signals are then reduced further. His integrator example distinguishes the certainty-equivalent controller's difficulty near a zero estimated gain from the cautious controller's tendency toward diminishing action as uncertainty rises. [AFC-W012, pp. 4–6]

Dual control deliberately takes the informational effect of action into account over a longer horizon. An action can have a current cost while improving the basis for later actions. The chapter's summary stresses that the exact optimal problem is computationally intractable in general practice and motivates simpler suboptimal controllers with active probing features. Adding a term that rewards information is not automatically enough: for the intended dual effect it must depend on the control signal being selected and on the quality of the estimates. [AFC-W012, summary and dual-control discussion]

Bayesian design: a limited but explicit decision framing#

The abstract of Chaloner and Verdinelli's review frames experimental design as a decision problem in which the chosen design maximizes expected utility from the experiment. It establishes a relevant orientation: the experiment is selected for an anticipated decision or inferential purpose rather than because more data are inherently better. [AFC-W016]

Statistical and decision criteria#

The accounts above preserve distinct achievements: a parameter-specific sufficiency criterion; attainable-loss comparisons; a finite garbling reconstruction; a qualification involving correlated prior information; admissible local knowledge; and the informational effect of control action. Each gives a different way of making information operationally precise.

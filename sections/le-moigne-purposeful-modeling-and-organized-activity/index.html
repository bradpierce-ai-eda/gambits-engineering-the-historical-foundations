A theory of modeling and its changing layers#

Jean-Louis Le Moigne's La théorie du système général: Théorie de la modélisation develops a connected account of contextual purpose, representation, organized processes, memory, coordination and adaptation. The relevant material concerns both how a model can economize inquiry and how a modeled arrangement can change the demands on its participants.

The 2006 presentation of the 1994 edition includes earlier layers, later rereadings, a postface and appendices. [UX-W027]

The 2006 preface positions the enterprise as a theory of systemic modeling rather than a universal ontology declaring what every natural system really is. The model is constructed for a project in a context. Ends, means, representations and reasoning can recursively revise one another. Finalization therefore includes the ongoing formation of purposes, rather than merely execution toward an eternally fixed goal. Choosing those purposes remains an epistemic and ethical responsibility. [UX-W027, PDF 13–15, 35–47]

The book does not present this as a finished formal calculus. It acknowledges incomplete tools for organization and memory, borrows extensively and permits plural methods. Its polemics challenge the dominance of analytical science. Classical methods remain available where appropriate; the objection is to their exclusive authority over every modeling task. [UX-W027, PDF 27, 35–47, 63]

A representation is selected for a question#

Le Moigne's precepts concern relevance to intentions, attention to a larger context, purposes and deliberate aggregation. A model includes selected traits and relations, rather than reproducing its object in every respect. Complexity consequently concerns the representation and its question, not simply a count of variables possessed by an object independently of every observer. The choice of what to aggregate can simplify inquiry or conceal what makes the question difficult. [UX-W027, PDF 51–69]

His “General System” is a modeling artifact, distinct from the aspiration to describe a single universal System-Universe. Context, project, activity, structure and changing identity enter the definition of the modeled object. His triangulation asks what it is, what it does and what it becomes. The point is to keep several aspects in relation, rather than infer function solely from a parts list or treat one instantaneous picture as the complete history. [UX-W027, PDF 70–94]

Systemography then constructs a particular model with reference to this general modeling device and to the observed object. Empirical correspondence is partial and purpose-dependent. Internal consistency and usefulness are different questions: a coherent formal construction can fail to address the intended situation. Conversely, a limited model can be useful without being a complete causal reconstruction. [UX-W027, PDF 95–110]

The observer and the representation-making process can themselves become objects of modeling. Intentions, values and disciplinary conventions affect what is selected and what a measure is taken to mean. Le Moigne's discussion of economic indicators, for example, refuses to let one aggregate stand for every dimension of well-being. That is an argument for explicit purposes and interpretive accountability. [UX-W027, PDF 107–110]

Functional use can require less internal knowledge#

The calculator illustration challenges the claim that useful understanding always requires dissecting every internal component. A user can interpret operations, improve use, carry out some upkeep or connect a calculator with another device through knowledge of its behavior. Complete electronics knowledge is not required for each such task. This is a specified economy in what a particular user must know for a particular operation. [UX-W027, PDF 58]

A black-box description can support use while leaving the internal physical relations needed for diagnosis unspecified. Its adequacy depends on the task. [UX-W027, PDF 58–63]

Le Moigne economizes modeling by choosing relatively weakly connected regions while retaining their relevant environmental relations. [UX-W027, PDF 105–106]

Processors make transport and transformation explicit#

The process framework distinguishes changes in time, space and form: storage, transport and transformation. Matter, energy and information can be processed, while a processor can itself become the object of another process. The vocabulary permits recursion. The modeler selects the processes needed for the question. [UX-W027, PDF 111–125]

Networks of processors represent activities and their relations. A processor can be multifunctional, and the boundaries of a selected subnetwork must retain its connections to surroundings. The network is an account of organized transformations, not merely a picture of boxes. A matrix showing instantaneous connections cannot by itself replace a description of delays or the sequence in which connections are used. [UX-W027, PDF 126–149]

Le Moigne's ideal neutral connector is a modeling convention. Actual transport requires activity, which can be represented by its own processor. Elsewhere he expressly treats a real communication channel as a processor. The distinction between a schematic relation and the machinery that realizes it is part of the book's account. [UX-W027, PDF 134–136, 256]

Transduction similarly requires specific energy. Events must be turned into signals and signals into events through actual operations. Le Moigne proposes distinguishing maintenance or logistical processors to represent the provision and upkeep of processors themselves. In a simplified diagram maintenance can be aggregated into the operating subsystem, an explicit representational choice. [UX-W027, PDF 164, 171 note 2, 196]

A separate decision box is not always required#

The book's nine levels move through passive, active, regulated, informed, deciding, memorizing, coordinating, imagining and self-finalizing descriptions. They are a pedagogical modeling construction. Information attributed to a modeled object also differs from the modeler's own knowledge of it. [UX-W027, PDF 150–173]

The Watt-governor discussion makes a more concrete claim. A predictable algorithmic operation can be embodied in operating structure rather than retained in an additional artificial decision processor. A separate decision box may be a useful representation but is not always necessary. [UX-W027, PDF 162]

When the relevant logic is not completely specified, the account instead retains heuristics, memory of earlier events and a question of when to stop. The child's ability to continue spelling without knowing where to finish illustrates the missing termination judgment. Decisions can require history and purpose, rather than only a transformation of the present input. Le Moigne acknowledges that imagination and self-finalization are only preparatorily represented by this architecture. [UX-W027, PDF 162, 168–171]

The book considers differentiated projects and nonhierarchical possibilities while admitting limits to its formal tools. Its categories help pose who coordinates what, using which information and capabilities. [UX-W027, PDF 164–171]

Programmes reduce description by retaining a rule#

The trajectory discussion replaces a large sequence of state pictures with an initial picture and a schedule of parameter changes. An animation need not store every frame independently if a representation and programme specify how the parts move. The economy lies in the selected description and its reusable rule. [UX-W027, PDF 176–190, especially 182]

Organization is then described through structure together with memorized programmes. Memory here is a modeling hypothesis and need not correspond to a literal genome or a distinct physical memory organ. The description can preserve a functional relation across different material realizations. [UX-W027, PDF 191–196]

Common programmes, codes and representations can simplify a network of decision communications. Instead of requiring each participant to obtain a separately tailored instruction through a complicated chain, participants can access a shared resource. Le Moigne describes a common representation of operations and a pool of available programmes as possible supports for coordination. [UX-W027, PDF 203 –206]

He immediately preserves a crucial distinction: storing a programme does not make it execute. Memory and deciding are different processes. A code must be established and remain common, representations must be maintained, participants need access, and current purposes must guide selection. [UX-W027, PDF 203–204]

The book also recognizes disagreement about both means and ends. A shared store is not a proof that participants share values or accept the same action. Coalition and conflict questions remain. The interpretation of common memory as more democratic or more economical is attributed to the sources discussed, rather than established as a measured general effect. [UX-W027, PDF 199–208]

Stability can come from absorption, correction or anticipation#

Stability is relative to selected purposes and criteria. Le Moigne distinguishes maintaining behavior through an existing structure, changing programmes, modifying the structure, and changing purposes or environmental relations. Treating all of these as the same feedback operation obscures the different activities involved. [UX-W027, PDF 217–237]

He explicitly corrects an overbroad claim for feedback by presenting three routes to regulation. The first absorbs or decouples disturbances through buffers, shields, insulation or similar arrangements. The second corrects deviations through a returning loop. The third anticipates and compensates for disturbances. The modeler is advised to consider these alternatives rather than automatically installing a feedback account. [UX-W027, PDF 223–224]

Shells and buffers attenuate disturbances before they reach the operating process, reducing the occasions for a corrective response. [UX-W027, PDF 223–224]

Adaptation can require stored resources and spare capacity. Le Moigne describes stable regions in which a trial response can be attempted, evaluated and retained or rejected. If almost everything changes faster than the trial, its consequences become difficult to learn. Excessive coupling can remove those regions and consume the slack needed for new connections. Stability and learning are therefore connected under specified timing conditions, rather than simple opposites. [UX-W027, PDF 225–226]

Le Moigne distinguishes effectiveness relative to purposes from input/output efficiency: maintaining a stable outcome or reducing an input can serve different purposes. [UX-W027, PDF 228–234]

Sparse links can require complicated programmes#

Le Moigne's differentiation–coordination discussion identifies a concrete tension. A very sparse tree uses few links but can require elaborate indirect sequences to accomplish interactions for which a direct connection is absent. A complete mesh supplies more possible routes but can exceed cognitive and relational capacity. The economical link diagram and the economical programme need not coincide. [UX-W027, PDF 260–277, especially 268]

The proposed design task is consequently to organize the relation between network and programme. New connections can enlarge what coordinators can do, while too many overwhelm them. Missing links can force fragile detours. Resources, purposes, communication capacities and timing must be specified. [UX-W027, PDF 268]

Variety is not necessarily absorbed only at the top. Local arrangements can deal with distinctions before they reach a wider coordinator, and the rate of relevant change matters as well as the number of possibilities. As the Ashby account in S03 explains, not every state distinction requires an individually commanded response, and mere possession of alternatives is not sufficient for appropriate regulation. [UX-W027, PDF 269–271; S03]

The warning about using human beings to absorb the unmodeled residue is particularly direct. An apparently complete technical arrangement can depend on a person's continuing cognitive and relational work to cope with what it omits. Naming a coordination subsystem does not specify that capacity or establish that the job can be done at the required rate. [UX-W027, PDF 270]

His discussions of Simon, Galbraith and Voge are attributed connections within this framework. They help explain conditional assembly, information demand and autonomy with solidarity. [UX-W027, PDF 270– 277]

Formal definitions and quantitative claims#

Some formal definitions in the book are loose. A bijection alone does not establish an isomorphism preserving the relevant structure, and the printed descriptions of homomorphism and polymorphism contain inconsistent mapping language. [UX-W027, PDF 101]

The Voge passage also places two different quantities near one another: a proposed connection count and a number of possible configurations. They cannot serve as lower and upper bounds on the same measure without further definitions. Similarly, the toy performance expression whose normalized contact fraction peaks halfway does not establish universally optimal fifty-percent connectivity. [UX-W027, PDF 147–148, 215]

The book acknowledges the absence of sufficiently general metrics and rejects one scalar measure of organization. Its entropy, variety, information, value and energy analogies concern different quantities. Schematic curves do not establish that every noise input improves organization. [UX-W027, PDF 215, 240 –259, especially 247, 251]

The chapters on evolution and creative time retain changing agendas, historical codes and values. Le Moigne labels important claims as hypotheses and leaves finalization unfinished. The concluding contrast between design, analysis and simulation concerns different intentional uses of models. [UX-W027, PDF 278–301]

Morin through Le Moigne#

Le Moigne explicitly reconstructs organization through capacities to transform, produce, relate and maintain identity, drawing on Morin's La Méthode, volume 1. He connects producing with self-producing and describes active organization in relation to surroundings. [RGC-W028 through UX-W027, PDF 39–42, 209–214]

The second volume is related through autonomy that requires dependence and through auto-eco-reorganization. Self-maintenance involves environmental relations; greater organization does not imply escape from them. [RGC-W029 through UX-W027, PDF 209–214, 276–290]

Critics: accessibility and the need for internal mechanisms#

Motulsky's 1978 review praises a documented and coherent synthesis while finding the entry difficult. Examples are insufficient for an introductory reader, parts resist isolation, and the exposition makes nonlinear demands. The review concerns the 1977 first edition, rather than the later 2006 additions. [RGC-W024, 217–218]

Braudeau's critical discussion addresses a different issue. For the physical delimitation and explanation of the water–soil system, he objects to an anti-Cartesian or black-box orientation that can leave internal mechanisms inadequately specified. He retains triangulation and operating, information and pilot models while recasting the description around a structural representative volume. The account shows how a model useful for one purpose may omit physical relations necessary for another. [RGC-W026, §3.1, printed pp. 16–25/PDF pp. 18–27]

Braudeau's stronger assertion that a black-box approach prevents an account of organization must be read alongside Le Moigne's explicit internal networks, programmes and permission to use classical methods where appropriate. [RGC-W026; UX-W027, PDF 63, 126–149, 191–216]

The author's later retrospective#

Le Moigne's 2015 chronicle derives from a revised 2014 oral account, with an editorial introduction by Dominique Genelot. It describes the formation and later development of the modeling programme. As autobiography, it identifies how the author understood his own questions. [RGC-W025]

The recollection of industrial optimization is especially pertinent. Fragile estimates entered elaborate models, while a single profit criterion organized the calculation. Le Moigne's concern was both the reliability of inputs and the choice of what was being optimized. Sophisticated computation cannot by itself decide whether omitted factors are negligible or whether one objective captures the purposes of the organization's members. The account is a criticism of the problem formulation. [RGC-W025, PDF 2]

He later distinguishes the published model from the situated, tentative process of constructing it. Merely replacing the word “model” with “modeling” can leave that work unexamined. His retrospective relates practical action, epistemic justification and ethical reflection, and treats algorithmic methods as possible heuristics within a wider inquiry. The testimony supports an account of his methodological intention while leaving model adequacy to the purposes, evidence and criticism relevant to each application. [RGC-W025, PDF 5–6]

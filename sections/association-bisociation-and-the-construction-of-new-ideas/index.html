Accounts and measurement of creative connection#

Two frames for one situation#

Koestler’s The Act of Creation distinguishes association within an accustomed frame from apprehending a situation through two self-consistent but habitually incompatible frames. A matrix is a pattern of possible activities; its code supplies governing rules; strategy is the particular course taken within those rules. His chess comparison separates the rules that define a game from the many ways a player may proceed. Bisociation brings frames into an intersection that ordinary conduct within either frame would not supply. [NC-W008]

The distinction gives surprise a structure. In the opening jokes, a situation that appears intelligible under one social code is reinterpreted through another. A reply can be reasonable within one frame while overturning the expectation established by the other. The connection is more specific than the occurrence of two thoughts: a situation is made to belong to two organized patterns of meaning or action. [NC-W008]

Koestler deliberately extends this account across humour, scientific discovery and artistic creation. The domains differ in what happens when frames meet, but his theoretical ambition is to describe creative activity through their encounters. Matrix, code and strategy preserve room for established organization as well as a course that departs from habitual expectations. Learning a domain’s code and discovering an unexpected intersection are consequently distinguishable activities within his account. [NC-W008]

His printing-press discussion connects practices of making impressions with the use of a press. The example makes a relationship between established activities, rather than a wholly unprecedented component, central to the proposed creative episode. Koestler presents a historical reconstruction of this connection. It explains his argument without requiring his account of Gutenberg’s private reflections to be taken as an independently authenticated discovery record. [NC-W008]

The opening of his discussion of false inspirations also separates the experience of an idea arriving from a connection that survives development. An apparent intersection can be suggestive before its implications have been worked through. Elaboration therefore belongs within the theory’s own account of creation. The sudden interpretation is part of a longer activity in which its usefulness, coherence or expressive possibilities become clearer. [NC-W008]

A concert contract seen through two financial problems#

Chabrillat, Lecoutre and Lièvre follow a managerial innovation at the concert venue they call Le Cabestan. A changed grant rule replaces a fixed subsidy with an amount calculated as a percentage of actual expenditure. At the same time, reopening the venue requires renewed relations with private concert coproducers. The manager initially faces these as separate financial and commercial problems. [NC-W019]

Revised coproduction agreements allow the venue to pay some production expenses directly while maintaining the agreed financial result and profit sharing with a partner. This reassignment can support concessions and negotiations needed to rebuild the commercial relationship. Expenses are not simply renamed; who pays and records them changes under the contract. [NC-W019]

A tense conversation with the financial manager connects that arrangement to the grant problem. Expenditure now borne by the venue affects the base used to calculate its public subsidy. A practice developed in the private coproduction relationship thus becomes consequential under the public-

financing rule. The connected operation depends on both the contractual allocation and the particular subsidy basis. [NC-W019]

The authors trace partner responses, contract versions and stabilization of the practice. Their concurrent observation and consulting involvement from February 2014 to March 2015 supplied meetings, interviews and documents as the activity developed. They interpret the case through matrices of public financing and private coproduction, with stages of an unresolved problem, a creative clash and validation in practice. Expertise in both domains and the longer work of stabilization are part of that interpretation. [NC-W019]

Associative routes and practical requirements#

Mednick describes creative thinking as combining associative elements in ways that meet specified requirements or prove useful. Remoteness contributes to his account of creative quality, but an unusual conjunction is not useful solely because it is remote. Serendipitous contiguity, similarity and mediation provide different routes by which elements can be brought together. His account of steep and flat associative hierarchies concerns how readily common and more remote responses become available. Requisite knowledge remains important to what can be connected. [NC-W010]

The refrigerator example keeps practical requirements beside the association. Preventing frost is different from reacting after it has accumulated, but a proposal never to open the door defeats the refrigerator’s purpose. A counter responding to door openings suggests a different way of controlling defrosting. The useful connection concerns an event associated with frost accumulation and an operation that can be controlled, while retaining the appliance’s ordinary use. [NC-W010]

The Remote Associates Test turns mediation into a research task. A participant finds an associate connecting three cues; in Mednick’s example, rat, blue and cottage can each be joined with cheese. A candidate that fits one cue must be tested against the others. The task gives associative theory an operational instrument, with a specific kind of successful answer. [NC-W010]

What an association test measures#

Wu, Huang, Chen and Chen’s systematic review retained 172 studies from 256 screened records covering 2000–2019. The review shows RAT tasks being used for different research purposes and with differing relations among cues. Producing candidate associates can involve divergent search; finding one that satisfies all cues involves convergence. Vocabulary, retrieval and overcoming a prominent wrong answer can also influence the result. A test score therefore needs to be interpreted through the task and version that produced it. [NC-W011]

王硕阳、林崇德 and 孙汉银 distinguish what is connected from the efficiency with which a connection is retrieved. Language-based co-occurrence differs from relations built through experience, function or shared features. Their account develops partly independent associative abilities and different roles in problems that do or do not require restructuring. A person’s facility with conventional verbal relations need not capture every way that relations can support a new problem representation. [NC-W012]

西田勇樹、服部雅史 and 織田涼 compare a compound-word RAT with insight problems, alternative uses, vocabulary and cognitive reflection. Their version requires a character that makes compounds with each cue. After vocabulary was controlled, their first experiment did not find the expected association with the combined insight or creativity tasks. The result concerns those tasks and the contribution vocabulary makes to answering them. [NC-W013]

Their second experiment retained 68 of 91 recruited participants and found a relation to making fewer typical cognitive-reflection errors. The relation with the correct-answer score did not persist in the same way after vocabulary control. The authors resist a binary verdict that RAT either is or is not insight: insight tasks themselves need not correlate closely. Their discussion gives inhibiting a salient but wrong response a visible role alongside vocabulary and associative search. [NC-W013]

Constructing and interpreting ideas#

Making the familiar strange and returning to the problem#

Gordon’s Synectics seeks to make deliberate work possible around activities often described only as intuition. Making the strange familiar is the analytic effort to understand a problem. Making the familiar strange changes its habitual representation, allowing a group to approach it through a different experience or image. The departure is followed by a return to the technical problem; an excursion that never returns would leave the original task unanswered. [NC-W009]

Personal analogy asks participants to identify with an element or process. Direct analogy draws on another phenomenon. Symbolic analogy compresses a problem into an evocative expression or image, while fantasy analogy imagines a desired fulfilment. Gordon treats these mechanisms as ways to encourage useful psychological states, not simply four labels for any unusual comparison. The mechanism chosen affects how the group leaves its accustomed account of the problem. [NC-W009]

Appendix II joins the initial problem, analytic understanding, excursions through the mechanisms, integration with the problem, a new viewpoint and a solution or research target. Some targets require a model that tests a principle; others require work on materials. The return to a technical viewpoint can change or reverse part of the image that prompted it. What is retained is a relation useful to construction, rather than a demand that a product imitate every feature of the analogy. [NC-W009]

From an imagined stitch to a space-suit closure#

The space-suit session concerns an opening that must close strongly and reliably. Familiar fasteners give the group an established set of possibilities but do not settle the task. Insect and stitching imagery opens a different way of thinking about joining the sides. The group’s responses move between imagined conduct and the load the closure must bear. [NC-W009]

The discussion develops steel stitching into overlapping springs embedded in the rubber lips of the opening. A wire passed through the springs can pull the sides together. This proposal gives the image an arrangement of materials and connections: the opposed lips carry the springs, the springs overlap, and the wire supplies the joining action. [NC-W009]

The load requirement brings the excursion back to the suit. The mechanism must answer the difficulty that made an alternative to ordinary fastening worth seeking. Gordon’s account ends with a proposed construction, so the significant result is the route from an unfamiliar image to a technically describable closure. The session’s ingenuity lies in the successive interpretations and their return to the original requirement. [NC-W009]

A roof that changes its exposed colour#

A second session asks for a roof that is light in hot weather and dark in cold weather. Changes in animal appearance supply possible analogies, but the time scale matters. A seasonal coat does not give the rapid response wanted for the roof. Chromatophores suggest coloured elements whose exposure can change instead. [NC-W009]

The technical viewpoint transforms that suggestion. White elements embedded in a black roof would expand into view when heated, changing what the surface exposes. The construction does not copy the animal unchanged; it selects a relation between temperature, element behaviour and visible colour, and reverses part of the analogy for the building’s purpose. [NC-W009]

Gordon presents a research target for materials and construction. The group has obtained something more definite than an animal metaphor: a proposed response and arrangement whose realization can be investigated. His account thus connects imaginative departure with a specification for further technical work. [NC-W009]

A form before its purpose#

Finke’s constrained imagery task asks participants to combine three selected object parts into a form before deciding what useful object it will become. Size, material and some relations can change while specified geometric constraints are retained. The result is a preinventive structure, open to interpretation rather than a drawing of a finished purpose already chosen. [NC-W015]

Supplying an interpretation category after form generation changes what the participant is doing. A structure first assembled without that category can subsequently be explored for functions that were not its initial rationale. Broad functional categories also leave different interpretive possibilities from narrow object categories. Finke reports especially creative inventions under conditions that delayed the category, judged through originality and practicality ratings. [NC-W015]

The operation is construction followed by a search for meaning under constraints. A part can acquire a role not apparent when it was selected, and its relation to another part can suggest a function for the whole. Interpretation then supports refinement, expansion or the generation of a different structure. Delaying a category in this task preserves a particular set of possibilities; it does not imply that every purpose should be postponed in every design problem. [NC-W015]

Geneplore makes the return paths explicit. Generation produces a preinventive structure; exploration interprets it under product constraints, and the interpretation can lead back to further generation. Constraints act on both activities. The process distinguishes a suggestive form from a developed invention while giving the former an active role in producing the latter. [NC-W015]

Comparing taught methods#

Azadyekta compared brainstorming, forced association and synectics with a control condition in 160 elementary pupils. Eight weeks of training were assessed with pre- and post-training Torrance pictorial tests. All three trained groups improved on the reported measure relative to the control pattern, while the overall differences among the trained methods were not statistically significant. This is a comparison of taught activities and task scores in those conditions. It supplies a different kind of evidence from Gordon’s accounts of technical sessions and preserves the distinction between improving on a measure and establishing a general ranking among invention methods. [NC-W018]

Exploring a space and changing what it permits#

Boden’s 1997 account distinguishes what is new to an individual from what is new in recorded history. A person can produce an idea without copying it even when someone else has already produced it. That distinction separates a psychological question about the individual’s creative activity from a historical question about priority. [NC-W014]

The paper organizes its discussion under combinational creativity and exploratory-transformational creativity. Exploration works within a conceptual space of permitted structures and operations.

Transformation changes that space so that something previously excluded becomes possible. The distinction concerns the representation that defines what can be generated, rather than only whether the resulting item looks unfamiliar. [NC-W014]

Computer examples make the representation consequential. Permitted structures, generative operations and changes to them affect a program’s productions; Copycat illustrates flexible representations. Evaluation remains a separate issue involving values and historical or cultural context. Boden’s account therefore connects producing possibilities to the conditions under which they can count as valuable, without treating a generator’s output count as an adequate assessment of creativity. [NC-W014]

A combinatorial account of discovery#

Simonton proposes a combinatorial account of scientific discovery and invention that includes activities Boden distinguishes as exploration and transformation. His formalization concerns an individual scientist’s subjective episode. It defines p as the initial probability or response strength of an idea, u as its final utility and v as prior knowledge of that utility, each ranging from zero to one. The proposed creativity measure is c = (1 − p)u(1 − v). [NC-W016]

The expression assigns its maximum to a useful idea that initially had low probability and whose usefulness was little known beforehand. An unlikely candidate is insufficient if it proves useless; a useful result whose utility was already known has a different status in the model. The extreme combinations distinguish such situations as expertise, blind problem solving, lucky guesses and perseveration. They expose the difference between producing a candidate and knowing that it will work. [NC-W016]

The formalization is Simonton’s proposed way of characterizing creative episodes, with its variables and interpretation kept together. His discussion of optimization and no-free-lunch reasoning depends on the averaging assumptions of that argument. His broader account and Boden’s conceptual-space account remain distinct theoretical positions. [NC-W016]

Running an imagined combination#

Fauconnier and Turner describe mental spaces as partial representations constructed for local understanding. Counterparts can be mapped between input spaces, a generic space captures shared structure, and a blend selectively projects elements from the inputs. Composition forms relations, completion recruits background frames, and elaboration runs the imagined situation. Emergent structure in the blend can then inform the understanding of an input. [NC-W017]

Their monk walks up a path on one day and down on another, with arbitrary changes in pace and stops. The question is whether a location is occupied at the same time of day on both journeys. Imagine the journeys occurring simultaneously, with two monks moving in opposite directions on the same path. They must meet. Mapping the meeting back to the separate days supplies the shared time and location. [NC-W017]

The inference depends on selective combination: the path and time of day are shared in the imagined scene, while the two journeys remain represented by distinct travellers. Running that scene makes a relation apparent that was harder to see in the separate accounts. The model explains the representational work of the inference rather than merely naming combination as its cause. [NC-W017]

Literature-based discovery and computational bridges#

Finding a hypothesis between disconnected literatures#

Swanson and Smalheiser ask what may follow from complementary literatures that do not cite one another. One literature connects A with B; another connects B with C. Reading them together can suggest an A–C hypothesis that neither states. The absence of citation concerns the public literature and does not establish that no person has privately made the connection. Nor do the two links alone prove the proposed hypothesis. [NC-W020]

ARROWSMITH begins with provisional A and C literatures, collects MEDLINE title files, filters shared-word candidates and displays titles and citations for guided reading. The expert interprets whether a common term marks a substantive connection. The representation and filtering make a large set of possible links examinable; interpretation identifies which might be scientifically complementary. [NC-W020]

The authors recount the fish-oil and Raynaud connection through literatures involving blood viscosity and report later corroboration by others. Their retrospective account preserves the difference between proposing a relation from public knowledge and testing it subsequently. A separate migraine/magnesium history supplies a benchmark for later computational work on recovering bridge terms. [NC-W020]

Representing and ranking bridges#

Kötter and Berthold’s information networks represent heterogeneous concepts, relations and pieces of evidence. A network can accommodate incompletely typed information, with weights expressing confidence in evidence. The representation provides relations that can be explored across domains without requiring every link to be treated as a causal assertion. [NC-W021]

Their bisociative patterns are distinct. A bridging concept links dense groups; a bridging graph connects domains through several elements or returns through another domain; structurally similar disconnected subgraphs supply a comparison without an existing connecting edge. The last pattern is especially different from finding a shared word: the relation lies in how structures correspond. [NC-W021]

Juršič and colleagues begin with two known literatures, represent documents as term vectors and identify outliers classified toward the other domain. These documents may contain ideas atypical of their labelled field and closer to the other. Frequency, similarity and outlier-related heuristics then rank candidate terms. Selected relations can be turned into a weighted network with pruning options. [NC-W022]

Their retrospective migraine/magnesium experiment uses 8,058 pre-1988 titles and 43 historical bridging terms. All tested rankings already put terms shared by both domains ahead of other terms, which gives the baseline a substantial advantage. Improvements in ranking concern recovery of known bridges, not a percentage of correct new hypotheses. The constructed network supports examination of possible relations after that retrieval step. [NC-W022]

Topics as sources of design inspiration#

Ahmed and Fuge use topics, represented by collections of words, rather than isolated bridging words. Their data contain 3,918 OpenIDEO ideas across fourteen challenges. Topic proportions represent each idea; a classifier identifies ideas that resemble another challenge more than their assigned one; topics prominent in these outliers receive higher bisociation scores. Topic co-occurrence and proportion relations supply network edges. Both the classifier and the bridging stage affect what the resulting network proposes. [NC-W023]

The approach seeks inspirational frames across several domains without requiring a designer to nominate only two literatures in advance. In the city-vibrancy example, a gardening and urban-farming topic prompts an idea for a community garden on vacant land, growing and selling produce. A paired response to a common topic centres on city, government and economic activity. The example shows a change in the proposed idea, not construction of a garden by the network. [NC-W023]

One experiment compares perceived promise of topics. Another asks workers to generate paired ideas from bridging and common topics, with other workers judging the results in two challenges. Novelty generally benefits, while quality findings are mixed and depend on the challenge and prompt. The authors thus connect a computational representation, a source of inspiration and an observed judgment of resulting ideas, with each stage retaining its own outcome. [NC-W023]

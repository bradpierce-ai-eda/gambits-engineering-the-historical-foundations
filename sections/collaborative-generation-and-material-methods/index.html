An observation written on a card can be moved beside another observation, given a new heading and connected to a larger account. A workshop can supply pictures of technologies, invite children to explain their schoolbags to imaginary visitors, or let designers build a proposal from physical pieces. These procedures give participants something to work on together. Their histories show how the preparation of material, the framing of a task and the opportunities for rearrangement enter into creative work.

Kawakita's field cards and the KJ method#

In a 2003 interview with Tetsuro Matsuzawa and Yoko Yamada, Jiro Kawakita recalled the fieldwork behind the KJ method. He described transferring observations from a notebook to separate cards, a practice he traced to his field investigations in 1946. A notebook preserves a sequence of recording. Cards allow observations to be read in different sequences and assembled according to relationships that become apparent later. The interview presents this development through Kawakita's recollections of his own research. [CRE-W030, pp. 14–16]

The movable statement retained information about its circumstances: time, place, source and recorder. Rearrangement did not require stripping the observation of its origin. Kawakita emphasized repeated reading of the cards and attention to what they actually said. Statements that seemed close in meaning could be brought together, and a heading could express the message emerging from their association. The heading was preferably a sentence conveying something substantive, rather than a noun that merely named a subject. [CRE-W030, pp. 14–16]

Successive groupings made larger patterns available for consideration. Arranging the material could also expose a gap: an apparent relationship might require information the researcher did not yet possess. The work could therefore lead back to inquiry. Kawakita's account joins the manipulation of records, the interpretation of their relationships and further observation within an ongoing investigation. [CRE-W030, pp. 14–16]

Hiroaki Tanaka's 2013 quick manual specifies a sequence for carrying out KJ work, based on Kawakita's later exposition. It begins by putting one message on each label. Labels are numbered so that they can be related to the underlying data. They are spread out and read before grouping begins. The initial groups are small and arise from similarities of meaning in the statements. A classification prepared in advance would bypass the interpretive work the procedure is intended to support. [CRE-W029, pp. 102–104]

Each small group receives a heading expressing what its labels say together. A label that does not fit can remain on its own. The headings can then be treated as material for another round of grouping, producing larger groups without requiring every statement to enter the same hierarchy. Different colors and the retention of the lower-level labels help participants keep track of these successive operations. The emerging account can be traced through its headings to the statements from which it was developed. [CRE-W029, pp. 103–105]

The groups are next placed spatially to make their relationships intelligible. Positioning comes before an elaborate network of lines; the manual recommends using relations economically rather than connecting everything to everything. A diagram gives the arrangement a visible form, and prose develops an explicit account of what it means. Writing is thus a further operation on the material, requiring the interpreter to state relationships that a suggestive diagram might leave implicit. [CRE-W029, pp. 105–106]

The sequence gives several distinct tasks their own working forms: a label states a message, a heading expresses a group's meaning, a diagram displays relationships, and prose articulates an interpretation. Their combination is what makes Tanaka's account more useful than the instruction to sort observations

into clusters. It explains both how the material is transformed and how earlier statements remain available during that transformation. [CRE-W029, pp. 102–106]

Preparing cards and inventing a workshop situation#

Michael Mose Biskjaer, Peter Dalsgaard and Kim Halskov examined creativity methods in design through nine components: process structure, materials, tools, combination, metaphor, analogy, framing, divergence and convergence. Their 2017 analysis grouped these into concrete, conceptual and design-space aspects. A workshop's instructions become more intelligible when considered with the material supplied, the situation participants are asked to imagine and the forms of development expected from them. [CRE-W031]

Their account of the Inspiration Card Workshop begins before participants assemble. Organizers prepare Domain Cards representing knowledge of the setting, often obtained through field studies, and Technology Cards representing technologies or particular applications. Each card carries an image and may include a short text. Selecting familiar technologies can make the workshop easier to enter; selecting unfamiliar or provocative ones can unsettle established expectations. Preparation helps determine the possibilities participants will encounter. [CRE-W031, analysis of Inspiration Card Workshops]

In the workshop, the cards are introduced and then combined to develop design concepts. Participants record emerging ideas on notes and collect them on poster-sized sheets. The main creative phase has room for different ways of working rather than prescribing every operation. Concepts are presented and discussed, but this procedure does not require reducing them to a winning selection. Afterwards, posters can be scanned and distributed. The physical combination of prepared cards, discussion around a developing concept and preservation of its representation occupy different parts of the sequence. [CRE-W031, analysis of Inspiration Card Workshops]

Fictional Inquiry changes the social situation in which participants contribute. In one case described by Biskjaer and colleagues, designers acted as Martians visiting Earth, and primary-school children explained their schoolbags to them. Children could no longer assume that their adult interlocutors already understood an ordinary bag or its use. The fictional visitors gave them a reason to articulate familiar experiences and imagine alternatives while contributing to the design of digitally enhanced schoolbags. [CRE-W031, analysis of Fictional Inquiry]

Another workshop asked participants to help the king of Atlantis understand what humans found extraordinary. Seashells, a scepter and other props supported exploration of interactive aquarium exhibits. The fictional situation supplied roles and objects through which participants could develop ideas; those ideas then had to be related to the real aquarium. The authors describe a method that adapts its narrative to a particular design setting, with the connection back to that setting as an important part of the work. [CRE-W031, analysis of Fictional Inquiry]

Their third example, Extreme Characters, exaggerates the people imagined in a design scenario. A proposed appointment manager might be explored through an unconventional character whose jewelry becomes a temporary stand-in for the device. Touching a ring can suggest an interaction such as moving between appointments. The procedure uses role, appearance, action and props together to provoke a different view of a user group. Biskjaer and colleagues treat such methods as combinations of interacting features, and their examples explain why a label such as analogy alone would leave much of the workshop undescribed. [CRE-W031, analysis of Extreme Characters and discussion]

What analogue tools supply#

Dorian Peters, Lian Loke and Naseem Ahmadpour reviewed analogue tools for collaborative ideation. Their review selected 76 tools from research and commercial sources and illustrated a smaller set in the article. They distinguished seven types: methods, prompts, components, concepts, stories, embodiment and construction. A card explaining a method, a question that provokes a response and a physical component to assemble offer different forms of assistance, even when all are described as creativity tools. [CRE-W032, §§4–5]

These distinctions concern what a participant can do with the supplied material. Prompts stimulate responses; components can be combined into a developing proposal; concepts make potentially relevant knowledge available. Stories give participants situations or sequences to develop. The format alone does not determine the activity: two decks of cards may support substantially different kinds of work. The review records features such as a tool's focus, availability and supporting studies alongside its type. [CRE-W032, §§5.1–5.3]

Embodiment and construction extend the account beyond printed cards. Reduced-visibility glasses and reduced-mobility gloves in an inclusive-design toolkit let designers experience particular difficulties while handling a product. Construction materials allow participants to make and discuss a representation. The authors distinguish generic building pieces from LEGO Serious Play's combination of a prescribed method and a purpose-made kit. In each case, the activity depends on the relation between the object and its use. [CRE-W032, §§5.3.5–5.3.6]

The review also draws attention to participation itself. Equipment for simulating an impairment serves a different purpose from tools that enable people with disabilities to participate in design. Peters and colleagues describe examples addressing such participation when discussing gaps and related tools. Their survey makes both material variety and the intended participants visible; it does not rank the 76 tools as though they had all undergone a common effectiveness trial. [CRE-W032, §§5.4.1, 6.2.2]

Working spaces and the continuing design cycle#

Scarlett R. Herring, Brett R. Jones and Brian P. Bailey interviewed ten designers about idea generation throughout their work. Seven interviews took place in project spaces, where the researchers could inspect artifacts, and three took place by telephone. The study identified nineteen techniques and placed them within an account of the design cycle, including implementation and evaluation as well as initial concept generation. [CRE-W033, §§3–5]

Their IR3 model describes research, representation and refinement within an encompassing search for inspiration. Research can produce several concepts; sketches or other external representations let a designer inspect and discuss them; refinement can change what further information is needed. These movements need not occur in long, neatly separated phases. A quick sketch may be evaluated and discarded almost immediately. A concept can concern the definition of the problem as well as a potential solution. [CRE-W033, §4.1]

The techniques reported by the designers include seeking examples, consulting others, acting out situations, manipulating concrete stimuli, documenting ideas and criticizing developing proposals. A critique can generate further ideas by drawing attention to a defect that needs a response. Work on a representation can similarly reveal something that was not apparent when an idea remained verbal. The study presents these activities as recurring parts of professional work rather than as exercises confined to a single introductory meeting. [CRE-W033, §5]

Project spaces make this recurrence tangible. Photographs, notes, comparable products and prototypes can remain spread out, visible and available to handle. A designer can return to an earlier representation

while developing another one, and colleagues can refer to the same material. Herring and colleagues use these observations to discuss requirements for computer support, including access to the working environment when a person or dispersed team is elsewhere. Their proposal for support grows out of observed practices and interview accounts. [CRE-W033, discussion]

Collaborative generation for accessible websites#

Daniela Satomi Saito and colleagues considered how a shared web environment could support creative collaboration in developing accessible websites. The proposed participants include people with different professional knowledge and users whose experiences matter to accessibility. The chapter examines techniques together with the functions a platform would need to make them available in a collaborative design process. [CRE-W034, pp. 170–203]

The authors consider written circulation of ideas, role-based generation, mind maps and evaluation. Circulating contributions offers a way for a multidisciplinary group to add to and develop proposals. Role-based exercises ask participants to approach the problem from another identity or perspective. A mind map can make requirements and their associations visible for further discussion. The chapter connects these operations to the task of eliciting and developing accessible interfaces, where the knowledge required is distributed among different participants. [CRE-W034, pp. 184–186]

This is a design and instructional proposal for bringing established techniques into a shared environment. Its account explains how participation, representation of requirements and generation of interface ideas could be supported together. The general written-exchange procedures discussed in Systematic accounts of methods for generating ideas provide another route into this material; Saito and colleagues develop their application to a specific collaborative task. [CRE-W034]

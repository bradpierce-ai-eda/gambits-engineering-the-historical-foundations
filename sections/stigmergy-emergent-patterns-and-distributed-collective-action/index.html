This section reports selected explanations of coordination through local interactions, a modified environment, and expectations about others. It also includes an important adverse case: local choices can produce an aggregate pattern unlike the mixture the participants would tolerate. “Emergent” identifies a relation between interactions and a pattern.

Grassé: the product of work stimulates subsequent work#

In the selected passages of his 1959 termite article, Grassé describes a mechanism in which construction changes a material product, and that changed product becomes a stimulus for subsequent activity by the same or other workers. The environment is consequently involved in the sequence of coordination. The next act need not be directed by a central representation of the completed nest or an instruction exchanged between each pair of workers. A worker can encounter a physically altered situation and act in relation to it. [W031, printed pp. 62–63, 71–75]

The selected §V also supplies boundaries. Grassé does not treat this construction mechanism as an exhaustive explanation of every social-insect behavior, or eliminate all possible roles for communication. [W031]

Heylighen: a broader trace-mediated proposal#

Heylighen’s selected definitions and components generalize stigmergy as indirect coordination mediated by traces left in an environment. An activity changes the medium; a later activity responds to the change. This description can cover more than insect building and need not require every participant to represent the whole collective process. It also makes the medium operationally important: the relevant change must persist sufficiently, be perceivable, and support a response appropriate to the activity. An uninterpretable mark is not a coordination mechanism merely because it is a trace. [W073, §§2–3]

The example and conclusion present Heylighen's broader explanatory proposal. The account acknowledges imperfect use of the available workforce. A trace can guide selection without ensuring that work is evenly distributed, that obsolete information disappears in time, or that all required tasks are covered. [W073]

Multi-robot coordination retains an engineered organization#

Gil, Aguilar, Rivas and Dapena’s article proposes an architecture for emergent coordination in multi-robot systems. In the selected architecture and communication passages, robots do not simply follow unsupported local rules. The design retains sensing, knowledge management, collective memory, and both direct and indirect communication. These layers provide the conditions under which local acts can become relevant to a collective task. The account therefore makes the supporting organization visible within a proposal described as emergent. [W067, §§2–4, printed pp. 76–79]

Schelling: tolerable local mixtures can produce segregation#

Schelling’s selected opening and model passages distinguish individual neighborhood rules from the collective pattern produced by movements under those rules. A person may tolerate some unlike neighbors without requiring the final population to be segregated. Yet moves made in response to locally unsatisfactory mixtures can change other neighborhoods, trigger further moves, and produce substantial separation. An aggregate pattern can therefore be different from the pattern one would infer by treating

each participant’s preference as a direct preference for the final collective outcome. [W070, printed pp. 147, 158–161]

The result depends on the model’s population ratios, neighborhood definition, thresholds and movement rules. Schelling explicitly limits quantitative generalization from an artificial board. Even describing the resulting arrangement as undesirable requires a stated social goal; the model does not derive that goal from its updating rules. This is relevant adverse material for a study of structural arrangements: local responses and successful individual adjustment need not establish an acceptable collective outcome. [W070]

JST CRDS’s 2026 landscape section on autonomous distributed intelligence maps questions across material, bodily and collective scales, including design theory, prediction, oversight and implementation. [W100, S2.03, selected pp. 1–6]

Double and multi-contingency: order between two agents may not scale#

Dittrich, Kron and Banzhaf reconstruct a problem associated with Parsons and Luhmann: agents form expectations about how others will respond, including expectations about others’ expectations. The article contrasts shared-culture explanations with the development of expectations through interaction history. The selected abstract and paragraphs 1.1–2.5 introduce their own simulation framework. [SG-W042]

In the dyadic model, two agents alternately display one of a finite set of messages. There is no prior semantic relation among the messages. Each agent’s selection is influenced by wanting to meet the other’s expectations and wanting the other’s response to be predictable. Those two motives can conflict. The model thus specifies a selection problem rather than assuming that “communication” automatically produces a common understanding. Memory and information about the other’s response are part of the arrangement that can produce regularities. [SG-W042, §§2.1–2.5]

The authors report that order achieved between two agents often fails to scale to a larger setting and that observation and the proliferation of information matter. That reported finding belongs to their model. The contribution is a specified obstacle to generalization: a mechanism that produces a stable relation in a pair need not organize many participants under changed information conditions. [SG-W042]

Leydesdorff: present decisions using a representation of a future#

Leydesdorff’s 2005 article relates Rosen’s account of anticipation to Dubois’s incursive formulations and to social meaning. In the selected introduction, a system can use an internal representation to consider alternatives and alter present behavior. This differs from describing every adjustment as a reaction only to a past disturbance. The selected derivation then proposes a mathematical treatment and distinguishes an observing system from the system under observation. The quality of observation and the difference between interacting observers and interacting observations become explicit modeling choices. [SG-W055, introductory definitions]

The proposal retains the work of constructing, evaluating and updating the model, selecting among possibilities and making a decision. For Leydesdorff’s later double-contingency exposition, see S47. [SG-W055; WME-W021]

Environmental traces and expectations in collective action#

The material distinguishes a physical stimulus, a readable environmental trace, an engineered memory and communication architecture, a neighborhood updating rule, an institutional research agenda, and formal models of expectations. These descriptions make different commitments.

S51 Organizational silence, dissent and learning #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

An organization can possess information that never becomes available to the people deciding what to do. It can also receive a warning and interpret it through assumptions that make the warning seem unimportant. The historical accounts in this section examine the organization of voice, learning, commitment and the interpretation of danger. They explain how reporting channels, authority, shared expectations and ordinary work practices affect what can be questioned and corrected.

Stinchcombe's account in S13 already connects useful information with tasks, incentives and recipients' ability to act. Beer's treatment in S22 explains how ignorance can filter the world into an apparently manageable form, while operational examination supplements routine reporting. The sources developed here examine more closely why people withhold concerns, how disagreement becomes usable, and why some decisions resist revision. The relevant engineering interventions concern the conduct of inquiry and the institutional relations that support it.

Hirschman: exit and voice are different responses to deterioration#

Albert Hirschman's 1970 book begins with organizations and firms that can deteriorate and potentially recover. Dissatisfied customers or members can leave, withdrawing purchases or participation. They can also attempt to change the objectionable condition. Hirschman calls the latter response voice. It ranges from a direct complaint to collective organization, appeals to higher authority and public protest. A person deciding whether to remain and speak faces a different problem from one deciding whether another supplier or organization offers a better alternative. Voice requires some prospect that effort can influence what happens. [OI-W007, opening; ch. 3]

The distinction gives deteriorating performance two possible routes back to management. A loss of customers or members can create pressure through reduced revenue or participation. Complaints and organized demands can convey more specific information about what is wrong and what might repair it. These routes need not reinforce each other. Easy departure can remove the people most willing to object, leaving managers with a quieter organization whose surviving members appear more satisfied. Whether exit supplies an effective corrective signal depends on how sensitive the organization is to the loss and whether those responsible can recognize and respond to it. [OI-W007, chs. 3–4]

Hirschman's discussion of Nigerian railways makes that dependency concrete. Dissatisfied shippers could turn to road transport. This alternative relieved their immediate difficulty, but it could also weaken the constituency pressing for railway improvement. Public financial backing could cushion the railway against the consequences of lost business. Competition would then provide an escape for dissatisfied users without necessarily producing the expected recovery inside the railway. The argument depends on both relations: access to a substitute and the organization's weak response to departure. [OI-W007, ch. 4]

Who leaves matters as well as how many leave. People particularly sensitive to quality may notice deterioration early and have strong reasons to complain. If they are also especially capable of finding an alternative, their departure can remove disproportionately valuable pressure for correction. The organization loses potential advocates for improvement along with their custom. Hirschman's analysis therefore asks about the composition of those who leave, the experience of those who remain and the relation between an exit signal and the specific knowledge that voice could have supplied. [OI-W007, chs. 3–4]

Voice itself has costs. A complaint can require time, coordination, information and confidence that somebody will respond. A person may wait for others to bear those costs. Repeated, ineffective protest can discourage further effort; excessive protest can also impede an organization's attempts to recover. Hirschman treats channels for complaints and the organization of collective action as capacities that can

develop. A judgment that voice is presently ineffective need not describe its permanent potential. Choosing departure can terminate the effort through which a more effective form of influence might have emerged. [OI-W007, ch. 3]

Loyalty changes the relation between these responses. Attachment can postpone exit long enough for someone to attempt repair, or to invent a way of influencing an organization that initially seems unresponsive. Yet an available exit can make voice more effective by supplying bargaining power. Hirschman distinguishes the willingness to speak from the ability to make speaking count. A credible possibility of leaving can strengthen a complaint even while an easy alternative reduces the incentive to make that complaint in the first place. [OI-W007, ch. 7, pp. 76–84]

Remaining is consequently ambiguous. A member may stay out of attachment, hope that others will intervene, lack an acceptable alternative, or fail to recognize deterioration. Management may also impose entry costs or penalties that obstruct both departure and opposition. Hirschman's selected discussion distinguishes such restraints from loyalty that sustains active efforts at repair. Continued membership can therefore conceal quite different responses to deterioration. [OI-W007, ch. 7, pp. 91–93]

Argyris: questioning the assumptions that govern correction#

Chris Argyris's 1976 article distinguishes correcting action within accepted goals from questioning the goals, policies and assumptions that govern it. He calls these single-loop and double-loop learning. An organization can encourage improvements while excluding inquiry into its fundamental design. He also distinguishes what people say guides their conduct from the theory of action inferred from their observable behavior. A professed commitment to openness can coexist with unilateral control and avoidance of threatening questions. [OI-W003, pp. 365–368]

His proposed alternative combines advocacy with inquiry: people state their reasoning, make it open to challenge and share control with those relevant to the problem. Changing an organization chart or teaching the vocabulary does not, in his account, produce that behavior automatically. Existing practices can make candor risky and recreate the old pattern inside a nominally different structure. The article presents an explanatory and prescriptive argument drawing on earlier studies; its discussion acknowledges incomplete evidence and the difficulty of testing the transition. [OI-W003, pp. 369–373]

Staw: responsibility for an earlier choice can affect the next allocation#

Barry Staw's 1976 experiment examines a particular way that an unfavorable outcome can strengthen a commitment. An investor can respond to disappointment by changing an interpretation, but can also commit additional resources in an attempt to justify or rescue the earlier decision. Staw asks whether personal responsibility for the initial choice changes the response to subsequent performance information. The question concerns the interaction between responsibility and outcomes, rather than persistence considered in isolation. [OI-W005, pp. 27–30]

Participants took the role of a financial vice-president allocating research and development funds between two divisions of a hypothetical company. In the high-responsibility condition, they made an initial choice and explained it. In the low-responsibility condition, they inherited an allocation attributed to another officer. Favorable or unfavorable financial outcomes were then presented independently of that manipulation. Participants subsequently divided a new pool of hypothetical funds between the divisions. This design separated having made the prior choice from learning that its consequences were disappointing. [OI-W005, pp. 30–35]

The largest allocation to the previously selected division occurred when participants had personally chosen it and then received unfavorable results. The corresponding response to negative outcomes was

not significant among those who inherited the decision. Nor did responsibility significantly change allocation following favorable results. These conditional comparisons are central to the finding. The experiment supports a combined effect of responsibility and outcome valence, rather than a claim that any unfavorable news necessarily increases investment or that taking responsibility always impedes correction. [OI-W005, pp. 36–39]

The analysis included 239 undergraduate business students, after one participant was excluded because the initial choice could not be made. The experiment measured one subsequent allocation of hypothetical funds in a stylized financial task. [OI-W005, pp. 30–39]

Staw interprets the result through self-justification and discusses competing accounts of how people infer their own attitudes. He also distinguishes maintaining a coherent image of oneself from demonstrating to other people that an earlier costly decision was defensible. The latter could matter especially where status and evaluation depend on the appearance of competence. Those motives were not separately manipulated in this experiment. They are parts of the explanatory discussion that follows the observed interaction. [OI-W005, pp. 40–42]

Morrison and Milliken: how silence becomes an organizational property#

Elizabeth Wolfe Morrison and Frances J. Milliken's 2000 article asks how withholding concerns about organizational problems can become a collective practice. Their object is more than the reluctance of an isolated employee. People can come to share an expectation that speaking is dangerous or useless, even when they possess different information and have never explicitly agreed to remain silent. The article develops a conceptual explanation linking managerial beliefs, organizational arrangements and employees' interpretation of their experience. [OI-W001, pp. 706–710]

The proposed mechanism begins partly with managers' fear of negative feedback and their assumptions about employees. If managers regard employees as self-interested, believe that management knows best, and interpret agreement as a sign of organizational health, they have reasons to retain decisions centrally and restrict the channels through which objections reach them. Informal conduct matters alongside formal design. Dismissing an unwelcome suggestion, soliciting advice mainly from agreeable people, or reacting defensively to adverse news can teach employees what happens to those who speak. Middle managers may withhold subordinate concerns because they anticipate the reactions of people above them, even when they do not personally share the senior managers' assumptions. [OI-W001, pp. 708– 714]

Employees also learn from one another. They discuss encounters with managers, observe the treatment of colleagues and infer the local rules. Morrison and Milliken explain how these interactions can produce a shared climate of silence. An unfavorable career event following an objection may be interpreted as retaliation even when the two events were unrelated. This is an illustration of their proposed sensemaking process: beliefs about the danger of speaking can spread beyond the people who directly encountered a discouraging response. Silence itself supplies a further cue, since the absence of objections can make an unwritten prohibition seem established. [OI-W001, pp. 714–717]

The consequences in their model include impoverished information, fewer challenges to prevailing interpretations and weaker correction of errors. Management can mistake acquiescence for agreement. Employees who withdraw psychologically may then appear to confirm the managerial suspicion that workers lack commitment, reinforcing the conditions that discouraged voice. The authors nevertheless distinguish useful participation from unlimited input, which can overload and delay decisions. [OI-W001, pp. 719–721]

Edmondson: interpersonal risk and the observable work of learning#

Amy Edmondson's 1999 field study treats learning as observable activity within teams: asking for help, seeking feedback, discussing errors, sharing information and experimenting. These activities can reveal uncertainty or failure to colleagues and supervisors. Someone may recognize that an inquiry would help the task and still avoid it because of the expected personal consequences. The paper examines team psychological safety, a shared belief that interpersonal risk-taking is safe, as a condition associated with carrying out that work. [OI-W004, pp. 350–355]

Psychological safety in this account differs from confidence that the team has the ability to succeed. A team can think itself competent while making it difficult to admit a mistake. It also differs from cohesion or an atmosphere in which every view must be agreeable. The survey asks about the treatment of mistakes, the ability to bring up difficult issues, asking for help and the acceptance of differences. The construct concerns the expected interpersonal consequences of participating, while team efficacy concerns confidence in collective capability. [OI-W004, pp. 351–355, appendix]

The study covered 51 work teams in one office-furniture manufacturing company. Team-member surveys were complemented by interviews, observation and measures from people receiving the teams' work. Psychological safety was positively associated with learning behavior, including measures supplied by outside observers. The analysis also examined whether learning could help account for the relation between safety and performance. Because the principal measurements were cross-sectional, those relationships do not demonstrate that an intervention increasing safety would cause learning or performance to improve. [OI-W004, pp. 358–368, 378–379]

The qualitative cases show what learning activity could look like. Members of the Stain Team described responding to reports of defects by working on the defects instead of attacking the person who reported them. They also sometimes sought another person's opinion without first announcing which defect they suspected. This practice made an additional inspection less dependent on the team's initial interpretation. [OI-W004, pp. 371, 375–377]

Resources and leadership remain part of the account. Time, staffing and access to support affect the opportunity to learn, while coaching and reactions to problems affect what team members expect from speaking. Some teams worked around material constraints; teams with apparently similar formal support could nevertheless differ in their learning behavior. Edmondson investigates those relations together. Learning also consumes time, and its value depends partly on uncertainty and task demands. The detailed failure-analysis account by Edmondson and Cannon in S31 develops a separate contribution concerning the work after a failure has been recognized. [OI-W004, pp. 354, 376–379; AFC-W015]

Vaughan: warning evidence is interpreted within a history of work#

Diane Vaughan's 1997 article examines how earlier policy decisions and ordinary engineering work contributed to the Challenger launch decision. It condenses her larger historical reconstruction, drawing on archival material and interviews. Her argument challenges accounts that locate the explanation chiefly in the final teleconference, the moral character of particular managers or a simple opposition between engineers concerned with safety and managers concerned with schedules. She traces how production concerns, bureaucratic accountability and the inherited technical culture affected both groups. [OI-W017, pp. 80–82]

The history of the booster joints matters to that explanation. Damage to O-rings was initially an anomaly relative to design expectations. Engineers analyzed the damage and accepted explanations under which a second O-ring would preserve the required seal. Subsequent decisions could rely on that analysis and on the precedent of flights that returned successfully. Vaughan describes a gradual extension of acceptable

risk: individual decisions could appear reasonable within the established assessment process while cumulatively accepting conditions that had originally been anomalous. Her term normalization of deviance concerns this development of official risk judgments, including judgments made by working engineers. [OI-W017, p. 85]

She distinguishes patterns in the evidence that helped sustain those interpretations. A danger signal followed by successful flights could suggest that a repair had worked. An unclear or seemingly unlikely event could remain a weak signal. Repeated erosion could become routine when engineers believed its occurrence confirmed their understanding of the joint. The same recorded observation could therefore have a different significance to someone reconstructing the disaster than to someone assessing the next flight within the accumulated history. Vaughan's explanation concerns the interpretation and sequence of information, as well as whether information was transmitted. [OI-W017, pp. 86–87]

The eve-of-launch discussion introduced circumstances that did not fit the routine. Predicted temperatures were below those of earlier launches, the contractor's engineering presentation was being handled by teleconference, and its engineers recommended against launching. Preparing charts under time pressure divided the work among specialists. Some participants assembled the final recommendation without examining the other charts as a complete set. The group did not collectively review all those charts before transmitting them. These details connect organizational divisions of work and timing to the evidence available in the decision itself. [OI-W017, pp. 91–92]

Within Vaughan's reconstruction, the presentation contained signals that weakened the recommendation as others interpreted it. Charts reused from earlier launch recommendations looked familiar. Damage at a warm launch complicated a simple temperature argument, while some statements about redundancy appeared inconsistent with the proposed launch limit. A culture requiring quantitative support for engineering recommendations made the engineers' observational concerns difficult to defend in the expected form. Hierarchy and the separation of participants among locations also affected who spoke and what they knew about support for their position. The warning was voiced, but the way it was assembled, challenged and interpreted reduced its force. [OI-W017, pp. 92–94]

The article's practical discussion addresses policy, culture and signals. Vaughan argues that leaders need to examine how goals and resource decisions change the conditions of hazardous work, including consequences appearing after the original decision makers have moved on. She also asks how an organization can preserve coordination through rules while recognizing a situation for which familiar rules are inappropriate. Her warning about organizational change includes the loss of memory, disrupted working relations and new opportunities for error during a transition. [OI-W017, pp. 96–99]

The concrete problem of preserving the reasons behind a decision also appears in Leveson's safety account in S29 and in the treatment of records and traceability in S42. The sources here add detailed accounts of voice, interpretation and the personal and collective consequences of questioning a course of action. S52 turns to the related work of generating and testing alternatives, including situations in which people are willing to discuss a decision but have considered only a narrow range of possible responses.

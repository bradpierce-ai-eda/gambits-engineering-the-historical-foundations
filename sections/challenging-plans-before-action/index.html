S95 Challenging plans before action #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

A plan can depend on assumptions its proponents have not examined. Organized opposition, an assumed failure or an opponent’s next action can make those assumptions available for discussion. The historical accounts below describe what participants are asked to do and what the resulting arguments, reasons and decisions reveal.

S44 examines approaches to organizing inquiry; S86 discusses independent forecast review and project governance.

Exposing assumptions behind rival plans#

David McDonald, Gabriele Bammer and Peter Deane’s Research Integration Using Dialogue Methods (2009) reconstructs strategic assumption surfacing and testing through group formation, assumption surfacing and rating, debate, and attempted accommodation. Groups develop their positions and identify assumptions that trouble them in rival positions. The procedure makes the premises sustaining competing courses of action available for examination. [PM-W015]

Their Winterton example concerns an agency divided between finding business opportunities and recruiting cooperatives to pursue them, or helping people form cooperatives and then find opportunities. The groups identified different stakeholders and assumptions. Debate did not reconcile their positions, but participants agreed to gather information and conduct limited experiments. The reported action preserved the unresolved issue while making further investigation possible. [PM-W015]

The Pfeiffer Library’s 1998 robotics exercise provides a teachable implementation adapted from Mason and Mitroff. Participants receive the same automation proposal and form pro and con subgroups. They identify stakeholders, surface assumptions and rate their importance and certainty before presenting positions and debating damaging disagreements. Mixed groups subsequently propose integrative plans, retaining unresolved issues where agreement remains impossible. [PM-W016]

The stipulated Elmire case combines an attractive financial calculation for robot conversion with possible job losses, strikes, employee commitments and community reactions. It supplies participants with a situation in which assumptions about other people’s responses matter to the proposed investment. The exercise shows how those assumptions enter discussion and revision within the training design. [PM-W016]

Criticism, counterproposals and consensus#

George Wright and Gene Rowe’s 2011 review reconstructs the procedures compared by Schweiger and colleagues. In dialectical inquiry, subgroups develop opposing alternatives and debate their assumptions and recommendations. In devil’s advocacy, one subgroup offers a proposal and another critically probes its elements and recommendations. The returned material consequently differs: a rival proposal supplies another course of action to debate, while an organized critique identifies weaknesses in the proposal under examination. They contrast these conflict procedures with discussion directed toward consensus, without an assigned adversarial role. Their account also cautions that disagreement can turn toward personalities rather than factual issues. [PM-W028]

Charles Schwenk’s 1990 meta-analysis reports an overall advantage for devil’s advocacy over an expert-only approach. It reports no demonstrated superiority of dialectical inquiry over expert advice on relatively ill-structured tasks and no established winner between the two conflict methods. These reported conclusions address comparisons earlier reviews had interpreted differently. The task

qualification and comparator are part of the result: an advantage over expert-only advice answers a different question from an advantage over another organized-conflict procedure. [PM-W017]

Jerry Fjermestad’s 2019 review introduction frames a disagreement between organizational case and field studies and laboratory experiments. Active and passive participation, leadership and communication technology distinguish the settings. Groups whose participants merely receive an argument have a different experience from groups that construct and defend it. His framing also questions whether results from face-to-face work will carry into distributed computer-mediated teams. These distinctions direct attention to the organization of the comparison as well as the technique’s name. [PM-W018]

How participants interpret opposition#

Charlan Nemeth, Keith Brown and John Rogers’ 2001 experiment used a vacation-scheduling dispute in computer-mediated groups. The opposing participant delivered the same scripted arguments, while the conditions changed whether others understood her as expressing a minority belief or performing a devil’s-advocate role. After the discussion, participants generated possible solutions. [PM-W019]

The final usable pool was 94 female undergraduates, with smaller samples for individual measures. Perceived authentic dissent produced more alternatives and a stronger combined quantity/quality score than the consistent-role and control conditions. Mean quality alone did not differ significantly between those conditions. The manipulation thus concerns how participants interpreted the same opposition, while the separate measures distinguish producing more alternatives from improving their average quality. [PM-W019]

Explaining an assumed failure#

Deborah Mitchell, J. Edward Russo and Nancy Pennington’s 1989 experiments varied both the temporal setting of an event and the certainty of its outcome. Participants explained professional and personal successes or failures. The first experiment enrolled 114 MBA students; the second combined 76 MBA students with 32 paid students, with time recorded for the latter. [PM-W020]

In the dinner-party scenario, the same background was paired with success or failure presented as certain or possible and situated in the past or future. Certainty affected the number and episodic character of reasons more consistently than temporal framing. [PM-W020]

Gary Klein’s 2007 project premortem briefs a team on its plan and stipulates that the project has failed. Members independently write reasons for the failure, then contribute different reasons around the group. The manager considers changes in response. The exercise gives reservations a concrete occasion for expression before implementation. [PM-W021]

Klein’s software example concerns algorithms that would run too slowly on field laptops. A previously unmentioned shortcut was substituted after the concern surfaced. In his account, the change emerged from examining an assumed failure and identifying a specific technical reservation. [PM-W021]

Beth Veinott, Gary Klein and Sterling Wiggins’ 2010 study assigned 178 students to evaluate an H1N1 university response plan using a baseline, critique, pros/cons, cons-only or premortem condition. Participants rated confidence before criticism, after pooling concerns and after proposing improvements. The premortem condition showed a larger reduction in confidence followed by a larger recovery after improvements were considered. [PM-W022]

The paper reports confidence and subjective understanding; its overall understanding test was nonsignificant. Participants reviewed a supplied plan and did not implement it. [PM-W022]

Whose knowledge enters the review#

Guillermo Wippold and colleagues’ 2025 implementation premortem brings potential recipients into examining local conditions. In the Fade to Fitness example, recipients were recruited beyond the barbershop customers involved in shaping the original programme. After an overview and a stipulated failure, participants generated reasons individually, discussed responses and prioritized concerns. Transcripts were analysed through an implementation framework. [PM-W023]

The authors illustrate how limited shop space could lead to planning delivery during non-peak hours. The article explains that procedure and its contextual example, directing readers to a separate report for empirical findings. [PM-W023]

Giovanni Cunico, Nici Zimmermann and Nuno Videira’s study, first published online in 2023, addresses homogeneous groups whose disagreements may circulate only knowledge already present. Their New Devil’s Advocate is a prepared facilitator who temporarily represents absent stakeholders through documented information. Other facilitators maintain the process. A role-plot connects a stakeholder, evidence, a possible intervention and relevant model variables. [PM-W027]

In their UK housing-retrofit application, the first workshop left supply-chain and homeowner perspectives poorly represented. The team gathered information through three expert interviews, literature and earlier research, and prepared 45 inputs for two advocates. The role-plot paired each issue with a stakeholder, a way to raise it and possible model variables. One prepared intervention asked how household reluctance to borrow could affect renovation policy, connecting that concern to mortgage refinancing and the willingness to take loans. [PM-W027]

The resulting map incorporated relationships concerning installer skills, retrofit quality and performance, as well as household behaviour and perceived monthly savings. The authors traced 20 of the 45 prepared inputs into the map by comparing the role-plot, workshop transcripts and model changes. Their appendix shows how an input could be reformulated during modelling: household compliance with a retrofit measure became a link from behavioural compliance to measure performance. [PM-W027]

The two advocates participated very differently. The performer experienced in group model building supplied nearly all their interventions; the other, despite substantial topic knowledge, reported difficulty entering the discussion while trying to formulate contributions in the modelling language. The experienced performer occasionally returned to facilitation or joined a decision about model content. In one such instance, the facilitator asked the regular participants again whether they agreed to include the proposed variable. Transcripts and feedback thus reveal both how missing perspectives entered the model and how participants retained authority over its construction. [PM-W027]

Critical review and adversary perspectives#

The UK Ministry of Defence’s Red Teaming Handbook (2021) distinguishes critical review by a red team from adoption of an adversary or stakeholder perspective by a red cell. Its assumptions review identifies explicit and implicit assumptions in an estimate or plan, examines their support, asks when they might fail and considers what failure would change. Judgments about support and caveats are recorded for subsequent use. [PM-W024]

Argument mapping starts at a recommendation and traces supporting deductions, assumptions and assertions backward. Missing links and unsupported steps are marked. Reviewers then examine the chain forward to determine the consequences of flaws and the information needed to repair them. Red-cell work has a different information arrangement: separation from friendly planning and withholding privileged knowledge of friendly intentions help participants reason from the represented actor’s position. The handbook makes actor expertise part of that prescription. [PM-W024]

Héloïse Fayet and Amélie Férey’s 2023 institutional account distinguishes red cells modelling an opponent’s reactions, red teams questioning assumptions and defence foresight constructing fictional futures. Short brain games bring officials and external experts into roles, alongside more developed games and scenario efforts. The activities give participants different jobs and work across different time horizons. [PM-W025]

The authors also examine promotion incentives, hierarchy and personnel rotation. Critical perspectives have to be voiced, retained and used within an institution whose career and staffing practices influence those possibilities. Their account places the design of a session alongside the organization in which its results must circulate. [PM-W025]

Examining a course of action through a game#

Masahiro Kurita’s 2025 review compares traditional analytical wargaming with experiments that use repeated games as synthetic data. Human decisions, adjudication and consequences make the course of play responsive to participants. His review examines the tension between simplifying scenarios to isolate a variable and preserving the rich setting and interaction considered necessary for realistic choices. [PM-W026]

His account of the International Crisis Wargame describes teams of four to six acting as a fictional nuclear-armed state’s national security council. They choose responses to two separate crises. Repeated sessions vary whether their own nuclear command arrangements are vulnerable to cyberattack and whether they can attack the opponent’s arrangements. The principal material for analysis is the teams’ response plans. Play ends with the response decision, without a further exchange of actions between competing sides. [PM-W026]

SIGNAL uses a different design. Players representing three fictional states pursue economic, military and diplomatic objectives over five rounds. They negotiate and signal intentions, then take actions whose consequences are governed by rules. A player can depart from an earlier signal. This produces a sequence of interacting choices, rather than only a team’s response to a stipulated crisis. Kurita uses the two examples to show that experimental wargaming includes substantially different forms of play and data. [PM-W026]

The disagreement about immersion concerns those concrete choices. Fictional settings can help separate the variable under study from participants’ knowledge of real countries; removing subsequent interaction also simplifies comparison. Critics question whether such designs elicit decisions resembling those of actual policymakers. Kurita reports little empirical evidence establishing how much immersion is needed or which design features produce it. He treats the appropriate degree of simplification as an unresolved question for traditional analytical games as well as experimental ones. [PM-W026]

Technical operation, its conditions and its human setting#

Simondon's On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects examines technical genesis, functioning and culture. Four scholarly articles reconstruct Lafitte's programme. Archaeology and conservation accounts address human dependence on things and the preservation of material objects.

Simondon: understand an object through its genesis#

Simondon begins by arguing that technical objects are poorly understood when culture sees them only as useful tools, alien machinery or imagined robots. They embody human invention and natural processes. To understand them requires technical knowledge: neither owning a machine, operating it habitually nor knowing an isolated scientific law supplies the whole account. His proposed cultural integration of technical knowledge is normative as well as explanatory. [W020, introduction, pp. 15–21]

The first part favors the genesis of a technical type over classification only by use. Objects performing the same named function can have very different internal organizations and histories. A later object is not better in every setting. Simondon's comparison of engines notes that an older design can be more repairable, autonomous and tolerant of imperfect conditions in a particular use. The history is not simply a list of newer devices replacing worse ones. [W020, part I, printed pp. 25–29]

Concretization is his central account of technical development. In an abstract arrangement, parts are conceived as separately complete organs, each with a dedicated function, and must then be made compatible. Their residual interactions can create burdens or conflicts. A more concrete arrangement develops a mutually supporting organization in which a structure participates in several compatible functions. The change is not merely cosmetic simplification or a smaller number of components.

Cooling fins that also reinforce an engine#

The engine cylinder-head example makes the mechanism precise. Fins exchange heat with external air, while the same ribbed structure stiffens the head. Because ribbing supplies rigidity, the wall can be thinner than an equally rigid unreinforced shell; the thinner wall can also improve heat exchange. In Simondon's account, structural and thermal functions converge rather than simply competing for a compromise thickness. [W020, printed pp. 27–28; PDF pp. 39–40]

The best location of ribs for strength need not coincide with the best position for airflow and cooling. Simondon therefore describes designs that remain mixed or incompletely concrete.

Simondon connects internal coherence with material, construction-work and operating-energy economies in particular developments. An air-cooled design still needs heat transfer to its surroundings; a water-cooled system can retain useful thermal buffering even while adding pumps and other dependencies. Similarly, removing a manual starting action can create dependence on a battery. The examples are historically situated comparisons, not timeless rankings of all engines. [W020, printed pp. 29–33]

Technical progress can reorganize the functions themselves#

Simondon distinguishes small improvements within an existing organization from a more substantial reorganization that changes the relation among functions. A previously troublesome effect can sometimes become a functional resource. The vacuum-tube sequence illustrates how electrode arrangement, feedback and electric fields are reorganized rather than handled as entirely separate problems. Changing

relative geometry can perform work otherwise assigned to an added specialized electrode, while another change can require an additional component. Concretization therefore has no general rule that fewer parts are always better. [W020, printed pp. 32–39]

The Crookes-to-Coolidge example concerns the separation of roles previously assigned to gas that was needed for one effect while obstructing another. The later arrangement retains a heated cathode, vacuum, fields, cooling and precise construction. Likewise Simondon's account of the diesel engine treats compression heat as useful while retaining more exact fuel-injection requirements. A structural gain can be accompanied by a narrower tolerance for some other condition. [W020, printed pp. 38–47]

Minor improvements can be useful without resolving an underlying antagonism. The rotating-anode discussion adds motion to spread heating while preserving a concentrated source. It also adds a new dependency: actual rotation must occur. An energized motor circuit is not the same fact as a rotating target, and a protective interlock can itself fail. [W020, printed pp. 39–43]

He treats complete concretization as a limiting condition, not an achieved property of every mature machine. Full knowledge of all relevant physical interactions is unavailable in practice, and experiments remain necessary.

Hypertely: high performance can narrow the usable setting#

Simondon uses hypertely for overadaptation to determinate conditions of production, use or milieu. A highly specialized object may perform well locally while being less capable of functioning elsewhere. Dependence on another technical system is also relevant: a device fitted to one supply network inherits its constraints. Technical success in a narrow environment is consequently different from broad autonomy. [W020, printed pp. 53–58]

Simondon distinguishes adaptation to a given environment from creating a technical–geographical relation that helps sustain the object. This leads to the associated milieu.

The Guimbal turbine: a mutually enabling environment#

Simondon's Guimbal example places a turbine and a compact generator within a water conduit. Water supplies the driving energy and removes heat. Pressurized oil lubricates, insulates the windings, transfers heat toward the casing and prevents water entering through the shaft seals. The small generator becomes possible because the surrounding arrangement supplies the heat removal needed at that size. The environment is not an incidental container; its operation participates in making the architecture viable. [W020, printed pp. 57–62; PDF pp. 69–70]

Simondon calls this reciprocal operational relation self-conditioning. A generator designed to depend on water cooling cannot deliver the same power in air.

The associated milieu is partly constituted through natural processes and partly through the technical operating regime. Inventive anticipation represents a future compatible whole during design. Simondon extends this relation philosophically to life and thought.

An ensemble can require separation rather than a common milieu#

The audiometer discussion identifies the technical boundary by the conditions of functioning, rather than the manufacturer's enclosure. A supply, oscillators, an amplifier, headphones or a speaker, and the acoustic room can all matter. Stable frequencies and intensities require suitable temperature, voltage and noise conditions. In a relevant configuration, the room belongs to the complete apparatus even though it is outside the instrument's box. [W020, printed pp. 63–66]

Simondon distinguishes merely stabilizing a power supply from feedback that regulates a particular variable. If the supply does not respond to the oscillator's relevant deviation, it cannot be described as regulating that deviation. He also identifies beneficial and harmful common dependencies. Shared oscillator drift can cancel in a beat-frequency arrangement, while interactions with an amplifier can interfere with the intended behavior.

An ensemble can therefore require separate supplies, shielding and high-impedance connections. A common associated milieu is useful for a technical individual only when the coupled functions support one another appropriately. Making every component share a medium is not his criterion for a well-organized ensemble. The source's own example includes deliberate insulation against unwanted interaction. [W020, PDF pp. 75–77]

This is also why element, individual and ensemble are not simply names for small, medium and large objects. Their distinction concerns relations of functioning and the associated milieu.

Small objects can carry a large technical history#

Simondon describes technical inheritance through elements, individuals and ensembles. An element can carry properties developed in a much larger production setting. A spring, adze or needle embodies metallurgy, forging, treatment and skilled fabrication, even if the finished item looks simple. Its visible shape does not reveal all the knowledge and organized work needed to give it the relevant properties. [W020, printed pp. 67–76]

Simondon distinguishes synchronization of technical developments from complete historical determination. He asks the reader to consider the ensemble that manufactured an object's elements.

The discussion of tools and machine operation likewise retains cleaning, lubrication, sharpening, waste removal, belt adjustment and maintenance. The historical displacement of the human tool-bearing individual does not remove humans from technical ensembles. Their relation changes, and Simondon assigns importance to coordination, invention, interpretation and upkeep. [W020, printed pp. 77–81 and 129–140]

Technical knowledge is neither mere habit nor a detached formula#

The second part considers different modes of acquiring technical knowledge. Apprenticeship can transmit implicit practical skill; reflective engineering connects operation with more explicit scientific understanding. Simondon criticizes social rankings that separate learned culture from technical work. Some categorical historical and psychological claims in the discussion remain source idealizations rather than established findings about all children, adults or trades. [W020, printed pp. 103–127]

The encyclopedia supplies a concrete example of portable technical representation. Schematics communicate selected structure without reproducing every detail of an image or practical encounter. Simondon also warns that a simultaneous inventory can hide the inventions, failures and crises through which technical objects became possible.

His ambition for information theory is broad: it would mediate among sciences, techniques and learning. [W020, printed pp. 114–127]

Timing, signals and the energy that an operation uses#

In discussing the governor and load changes, Simondon distinguishes reacting after speed has changed from receiving a separate signal about a disturbance soon enough to act. A faster information path can

matter because waiting for the output deviation can allow an undesirable event. [W020, printed pp. 142– 147]

The book distinguishes efficiency in energy conversion from fidelity in signal transmission. A narrow resonance useful for energy transfer can be poor for preserving a broader range of informational variation. Low carrier energy also encounters noise. Simondon discusses thermal and shot noise and the importance of bandwidth and signal conditions. [W020, printed pp. 145–150]

Metastability and relaxation explain how a small trigger redirects stored potential energy: the signal does not supply all the energy released. Synchronizing oscillators require compatible phase and frequency conditions. [W020, printed pp. 151–156]

Openness, memory and the historical machine claims#

Simondon does not equate increasing fixed automation with greater technical perfection. He values a margin of indeterminacy through which a machine can receive information and enter relations with other machines. This can include earlier programming and configuration rather than a human supplying every instruction at the instant of action. [W020, introduction and printed pp. 135–163]

His contrasts between machine memory and lived human memory, and his claims concerning future-oriented invention, are historically situated philosophical positions from the 1958 work. His criticism of the homeostat concerns a particular device and problem.

The culturally important claim is that technical understanding cannot be replaced merely by ownership or command. Both an owner and a worker can be separated from the machine's operation. Simondon's preference for a responsible technical relation is normative; it does not settle every conflict over property or labor.

The philosophical third part: a proposed genesis, with plural values#

The third part explicitly advances a hypothetical genetic interpretation of technical and other modes of thought. Simondon describes an original unity differentiating into technical and religious phases, with aesthetic and philosophical mediation. These phases are not presented simply as a necessary chronological succession. Their role is to situate technicity within a wider human–world relation, rather than make technology the answer to every question. [W020, part III, printed pp. 167–190]

The relevant aesthetic examples depend on functioning and setting: a sail in wind, a pylon in a valley or a communications installation can be understood differently when their operation is known. Cosmetic appearance can obscure that relation. Yet aesthetic, ethical and technical judgments remain distinguishable. Simondon also warns that a premature apparent reconciliation can stop inquiry too soon. [W020, printed pp. 191–210]

Failure has an explanatory role. A suction pump's limit cannot necessarily be removed by more precise construction; an adequate account must incorporate pressure, material and environmental conditions. This is a source example of revising the conceptual problem rather than merely improving execution. [W020, printed pp. 211–216]

Simondon explicitly separates the value of ease from the value of efficiency. At printed p. 217 he denies an analytic link between them even while regarding their combination as desirable. An easy action is not automatically resource-efficient. Practical experience informs a plurality of values. The later chapters develop political, ethical and cultural proposals about mediation and responsibility. [W020]

The material mold and the continued act of construction#

The conclusion returns to a technical operation obscured by descriptions that name only a worker, an intention and a result. In the clay-and-mold example, the worker prepares malleable clay and a material mold, brings them together and applies pressure. The coupled physical system then effects the taking of form. The mold is an actual prepared object, not an abstract shape floating independently of matter. [W020, printed pp. 247–253; PDF p. 261]

Simondon calls for knowledge of the operation itself, beyond the customary gesture that brings it about. The worker prepares the clay and mold, while their physical interaction effects the taking of form.

The following pages reject a once-for-all view of construction. Manufactured units realize the inventive schema imperfectly and require adjustment, repair and sometimes running-in. Maintenance continues construction. His claim that serviceable objects do not deteriorate is explained through replacement and repair, not literal immunity to wear. A technical object can carry knowledge and still require a competent recipient to interpret, maintain and adapt it. [W020, conclusion, PDF pp. 267–269]

The proposed response includes channels for technical information and worker participation following actual operations rather than only the social hierarchy. The conclusion also distinguishes a productivity number from the technical schema: different arrangements can produce the same output figure while functioning differently.

Lafitte through Sandrone, Vaccari and Lawler#

Sandrone, Vaccari and Lawler reconstruct three connected dimensions of Lafitte's thought: how to study machines, what sort of organized bodies machines are, and how human life and machinery develop in relation. They distinguish the art of conceiving, building and ensuring operation from descriptive history or mechanography and from a proposed science of machines, mechanology. Physical events occur within machines, but a list of those events does not exhaust the organization of the machine as an object of inquiry. [UX-W023, especially PDF pp. 2–6]

Their account identifies passive, active and reflex or reflective machines. Passive cases such as columns or rafts can do useful work through resistance or support without an internally cycling transformation. Active machines transform, transmit or transport energy. Reflex machines regulate operation through sensitivity to their surroundings. These are Lafitte's categories as reconstructed by the authors. A passive object is not useless, and an active or reflex object is not automatically more economical. [UX-W023, PDF pp. 6–11]

The wider proposed organology includes natural and artificial organized bodies. It asks about relationships among machines, organisms and human organization without reducing them to a single physics formula. Human creation matters to the existence of machines, but their operating properties and lineages are not arbitrary consequences of an inventor's intention. The article relates this to ethical and political questions about living with machines. It expressly presents an early programme and germs of arguments, rather than a finished unified theory. [UX-W023, PDF pp. 9–14]

Sandrone, Vaccari and Lawler's account reconstructs Lafitte's Réflexions sur la science des machines (1932). [W074, through UX-W023]

Guffroy and Bontems: a lineage is a historical problem#

Guffroy and Bontems reconstruct the genealogy of mechanology while asking how a technical lineage can be identified. Their account distinguishes the 1932 work, Simondon's 1958 thesis, his mediated encounter with Lafitte through Jean Le Moyne in 1968, and later colloquia and continuators. The dates

prevent an easy assertion that Lafitte directly generated the argument of the 1958 book. Similarities and later acknowledgment do not establish that earlier influence. [UX-W024, printed pp. 255–280]

The article also treats the organization of machine science as unfinished. What counts as a lineage, branch or generation depends partly on the scale and criterion of comparison. Data collection, processing and representation need more stable methods. Its discussion of genetic diagrams includes abandonment, resurgence, saturation, branching and hybridization, rather than inevitable progress along a single line. Institutional failure is not taken to settle the worth of the questions, but neither is methodological ambition treated as a completed synthesis.

The authors report criticism of attempts to unite naturalist and formalist approaches in other scholarship. They also distinguish classification by function from classification through functioning in comparing Lafitte and Simondon. This distinction concerns functioning, while Sandrone and colleagues emphasize intrinsic organization. [UX-W024]

Bontems: several aspects can coexist in the same object#

Bontems's article reconstructs Simondon's classification and develops it through later material. It explains concretization through structural and functional synergy, while retaining hypertely and the associated-milieu criterion for element, individual and ensemble. The article notes ambiguities in early examples and the need to distinguish the 1958 text from later lectures and ethical work. [UX-W025, printed pp. 184–190]

The later passive, active and informational classification can identify different performance aspects within one object. Structural integrity, energy transformation and fidelity of information all matter, with their own supports: resistance to wear, energy supply, sensors, filtering or cooling. Bontems proposes a matrix crossing types and levels; it is his analytical proposal rather than a verbatim table from Simondon. [UX-W025, printed pp. 190–196; matrix, PDF p. 12]

His ChemCam example describes separate structural and thermal validation, optical and electronic validation, and later combination and compatibility checking. The point is not that separate tests make integration unnecessary. Different aspects must first be examined and then checked together. He also qualifies the idea that infrastructure, energy and communication technologies necessarily form one beneficial synergy. Network participation can impose dependencies and constraints.

A programme prepared earlier can reduce instructions supplied during action. Bontems connects stored programmes with later adaptation. [UX-W025]

Marratt: resonance without a simple direct descent#

Marratt offers an architectural and historiographic reading of the relation between Lafitte and Simondon. It explores convergences, differences and multiple technical temporalities, including networks as encounters among natural structure, human need and invention. Its operative body recognizes the mediated later encounter, even where an abbreviated introductory account might invite a simpler genealogy. This is an interpretive account, not a finished formal mechanology. [UX-W026, printed pp. 203 –214]

Marratt's paired quotation and translation differ: the quotation says that machines cannot be defined any better than living beings, while the translation suggests that living beings define machines best. The latter wording changes the claim. [UX-W026, note 11 and associated body]

Sigaut and related accounts of mechanology#

Part IV of Sigaut's manuscript reconstructs a debate about machines, technology and human activity. It questions whether renaming a science of machines supplies a genuinely new undertaking, and it distinguishes the machine phenomenon from an account exhausted by physical sciences. It also locates proposals extending inquiry beyond objects toward human activity. [RGC-W035, PDF pp. 25–30]

The abstract of Hayward and Thibault's Ethics in Jacques Lafitte's Mechanology objects to a precursor-to-cybernetics reading that overlooks Lafitte's ethical and social purpose. [RGC-W033, abstract]

Sigaut comments on Lafitte's 1933 Sur la science des machines. [RGC-W034, through RGC-W035]

Hodder: mutual dependencies and an unfinished integration#

The conclusions of Hodder's 2011 article describe human–thing entanglement through interdependent changes in material and social practices. His pottery discussion relates changes to houses, hearths, bricks and cooking, rather than explaining an artifact lineage solely by transmission or reproductive success. A trait's fittingness concerns how it works within those mutual dependencies. [GL-W012, printed pp. 172– 173]

Hodder explicitly acknowledges that his attempted integration has not fully brought the different theories together. He still argues for the value of a partial integrative account.

In Where Are We Heading?, Hodder asks where the wheel ends as an object of historical explanation. A wagon wheel needs an axle and a frame, as well as a source of motion. Making the wheel calls for tools and carpentry skills. Drawing the wagon with cattle calls for a yoke and trained animals. Where wheels serve road transport, road building and upkeep enter the account as well. Hodder argues that different answers about where the object ends lead to different accounts of its origin and use. [GL-W096, pp. 54– 55]

Conservation: preserving information can constrain intervention#

The Canadian Association for Conservation of Cultural Property defines conservation around safeguarding culturally significant qualities with the least appropriate intervention. Its statement includes examination, documentation, prevention, preservation, restoration and reconstruction. Preventive action can be directed at an object's surroundings rather than repeatedly treating the object itself. [GL-W099, definition]

The source explains why more intervention can be worse: treatment can erase information about manufacture, use and prior history. Earlier repairs or wear may themselves be significant. Treatment materials should avoid future harm and, as far as possible, remain removable; documentation should distinguish an addition from original material. The degree of intervention involves trained judgment and consultation with the custodian. This is a professional purpose and set of principles.

The Canadian Conservation Institute's selected painting guidance gives more concrete material examples. An unstable support or inherent material problem can persist after a treatment. A controlled microenvironment can reduce environmental variation, while ongoing delamination can still require professional intervention. The account therefore distinguishes shielding an object from repairing every internal defect. [GL-W100, inherent vice and microenvironments]

In the documented Buffalo Stampede example, consolidation did not remove the painting's sensitivity to humidity because reactive glue remained in its structure. An enclosed frame was added to moderate the local environment. In Charlottetown Revisited, the account describes adding supportive inserts and a

protective back rather than removing the painting from its original strainer. These documented professional decisions retain historical material and improve support. [GL-W100, selected examples]

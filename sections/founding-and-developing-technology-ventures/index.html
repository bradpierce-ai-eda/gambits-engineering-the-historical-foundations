Who founded the firm and when#

Arnold Cooper’s 1971 account gives a semiconductor company that nearly failed with its first founders. A later group brought management and capital and got the enterprise operating. The episode makes the founding date a question about what event counts: organizing the original company or assembling the group that made it viable. In another firm, an early founder took up product development before a later founder took responsibility for management and marketing. The histories of the team and the legal organization can therefore begin at different dates. [T02-W001]

Cooper also describes the previous employer as an incubator in a specific sense. Prospective founders encounter one another through their work and learn what their future colleagues can do before forming a venture. In S83 the Nairobi incubator is a program that deliberately connects entrepreneurs with contacts. Cooper’s example concerns an employing organization that can become a source of founding teams. [T02-W001]

The changing work of a founder#

榊原清則 and colleagues asked founders of Japanese technology ventures which duties they performed as the business developed. The report describes a pattern of founders covering many functions at the beginning and gradually concentrating on line functions. The same broad shift appears among firms aiming for an initial public offering, although the report finds a stronger tendency for the chief executive to combine several functions in that group. The study compares reported allocations of duties across stages and groups of respondents. [T02-W002]

Sarasvathy distinguishes deciding on an objective and finding means to reach it from starting with means already at hand and considering which ends those means make possible. A conventional marketing sequence begins with a market and a selected target. Her example of a business developing through contingent problem solving illustrates the second direction of choice. It explains a proposed logic of founding and the place of opportunity in it. [T02-W003]

Which ventures enter a survival estimate#

Arcuri, Russo and Gandolfi matched the Italian register of innovative start-ups with financial records. Their analysis covers 9,171 firms from 2013 to 2021 and excludes firms founded after 2020 and known exits such as mergers or acquisitions. They measure survival from foundation and identify failure from the cessation of annual reporting, with non-failing firms observed through the study cutoff. The three registration criteria they examine concern research spending, staff qualifications and ownership of industrial property. Firms could satisfy more than one. [T02-W004]

Table 4 puts estimated five-year survival for the whole sample just under 90 percent. The curves in Fig. 2 compare firms that met each criterion with the other registered ventures in the sample. Those meeting the research-spending criterion had lower estimated survival in that comparison. The curves went the other way for staff qualifications and for ownership of industrial property. The authors then used a model that included firm characteristics and regional, sector and year differences. In their further models accounting for selection into the criteria, the results for staff qualifications and industrial property stayed positive, while the association for research spending was less clear once the other criteria were included. These are comparisons within the register of innovative ventures. [T02-W004]

The Kauffman Firm Survey uses a different frame. Its baseline report records 4,928 completed surveys and a weighted response rate of 43 percent, with an oversample of high-technology firms. A firm founded in 2004 that closed before the sampling frame was constructed could be absent even though the survey describes a cohort of new firms. The report identifies this interval explicitly. Each survey therefore offers evidence about a defined population of entrants and survivors, while the founding episodes in Cooper describe events before such a count is possible. [T02-W005]

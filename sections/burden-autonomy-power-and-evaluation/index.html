These sources examine effort, authority, care, autonomy and alienation, asking whose purposes and experiences enter judgments of an economical arrangement.

Knowledge and power inside hierarchies#

Dosi, Marengo and Virgillito treat hierarchy as an arrangement of knowledge and power as well as a means of organizing production. In this historical and interpretive framework, authority can shape who knows what, whose decisions count, and which choices remain available to others. The organization of knowledge is therefore not necessarily independent of the organization of control. Making a worker’s local task simpler can coincide with relocating understanding and discretion into managerial routines. Productivity alone does not describe that redistribution. [W093, §§1–3]

Their comparison of Taylorism and Toyotism concerns different ways of using and organizing workers’ knowledge, rather than a simple contrast between an organization possessing knowledge and one lacking it. Participation in improvement and discretion over execution need to be read alongside the surrounding authority relations. The consequences of local simplification depend on who acquires the corresponding knowledge and power. [W093]

Tainter: complexity as a costly problem-solving investment#

Tainter’s selected introduction, definitions and discussion of problem-solving costs present social complexity as an investment made in response to problems. More elaborate organization can provide benefits, but it must also be supported. As solutions accumulate, the resources required to sustain institutions and specialized activities can become consequential to sustainability. In this argument a successful response to an immediate difficulty can create continuing obligations rather than simply remove a problem from the system. [UX-W006, printed pp. 91–94]

The relevant material is historical and organizational reasoning about returns and support requirements. Tainter expressly cautions about transferring ecological generalizations to societies. [UX-W006]

Work and care are different evaluative concerns#

Murphy and Parry examine household sustainability through work and care. Counting domestic activities as unpaid work can reveal effort hidden by monetary accounts, but the authors argue that this description can also lose personal and relational aspects of the activity. Their selected care discussion reconstructs scholarship in which maintaining people and their world is an ongoing relational activity. Who undertakes care, and whether adequate care occurs, are consequential questions in addition to the number of hours recorded. [GL-W006, care section]

Their integration discussion consequently does not equate a more equal division of household work with every possible improvement in care. Technology can alter expectations, standards and relations as well as the duration of a task. Its relevance is the more discriminating question: which work changes, whose work it becomes, and which quality or relation the proposed improvement is intended to preserve. Equal allocation and better care remain distinct normative goals. [GL-W006]

Administrative burden: three kinds of cost#

Moynihan, Herd and Harvey distinguish learning, psychological and compliance costs in citizen–state interactions. Learning includes discovering that a programme exists, determining eligibility and

understanding its procedures. Compliance concerns the actions required to meet those procedures, such as supplying information or satisfying process requirements. Psychological costs concern experiences such as stress, stigma or loss of autonomy associated with the interaction. These categories distinguish burdens that an account of administrative processing time alone can miss. [GL-W018, introduction and table 1, printed pp. 46–47]

The allocation of these costs between citizen and state can be political. A procedure that is inexpensive for an agency may require the applicant to assemble information, interpret requirements or repeatedly establish eligibility. Conversely, reducing citizen effort can require the agency to perform additional work. The authors also recognize that procedures can protect legitimate public values. “Minimum burden” is therefore not automatically the institution’s sole objective. [GL-W018]

What the administrative-burden review covers#

Halling and Bækgaard’s selected systematic-review passages distinguish government actions, citizens’ experienced burdens and subsequent outcomes. These are links in an explanatory account, not interchangeable observations. A formal requirement can exist without affecting everyone in the same way; an experienced difficulty does not by itself identify which state action caused it; an outcome can have several causes. The review identifies a larger research agenda while flagging missing causal links and questions about legitimacy. [GL-W019]

Its corpus comprises 119 English-language studies from 2012 to February 2023 within the administrative-burden framework. The authors note the predominance of Western settings. [GL-W019]

Relational autonomy: dependence does not settle the question#

The selected Stanford Encyclopedia account distinguishes causal from constitutive relational theories of autonomy. On a causal account, relationships and social conditions can develop or undermine a person’s capacity for autonomy. Support from parents, teachers or friends can enable agency; oppressive socialization can impede it. On a constitutive account, certain interpersonal or social conditions belong to what autonomy itself consists in, rather than merely explaining how it arose. The distinction changes what would count as an adequate explanation of a person’s autonomy. [GL-W040]

The selected discussion of stronger substantive and social-relational views also considers severely constraining relationships in which routine aspects of a person’s life remain under another’s control. Subjective endorsement alone need not settle the autonomy question for those theories. Other positions resist making every preferred way of life subject to the same substantive test. The entry presents a controversy, not a single established metric. Dependence on a person, institution or technical aid cannot simply be read as absence of autonomy. The quality and structure of the relationship matter within the competing accounts. [GL-W040, selected lines 246–260]

Alienation: technology and its social employment#

Leopold’s selected reconstruction separates objectification from alienation and distinguishes a criticism of technology’s social employment from a rejection of technology as such. Producing an object does not, merely because the product exists outside its maker, establish an objectionable separation. A theory of alienation must identify the relationship that has become problematic. Similarly, a machine’s technical capability does not determine the social relationship in which people use it. [GL-W049, objectification/fetishism discussion]

The entry’s selected later discussion raises problems of measurement and of connecting a diagnosis of alienation to a practicable account of unalienated production. This prevents a short circuit from “less

effort” or “more automation” to emancipation. Whether an arrangement improves control over activity, its products or its social meaning is a further question within the theory being discussed. [GL-W049, §§6.2 –6.3]

Peripheral case: coordinated roles in a traditional account#

China’s National Health Commission’s 2017 institutional description presents traditional Chinese medicine as treating a person’s condition holistically and tailoring formulations to that condition. Its selected account assigns differentiated roles within a formulation: a chief ingredient, an ancillary ingredient, an ingredient intended to reduce adverse effects, and one intended to coordinate the others. The source is relevant here as a public description of how a tradition conceptualizes an organized combination, rather than as evidence that every ingredient has the proposed effect. [GL-W061]

The NCCIH public evidence orientation distinguishes modalities and outcomes instead of validating traditional Chinese medicine as a unitary whole. It reports that some physical or psychological approaches may help particular outcomes, while results for herbal products vary; it also identifies contamination, substitution and other safety problems. The relevant lesson within this source is that an account of a coordinated formulation must remain distinguishable from evidence about specific interventions and harms. [GL-W062, selected effectiveness, safety and definition sections]

Effort, care, autonomy and power#

These source accounts leave their evaluative objects distinct. Task effort, allocation of knowledge, political control, experienced burden, adequacy of care, autonomy and alienation cannot be substituted for one another merely by calling an arrangement efficient.

What the seller takes to be the market#

Wendell Smith’s 1956 article distinguishes product differentiation and market segmentation as alternative approaches to marketing. Differentiation seeks preference for a particular offering. Segmentation begins from differences in demand and asks which offerings or efforts fit the resulting groups. The distinction directs attention to a decision made before a selling encounter: whether to shape preference around an offering or to organize the offering and effort around differences already apparent in the market. [T04-W001]

小原博’s 2010 essay follows changes in Japanese accounts of sales management, marketing management

and market management. In his discussion of 光澤’s two books, the earlier work moves from the formation of marketing management through management of processes and strategy. The later work sets

aside space for what lies beyond marketing management. 小原’s account locates continuity in the turn

toward 市場管理, or market management. [T04-W002]

Exchange as a continuing relationship#

Christian Grönroos argues that the conventional marketing mix leaves much of service and industrial exchange unexplained. His relationship account draws attention to continuing interaction and to contributions made by participants beyond the immediate buyer and seller. [T04-W003]

Robert Salle and Jean-Paul Valla’s Lyon Acier case in Håkansson’s 1982 volume shows what particular terms and adaptations can mean. Lyon Acier obtained refractory bricks from three suppliers within a larger pool and kept a reserve stock. Field, the British supplier, adjusted the alumina content to fit local working practices. When one of its early oven vaults failed after about 60 percent of its normal service life, Field sent a technical engineer and sales director and replaced the vault at its expense. It accepted Lyon Acier’s usual payment terms. The buyer reported preferring Field when prices were equal. The authors also describe the bearing of the exchange rate on Field’s position. [T04-W005]

In the same study, Lyon Acier accounted for little of a German supplier’s sales of oil coke. The supplier provided a standard product and required shorter payment terms. The French company’s large size provided little advantage in that exchange. Salle and Valla use the comparison to explain the different prospects for negotiation and adaptation across the buyer’s purchases. The report gives the buyer’s interview perspective and the authors’ interpretation of the terms and practices they found. [T04-W005]

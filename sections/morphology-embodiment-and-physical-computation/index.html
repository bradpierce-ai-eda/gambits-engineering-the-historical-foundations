The body changes the control problem#

The sources in this section examine how a body's shape, materials and dynamics affect behavior, perception and control. They disagree about when such contributions should be called computation.

Hoffmann and Müller: a design tradeoff, not an automatic transfer#

Hoffmann and Müller's 2014 paper considers a design space extending from relatively simple bodies with model-based control to complex, compliant bodies with distributed or model-free schemes. Conventional rigid-robot engineering often simplifies the body dynamics that a controller must manage. A soft or highly compliant body supplies richer dynamics that can help produce useful behavior, but can also be difficult to model faithfully. The contribution of morphology therefore cannot be evaluated solely by counting fewer instructions in an otherwise unchanged controller. [W007, relevant definitions and body–control discussion]

The paper distinguishes exploiting physical properties to simplify control from performing a computation. It also asks whether a model is required and how difficult that model is to construct. A body well suited to a task can make the task easier, while its high dimensionality and nonlinearity make accurate modeling harder. The proposed tradeoff includes the design and knowledge problem.

Their criticism of proposed morphological-computation measures is particularly pertinent. A measure that rewards little influence by the agent's actions can favor a system that is difficult to control. A measure that rewards strong natural dynamics need not favor the body that makes the intended task easy. The authors distinguish these properties from engineering usefulness. Physical dynamics can resist a controller as well as assist it, and the desired balance depends on the task and environment. [W007]

Müller and Hoffmann: control, perception and computation are distinct contributions#

The 2017 article examines characteristic cases including passive walking, self-stabilization and gecko adhesion, task-shaped visual sensing and physical reservoir computing. Its classification separates morphology that facilitates control, morphology that facilitates perception and morphology that performs computation under a defensible account of physical computation. A useful foot or leg does not necessarily calculate an output merely because its properties reduce a controller's difficulty. [W008, characteristic cases and conceptual analysis]

In a passive walker, the movement can result from the physical dynamics without an abstract computational level. In a sensing arrangement, geometry or the distribution of receptors can make task-relevant distinctions available before later processing. These mechanisms deserve explanation in their own terms. Calling them all offloaded computation can obscure what the arrangement actually does and what sensing or action remains.

Reservoir examples offer a more specific computational use of rich dynamics. Inputs perturb a physical system, and a trained readout extracts an output useful for a task. The article discusses a tensegrity body used to classify terrain, as well as arrangements with feedback and a readout producing a periodic command pattern. The trained readout, input coupling and interpretation of the output remain parts of these examples. [W008, physical-reservoir discussion]

Miłkowski: when physical assistance counts as computation#

Miłkowski's abstract and opening passages argue that physical implementation matters to computation generally, and that morphology facilitating control need not itself constitute computation. A special label should not suggest that ordinary physical computing is independent of its material substrate. This challenges the language of outsourcing computation to a body. [W054, abstract and opening]

Liu and colleagues: behavior generation and morphology-based intelligence#

The opening sections of Liu, Guo, Sun and Zhang's 2023 review place body, brain and environment in interaction. They organize a broader research area around behavior generation through morphology, learning-based morphology control and learning-based morphology optimization. The early sections are especially useful because they distinguish resembling an organism from actually exploiting a body's dynamics in control. A biomimetic shape can enable a new capability while still requiring a complex controller. [W011, printed pp. 1132–1137, §§1–2.2.2]

Their account of morphology uses shape, material and dynamic properties to affect behavior. The examples include passive walking and physical interaction that produces movement, with an emphasis on implicit physical processes rather than merely wearable or implanted digital devices. The review's vocabulary is broad: it often calls this contribution morphological computation. That choice is reported as the review's usage, alongside the stricter distinctions in the preceding papers.

The review also keeps learning and morphology in a two-way relation. Behavior can gather samples that support learning; learning can alter control; control can alter morphology; and optimization can change the designed body. These are different routes, not a single completed method. The wider device literature is mediated through the review. [W011, conclusion, printed pp. 1148–1149]

Chen and colleagues: controllability depends on the arrangement#

Chen, Yu, Zhang and Su analyze vibration controllability in an underactuated flexible manipulator. Their example separates two questions. First they examine state controllability of the corresponding rigid system under different actuator placements, disregarding elastic deformation. Then they investigate structural vibration controllability of the flexible system. The operative variables include both joint configuration and where actuation is supplied. [W009, model, analysis and conclusion, printed pp. 391– 398]

Their conclusion makes controllability conditional on configuration, placement and the vibration-controllability matrix. It also uses a minimum singular value and a modal-reachability indicator to distinguish configurations and relative difficulty. Thus fewer actuators or a flexible link do not by themselves determine whether a particular motion can be controlled effectively. The arrangement matters within the approximated model.

The paper's simulation supports comparisons within its planar manipulator example and associated generalization. The informative contribution is the explicit dependence of control capability on how physical degrees of freedom and inputs are connected. [W009]

Montúfar, Ghazi-Zahedi and Ay: capacity for embodied behavior#

A Theory of Cheap Control in Embodied Systems provides a more formal meaning of cheap control than a general appeal to simplicity. Its main finite, reactive sensorimotor-loop model has fixed body and world channels. A policy maps sensor states to action distributions; the induced behavior also depends on the embodiment. Distinct policies can consequently be equivalent at the level of behavior. A controller need

not represent every possible policy distinction when those distinctions have no effect on the specified embodied behavior. [UX-W041, methods, lemma 1 and main theorem]

The authors exploit this reduction to give a sufficient approximation result for a model class of conditional restricted Boltzmann machines. The main theorem bounds a sufficient number of hidden units using the relevant sensory support and the dimension of embodied behavior. It establishes representational capacity to approximate the specified behavior, rather than a universally exact minimum controller size. The fixed channels, finite variables and reactive policy setting are part of the result.

The experimental treatment shows why estimating the relevant support is itself a substantive task. The authors sample a target behavior, estimate frequencies and prune the data before estimating support and a behavior dimension. They acknowledge that support estimation is a research problem in its own right and state that their pruning choice preceded the reported experimental outcomes. The controller-capacity calculation therefore relies on empirical estimates and a declared treatment of sampling noise. [UX-W041, experimental support estimation and discussion]

The result supplies an explanation of how an embodiment can restrict the behavior space a controller must cover. Representational capacity does not establish how quickly a learning algorithm can find the corresponding policy. [UX-W041]

Langer and Ay, 2021: a controller measure need not measure behavioral importance#

In their 2021 study, Langer and Ay use simple simulated agents moving on a racetrack without touching its walls. Changing sensor length changes the relation between the body and environment. They optimize policies and analyze information flows among environment, sensors, controller and actions. This provides a controlled setting for asking how the complexity of a task is distributed across interacting parts. [UX-W022, abstract, §1.1 and §4 discussion]

Their discussion points out that the integrated-information quantity in a controller can be high even when that quantity has no behavioral relevance. Information flowing into and out of the controller must also be considered. An internal measure cannot, by itself, establish what the controller contributes to the task. The optimization can converge to different local optima depending on its starting distribution, yielding more than one reasonably successful strategy.

The discussion separates directly optimizing a policy from learning an adequate world model. A result about a prepared policy does not settle how the agent would acquire the knowledge needed to behave that way. This limitation motivates the later study. [UX-W022]

Langer and Ay, 2024: learning can temporarily require more controller complexity#

The later paper makes the acquisition problem explicit. Again using simulated racetrack agents and information-theoretic measures, it adapts an em-algorithm that alternates improvement of goal-directed behavior with updating an internal world model. It compares agents whose world model is supplied with agents that must learn one, and includes split-controller arrangements that cannot integrate information in the same way. [UX-W015, selected v3 introduction, learning results and conclusion]

In the reported internal-world-model agents, successful learning is associated with initially higher controller complexity, followed by a decrease as useful body–environment interaction increases. Agents supplied with an ideal world model do not face the same prediction-learning demand. The comparison locates a possible earlier burden behind an apparently simple eventual controller.

The split internal-world-model agents predict the next sensory state less well and, on average, perform only marginally better than random movement, with relatively few successful agents. The authors take

this as support for the importance of integrated controller processing during world-model learning. They also recognize alternative explanations associated with the simplicity of the task, including the possibility that a single wall-avoidance problem demands little once an appropriate policy is available. [UX-W015, selected PDF pp. 14–18]

The paper supplies evidence about this sensorimotor loop, prediction task and learning scheme. [UX-W015; UX-W022]

Wang and colleagues: responsiveness is not sufficient for their physical-computing definition#

The selected opening of Wang, Zhou, Kahak and Li's 2026 Perspective defines a physical-computing kernel in which encoded inputs evolve through physical interactions into decoded outputs, with the possibility of programming the input–output evolution. It distinguishes analog and algorithmic forms and names analog oscillators, physical reservoirs and physical algorithmic computing as strategies for soft robots. The paper explicitly treats its framework as a proposed definition rather than universal agreement. [W098, published PDF pp. 1–2]

A responsive material can change shape or properties under heat, light, magnetic fields or other stimuli. Such a response can help adaptation or simplify operation while failing to satisfy the paper's computing definition. The distinction gives a concrete test of terminology: material responsiveness alone does not establish encoding, decoding and programmability. [W098]

Xie and colleagues: count persistence as well as task achievement#

The energy-accounting discussion in Xie and colleagues' article argues that task scores alone can conceal important embodied costs. Their reserve-based formulation tracks an internal energy stock, expenditure during a time step and energy intake. It treats remaining above an admissible energy boundary as a persistence condition. Energy is the accounted physical quantity; viability is a condition assessed through that account, rather than a synonym for energy. [W099, selected Energy as a Common Currency section]

The source points to search, movement, docking, processing, conversion and storage as possible demands associated with obtaining energy. Maintenance and persistence costs can also distinguish agents that achieve the same immediate task. Environment structure can change intake or dissipation. The proposal is consequently a call for explicit comparable accounting.

In their illustrations and discussion of open challenges, the authors expressly avoid claiming one universal energetic model for organisms and robots. [W099]

Co-design of morphology and control#

Rosendo, von Atzigen and Iida built two-module robots and tested how closely they could move toward a target. Their joint search varied the orientation and offset of the modules along with the amplitude and angular offsets of their motion. In a second set of trials, they froze the body design at several points in the search and used the remaining time to tune only the controller. Controller tuning improved several of the poorer body designs. For the same design time, continuing to search both body and controller produced the higher best scores later in the trials. [W034, figs. 2–5]

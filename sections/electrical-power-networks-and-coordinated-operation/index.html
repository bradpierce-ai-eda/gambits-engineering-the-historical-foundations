Scheduled exchange and physical flow#

On 4 November 2006, E.ON Netz took the two circuits of the 380 kV Conneforde–Diele line out of service at 21:38 and 21:39. The outage was arranged to let a ship pass under the crossing of the Ems. The operators’ analyses before the outage indicated that their grids could accommodate the change. An agreed schedule specifies exchanges between systems, but actual power flows through the connected network. The UCTE investigation reports that flow on a different line, Landesbergen–Wehrendorf, rose by more than 600 MW after the switching and exceeded 1,200 MW. [T11-W001]

Landesbergen–Wehrendorf linked the E.ON Netz and RWE control areas. As loading rose again after 22:02, E.ON Netz dispatchers looked for a switching action to relieve it. At 22:10:11 they coupled two busbars at Landesbergen, joining parts of the substation they expected would redistribute the flow away from the loaded line. The investigation’s subsequent simulation estimated that this action instead raised current on the line by 67 A. At 22:10:13, the distance protection at the Wehrendorf end automatically disconnected it. Figure 3 shows the changes in power flow before the trip. Figure 5 puts the manual switch and the automatic trip on the same timeline. [T11-W001]

The report traces further protective trips as the surviving lines took on the changed flows. By about 22:10:29, the network had separated into western, northeastern and southeastern areas operating apart. Frequency fell in the western area, which had lost imports, and rose in the northeastern area, which had lost exports. The switching intended to relieve one line had been a dispatch decision. The trips and the initial frequency responses unfolded through the equipment and the resulting balance of generation and demand in each area. Times are those reported by UCTE. [T11-W001]

Separate responsibilities in a second regime#

The joint United States and Canada report on the 2003 blackout gives a different example of the division of work between a local operator and a regional reliability coordinator. It found that FirstEnergy’s failed control computers and alarm system helped leave its operators unaware of deteriorating conditions. MISO, the reliability coordinator, had its own limitations. It lacked correct data for the state estimator, the tool it used to infer the condition of the wider network from the measurements available to it. The task force called for stronger real-time tools and backup capabilities for both control areas and reliability coordinators. [T11-W002]

Energizing a network in stages#

顾雪平 and colleagues describe restoration beginning with a source that can start without outside electricity. It energizes a path to equipment that requires auxiliary power to start. Operators can then restore additional paths and admit load in steps. A carefully chosen load can help stabilize an energized island, while cold load pickup can cause a sudden increase in demand and constrain how much can be reconnected at once. In the parallel approach reviewed by the authors, operators restore regions separately and then synchronize them. The authors discuss how available starting sources, voltage and frequency control within each region, and the ability to synchronize across boundaries bear on the choice of regions. [T11-W003]

Reserves before a disturbance#

The Balance Management Task Force’s 2003 account distinguishes responses by how they are activated and what they accomplish. In England and Wales it describes a primary response delivered within 30 seconds to arrest a sharp fall in frequency, and a secondary response delivered from half a minute to 30 minutes to restore frequency. Its reserve tables separately describe fast reserve from hydro generation, which the operator could instruct manually. The first services respond on the timescale of the frequency disturbance. The fast-reserve entry describes capacity the operator could call on. [T11-W004]

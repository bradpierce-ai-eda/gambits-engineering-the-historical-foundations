S96 Negotiation: preparation, bargaining and agreements #

Part of Gambits Engineering: The Historical Foundations (Brad Pierce, 2026). No independent publication identifier.

Negotiation can change the proposal under discussion, the alternatives available outside it and the parties' understanding of what they want. Reaching an agreement also creates questions about representation, implementation and continuing relationships. The accounts developed here examine these activities through practitioner methods, training exercises, reviews and experiments. Institutional bargaining and brokers have their developed account in S15; deception during negotiation is discussed in S93.

Preparation and the choice to negotiate#

Алла Стремовская's 2012 discussion makes strategy depend on the importance of substantive results and of the relationship with the other party. Avoidance, accommodation, competition and cooperation consequently have different purposes. Her account includes the possibility that someone already satisfied with their position has little to gain by entering a negotiation. A presidential candidate leading in the polls, for example, may decline an opponent's request for additional debates. Preparation includes identifying other parties' strategies and adjusting one's own conduct as the exchange develops. The article brings together earlier writers' approaches and examples. [NN-W007]

Aníbal Sierralta Ríos places commercial negotiation within institutions and their histories. People represent firms, states and other organizations whose policies, market positions and experience shape what their representatives can pursue. His account of negotiator education recalls the OEA–Brazil initiative CICOM and its contribution to training in Latin America. Preparation in this setting concerns the organization represented as well as the person speaking: institutional experience and commitments enter the encounter through the negotiator. His opening discussion gives these collective circumstances a place alongside the individual's conduct. [NN-W008]

Fisher and Ury: interests, options, standards and alternatives#

Roger Fisher and William Ury's Getting to Yes, first published in 1981, develops principled negotiation through four connected practices: separating interpersonal difficulties from the substantive problem, investigating interests behind positions, generating possible agreements and discussing standards for choosing among them. The parties can remain concerned about their relationship while examining a disputed question on its merits. Their method asks negotiators to discover what a demand is meant to accomplish, rather than treating the demand as the only possible expression of the interest. [NN-W001]

Their orange illustration makes the distinction concrete. Dividing the fruit appears to require a compromise until the parties discover that one wants the flesh to eat and the other wants the peel for baking. The uses differ, so an exchange can satisfy more of both parties' interests than equal division. Their interpretation of the Sinai settlement develops a larger example. They describe Egypt's concern with sovereignty and Israel's concern with security: returning territory while restricting military deployment addresses different interests within what initially appears to be an incompatible territorial demand. This is the authors' explanation of the settlement. [NN-W001]

Generating an option and judging it are distinct activities in their method. The discussion of objective criteria asks the parties to examine standards and the reasons for selecting them. In a disagreement about an insurance settlement, the relevant question can become the cost of replacing the destroyed car with a comparable one. In a disagreement about foundations, construction requirements provide a different kind of standard. The proposed change is in what the negotiators argue about: they can examine a basis for the decision instead of merely repeating how far they are willing to concede. Competing standards can themselves require discussion. [NN-W001]

The book's best alternative to a negotiated agreement, or BATNA, is developed through three operations. First, list possible actions if agreement fails. Second, improve promising possibilities into practical alternatives. Third, select the best available option. Thinking about a job in another city is a possibility; obtaining an offer there makes it an alternative against which a current offer can be assessed. The book similarly describes turning possible union action into operational decisions, such as obtaining authorization for a strike. Negotiating power in this account includes work performed outside the exchange. [NN-W001]

The resulting alternative supplies a basis for accepting or rejecting an offer. Fisher and Ury also ask negotiators to investigate the other party's alternatives, including whether that party overestimates them. If both sides have attractive alternatives, discovering that no agreement would improve their positions can itself bring the negotiation to a satisfactory close. Their treatment therefore joins the creation of possible agreements to the development of a feasible course of action without agreement. [NN-W001]

James White credits the book's search for mutual gains but argues that it neglects distributive bargaining, where an additional benefit to one party imposes a cost on the other. His labor-contract example distinguishes changing an insurance provider to obtain better services at lower cost from bargaining over wages that reduce the profits available to shareholders. Discovering a mutually beneficial adjustment does not, in his account, eliminate the subsequent dispute over distribution. [NN-W009, pp. 115–116]

White also questions the distinction between objective criteria and persuasive rationalizations. A seller may obtain different prices from buyers with different needs, while numerical tables for pain and suffering can give subjective judgments an appearance of objectivity. He regards standards as useful arguments, but disputes the suggestion that they generally remove the influence of bargaining power. His objection concerns the scope claimed for the method, alongside his appreciation of its practical techniques. [NN-W009, pp. 116–117]

First offers and the unfolding exchange#

John Oesch and Adam Galinsky's review manuscript examines four connected questions: who opens, how extreme the opening is, how it affects the final agreement and how participants subsequently evaluate the outcome. An opening can convey information about a bargaining position, signal an intention or establish a reference point. Their discussion of resistance to anchoring concerns the recipient's attention. Considering the opponent's alternatives or reservation point, or concentrating on one's own target, can direct judgment away from the initial proposal. They also discuss the possibility that an implausibly extreme offer undermines agreement. [NN-W004]

Wolfram Lipp, Remigiusz Smolinski and Peter Kesting's review of 119 articles distinguishes a first settlement proposal from anchoring, the judgment process that it may activate. A proposal can contain qualitative conditions as well as a numerical amount. Their process model connects antecedents and offer characteristics to the counterpart's response and to subsequent interaction. What the recipient knows and does consequently belongs within the explanation of an opening's effect. Economic results and subjective evaluations occupy different places among the outcomes considered. The review develops comparisons and open research questions across this literature. [NN-W003]

张真 and 皇甫刚 organize influences on integrative negotiation into contextual and process factors. Their contextual category includes cultural values, social motives and emotions already present when formal negotiation begins. Process factors concern the frequency and sequence of strategies and their use at different stages of the exchange. This distinction separates initial circumstances from conduct that unfolds between the parties, allowing a question about a tactic's use to include when it occurs and what precedes or follows it. [NN-W011]

苏勇, 程骏骏 and 吴展's review of cooperative negotiation argues that much of the research has remained dominated by linear and static models. They propose developing dynamic and relational perspectives on the mechanisms of cooperation. Their criticism concerns the way a research setting represents negotiation over time and among people in relationships. Their proposed direction gives the evolving exchange a different emphasis from a comparison of isolated strategies or average outcomes. [NN-W010]

Erica Boothby, Gus Cooney and Maurice Schweitzer's review likewise emphasizes interactions, evolving conversations and real settings. Its relationship discussion treats relational outcomes as part of what negotiation produces. Its cultural discussion includes adaptation and flexible exchange of information alongside knowledge of the setting. The authors also examine how simplified role-play paradigms shape available evidence. These accounts distinguish a settlement's economic terms from whether agreement is reached and from what happens to the relationship between the participants. [NN-W005]

Discovering trades and making an agreement operate#

The Organisation internationale du Travail's 2010 training manual makes differences in preference tangible through an exchange between Mariama and Abdoulaye. Mariama has a green sweet and two orange sweets, dislikes green and particularly wants yellow. Abdoulaye has a yellow sweet and two brown sweets, dislikes yellow and particularly wants green. Both also value variety. Exchanging the unwanted green and yellow sweets benefits both; exchanging an orange for a brown creates a further opportunity. The exercise asks participants to discover interests that a simple division of the stock would conceal. [NN-W002]

The manual carries the exchange into the work of establishing an agreement. Participants assemble acceptable options, prepare a draft and check whether the parties understand it in the same way. Responsibilities then require specification: who will do what, where, when and how. Evaluation, follow-up and procedures for difficulties make the agreement an operating arrangement. Partial, trial and time-limited agreements appear among the possibilities when a final settlement is difficult. Its labor-dispute setting also includes consultation with constituencies and assistance from third parties. [NN-W002]

Maurice Schweitzer and colleagues' handbook chapter distinguishes opportunity negotiations from disputes arising after loss or conflict. Preparation includes recognizing the setting, identifying issues, gathering information and considering alternatives and the counterpart. Its account of logrolling develops exchanges across issues valued differently by the parties. Bringing a start date into a salary discussion, for example, introduces a further dimension on which preferences can differ. Its phases extend through closing and subsequent behavior, placing continuing relationships and implementation within the subject. [NN-W006]

The handbook also distinguishes teams negotiating against one another from negotiation among several parties. A team can bring several participants to the interpretation and exchange of information. Several parties introduce additional questions about coalitions and whose agreement is required. Economic gains and relational outcomes remain separate matters in the chapter's assessment of group negotiation. These distinctions connect preparation and exchange to the people entitled to participate and to the rules under which a decision can be made. [NN-W006]

Decision rules and procedural fairness#

Bianca Beersma and Carsten De Dreu's experiments assign participants to represent a bakery, flower shop and grocer planning a joint market. They negotiate design, temperature and rental-cost allocation with private profit schedules. Their first experiment involves 90 students; the second involves 141. The instructions vary social motives, and the second experiment also varies the decision rule. Unanimity

requires all three parties to agree. Majority rule allows two to settle, making it possible to impose an agreement on the third participant. [NN-W025]

Under majority rule, prosocial groups report greater procedural fairness than egoistic groups; the corresponding difference does not appear under unanimity. Joint agreement points and participants' fairness ratings are distinct outcomes. The experimental construction makes the mechanism of exclusion concrete: the minority's consent has a different role under the two rules, while motive instructions change what the participants are encouraged to pursue. The available coalition is a feature of the task, distinct from what every group actually chooses to do. These experiments give a particular institutional form to the broader bargaining and veto questions developed in S15. [NN-W025]
